# Budgeted Admission from Timestamped Capacity Records in Shared Clusters 

Andrew Bond

Abstract —A cluster admission gate can receive a capacity record that no longer matches current schedulability. We study one exogenous binary schedulability unit under a bound on the joint probability of admission and unavailability. For a symmetric Markov state, we derive the stationary admission frontier for a fixed observation delay and apply finite likelihood allocation to a delay-tagged delivered record. A one-sided exact binomial endpoint calibrates each fitted policy, and exact enumeration con-firms the per-policy confidence target in every tested parameter cell. A separate deterministic guard charges every unresolved admission against the realized allowance and bounds unavailable-state admissions at every prefix under truthful outcome feedback. An executable admission procedure composes the allocation and guard. The controlled system binds source sampling, NATS delivery, randomized admission, Kubernetes objects, and API-derived outcomes in one append-only record. Independent audit finds no missing event, identity mismatch, or state mismatch in the completed functional block. A separate CPU request-accounting check preserves Job and Pod identity across an unschedulable-to-complete transition. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Shared clusters often receive discretionary Jobs from users who do not control placement. A guest-side gate can suppress an offer before it reaches Kubernetes, but the gate may rely on a capacity record sampled by a separate source and delivered over a networked control path. Stale allocable-capacity views also arise in provider-side cloud admission [1]. We study the guest-side decision, where sample time, delivery time, decision time, and scheduler outcome are distinct events. We represent the target as one exogenous binary schedu-lability unit. A positive action offers one Job. Available-state admission counts offers made while the unit is available, while unavailable-state admission consumes a prescribed probability budget. Both quantities are joint probabilities over the action and the current state. They are not conditional rates among admitted Jobs. The action does not affect the modeled state. Three claims require different evidence. A known stationary model defines a population optimum within the stationary policy class. A policy fitted from finite transitions requires a confidence statement for that fitted policy. A realized run whose outcomes arrive late requires an accounting rule that remains valid before every label has been received. None of these statements implies the other two. Collision-constrained opportunistic access studies the corre-sponding likelihood allocation for Markov on-off state, aged observations, and randomized decisions [2], [3], [4]. We specialize that decision basis to one timestamped schedula-bility record, combine it with finite-sample calibration and unresolved-outcome accounting, and connect each admission to its source sample, NATS record, Kubernetes objects, and API-derived outcome. We make three contributions.  

> •

We specify the event contract from a timestamped capac-ity sample to an API-derived outcome. For the symmetric one-unit Markov model, we give a closed-form stationary fixed-delay benchmark and the finite-cell ordering for a memoryless policy that consumes one delay-tagged delivered record.  

> •

We calibrate each fitted fixed-delay policy with a one-sided exact binomial endpoint [5]. The resulting per-policy statement holds with the prescribed confidence over calibration data under the stated model. A separate prefix guard charges every unresolved admission as un-available and bounds every realized prefix for arbitrary binary state and proposal sequences with truthful feed-back. We give an executable procedure that composes the finite-cell allocation and guard.  

> •

We implement the event contract in a controlled NATS and Kubernetes path with timestamped records, Jobs, and API-derived Job and Pod outcomes. The functional audit recomputes policy decisions, prefix accounting, object identity, and outcome agreement from recorded events. A separate repeated check verifies an identity-preserving Kubernetes transition under CPU request accounting. The next section defines the record and outcome contract shared by the analytical model and the controlled integration study. II. R ECORD CONTRACT AND SYSTEM SCOPE 

A. Admission boundary 

The guest gate decides whether to offer one Job to the host scheduler. It does not place the Job, reserve capacity, or preempt another tenant. The analytical state represents one exogenous schedulability unit. The controlled integration arXiv:2610.28392v1 [cs.DC] 20 Oct 2026 study realizes that state through one run-owned scheduling taint. This signal provides a known binary state for evaluating the admission path. It does not represent physical device occupancy or cluster throughput. 

B. Timestamped capacity record 

At decision epoch t, the policy consumes a record contain-ing a source identifier, the sampled bit Xt−At , its logical age 

At, a source timestamp, a receive timestamp, and a decision timestamp. The logical age counts state transitions and selects the conditional law used by the policy. The wall-clock interval from source sampling to the decision is recorded as source-to-decision elapsed time. It is analyzed separately from logical age. NATS request reply and leaf connections transport messages across clients and NATS systems [6], [7]. These mechanisms carry a record but do not establish when its source state was sampled. Transport elapsed time therefore cannot substitute for record age. A Kubernetes resourceVersion identifies the object version returned by a read [8]. The analysis treats this identifier as opaque and never as a clock. 

C. Outcome record 

Only a positive admission creates a Job. The controlled harness releases an outcome label only after the required Job and Pod conditions hold and the recorded object identifiers and decision nonce match their predeclared values. Verified completion maps to an available-state label. Scheduling rejec-tion caused by the run-owned taint maps to an unavailable-state label. Any other terminal state, timeout, API failure, or identifier mismatch invalidates the experimental attempt. The stochastic model treats the capacity process as exogenous. The prefix theorem requires every released binary label to be truthful under this contract. III. M ODEL AND EXACT FRONTIER

A. Controlled integration harness 

The functional check uses a controlled harness. It exercises NATS delivery, the timestamped-record policy, guard deci-sions, Kubernetes Job creation, scheduling, completion, and outcome accounting. The completed guarded treatments have zero allowance and create no Job. Kubernetes outcomes in this block therefore come from the unguarded treatments. The har-ness calls the Kubernetes client directly, so its measurements concern this controlled path rather than the general request bridge. One runner controls a dedicated single-node development cluster. Namespaced objects are isolated in an experiment namespace. The Node taint is cluster-scoped. At each log-ical epoch the runner applies or removes one run-specific value under a dedicated NoSchedule taint key. After a 100 ms settling interval, the runner reads the Node through the API, records the returned taint state, UID, and opaque 

resourceVersion , then assigns a local sample timestamp immediately after that read [10], [8], [11]. The analysis never parses resourceVersion or treats it as a clock. After each API read, the runner stores the current sample in a local record buffer. For a treatment with logical age At, it selects the entry whose trace index is t−At and publishes that selected entry to the policy subject. NATS therefore carries an intentionally old timestamped record. The age At is injected by trace-index selection and is neither produced nor estimated from transport latency. Distinct NATS connections publish and consume this policy-facing record. The delivery contains the source identifier, sampled state, logical record age in epochs, source time, publish time, and receive time. The policy records its admission probability, common random draw, proposal, and decision time. Logical record age and source-to-decision elapsed time remain separate quantities. A positive action creates a Kubernetes Job. Its Pod uses a hostname nodeSelector for the selected node and has no toleration for the experiment taint. The taint therefore prevents scheduling in the controlled unavailable state [10]. The runner holds that state until it records Job completion or a taint-caused scheduling rejection, then deletes the Job before the next state transition. The outcome classifier reads the Job and Pod API objects together with the predeclared taint identity and a decision-specific nonce. Both accepted outcomes require the exact Job annotation, Job UID, Pod UID, and Pod owner reference. A verified completion also requires a true Complete Job con-dition, a true PodScheduled condition, container exit code zero, and a Kubernetes-populated termination message [12], [13], [14]. The harness accepts that message only when it equals the predeclared nonce. A controlled unavailability outcome requires PodScheduled to be false with reason 

Unschedulable . The classifier then reads the Node again and requires the same Node UID, a true Ready condition, a schedulable Node, the run-owned NoSchedule taint, the expected hostname selector, and no matching Pod toleration. Kubernetes condition text is retained for diagnosis but never determines the outcome. Any API failure, timeout, terminal Job failure, nonzero exit, identity mismatch, or other unclas-sified outcome aborts the classifier and invalidates the run. The classifier obtains each admitted action outcome before the next capacity transition. For a guarded admission, the controller releases that API-derived label after a prescribed three-epoch hold. Labels due at the same epoch are released newest first, including any labels released during the final flush. The completed four-epoch treatments did not admit a guarded Job and therefore did not exercise this delayed guarded-feedback transition. The pathwise result covers every truthful release order. Infrastructure failures instead invalidate the run under the classifier rules above. All treatments in the functional block use the same state sequence, record delays, offered requests, and random draws. The block is used for path coverage rather than policy effect estimation. The append-only writer flushes and synchronizes each JSON record and refuses to overwrite an existing file. The analyzer independently recomputes the policy probability, proposal, guard state, deterministic identifiers, event ordering, and prefix allowance. It checks the recorded Kubernetes UIDs and computes MtXt and Mt(1 − Xt) from the current state. Job completion and taint rejection separately verify that the controlled state produced the intended Kubernetes behavior.

A. Evidence roles 

The evaluation separates calculation from execution evi-dence. The analytical figures evaluate the stated probability model. The calibration sweep sums the complete finite-sample distribution. A controlled functional block checks the record-to-outcome implementation path but is not used to estimate policy effects. A separate repeated check tests a Kubernetes capacity transition driven by CPU request accounting. 

B. Controlled functional path 

The functional block uses the controlled state process with 

p = 0 .10 and ϵ = 0 .10 . It exercises record-independent, fixed-delay, delay-hidden, delay-tagged, and calibrated policies with and without the prefix guard. Fixed record ages are one, three, and six decision epochs. Variable ages use the same three values. Each of the 16 treatments executes four evaluation decisions on one shared state path. The resulting 64 decisions are a functional coverage record rather than an independent-unit performance study. We therefore report event integrity and local path timing but no policy effect estimate. For every guarded treatment, the allowance ⌊ϵt ⌋ is zero at all four decisions. The 32 guarded decisions therefore produce no action or delayed outcome release. All positive actions in this block come from unguarded treatments. The independent audit found all 64 policy deliveries and all 64 decisions. Twenty-six positive actions produced 26 classified Kubernetes outcomes. Twelve Jobs completed in the controlled available state and 14 Pods were unschedulable under the run-owned taint. There was no missing delivery, outcome, or API result. The audit found no identifier, record-age, state-to-outcome, or event-order mismatch. Recomputed guard decisions and prefix allowances matched the record. Zero prefix excess follows from zero guarded actions and is not a positive-action guard test. NATS publish-to-receive time had median 1.593 ms and type-seven empirical 95th percentile 2.554 ms. These local timings describe the exercised port forward and are not multi-host latency measurements. 

C. Kubernetes request accounting check 

We also tested one capacity transition produced by Kuber-netes CPU request accounting [15]. A locally frozen protocol ran 32 sequential cycles on one Ready untainted node. Ex-isting Pods requested 960 mCPU from 18000 mCPU allocat-able. A sleeping holder requested 17015 mCPU and left 25 mCPU, so a 50 mCPU target did not fit. Kubernetes reported 

PodScheduled=False with reason Unschedulable 

and message Insufficient cpu in every cycle. After verified holder deletion, the same Job UID and the same Pod UID completed in all 32 cycles. Every completion had exit code zero and the exact termination nonce. The background request inventory returned to its initial identities and request total. Holder deletion to observed completion had median 3.573 s and range 2.967 to 3.625 s. This interval includes scheduling, container startup, execution, API obser-vation, and polling. The sleeping holder generated no synthetic CPU work. The check concerns request accounting and object lifecycle, not CPU utilization, throughput, or interference.

E. Reproducibility checks 

The functional JSONL record is append only. The audit binds its digest and independently recomputes record selec-tion, policy probability, proposal, counter transition, prefix allowance, object identity, event order, and state-to-outcome agreement. The CPU request analyzer checks exact Job, Pod, holder, Node, image, nonce, and request-inventory identities across every cycle. It rejects a changed scheduler cause, object replacement, request arithmetic error, background inventory change, timeout, or image drift. Automated checks cover policy arithmetic, the exact end-point, finite-cell ordering, the serialized request gate, the outcome classifier, the prefix counter, event-path auditing, request accounting, and deterministic figure generation. No incomplete randomized-block artifact contributes to a reported number or figure. VII. R ELATED WORK 

Cloud admission and scheduling. Kerveros performs provider-side cloud admission from allocable VM counts and documents stale cached views and optimistic concurrency [1]. Borg represents operator-side cluster scheduling [16], while KUBEDIRECT reduces Kubernetes API exchange during burst provisioning [17]. Our gate acts on the guest side before one Job offer. It neither packs resources nor replaces the scheduler. 

Delayed state decisions. Delayed network state has long been studied in Markov control and wireless schedul-ing [18], [19]. Delayed queue admission also predates this work [20], [21]. The direct mathematical antecedent is collision-constrained access with aged or erroneous Markov observations [2], [3], [4]. Li et al. derive memoryless access under tight collision constraints. Our fixed-delay result is the one-unit closed form after converting conditional collision probability to joint unavailable-state mass. The action does not change the modeled state and the objective is not queue stability. 

Record age and calibration. Age of information is the time since generation of the newest delivered update [22]. We use the generation time and logical age of the record consumed by one decision. That record need not be the newest retained update. Clopper and Pearson give exact binomial confidence limits [5]. Learn then Test gives finite-sample risk calibration for general predictive algorithms through multiple testing [23]. Our construction controls one fitted policy under the symmetric-chain model and gives no simultaneous sweep guarantee. 

Delayed outcome protection. Runtime shields enforce modeled safety properties [24]. Delay-resilient shields have been synthesized for delayed input observations [25]. Our guard instead receives an outcome after the action. It keeps every unresolved admitted outcome charged and bounds a cumulative binary prefix. Its maximality statement is pointwise for one pre-action counter under arbitrary binary completions. 

Kubernetes event evidence. Sieve and Kivi test Kubernetes controller observations and event interleavings [26], [27]. Our evidence does not verify Kubernetes. It checks identity, order, and outcome agreement for one admission path from the recorded client view. VIII. L IMITATIONS 

The analytical frontier applies to one exogenous symmetric binary Markov unit and stationary policies. It does not estab-lish optimality for asymmetric, multilevel, nonstationary, or action-dependent capacity. The calibration theorem addition-ally requires independent Bernoulli transition indicators. If the transition model is wrong, its population confidence statement need not hold. The variable-delay result covers memoryless policies that consume only the current delivered pair. A controller that retains prior deliveries can have a newer sample than the record currently delivered. The experiment samples delivered-record delays independently. This distribution need not be the age process seen by a receiver that always retains its freshest update. The ordering also requires record delay to be independent of the capacity process. The deterministic prefix guard requires no stochastic state model, but it can reject available-state actions. An unresolved admission consumes allowance until a truthful binary label is released. The pathwise result concerns an abstract binary counter and truthful labels. It does not establish correctness of the Go runtime, NATS, Kubernetes, or the correspondence between an API condition and physical capacity. The integration experiment controls schedulability through one NoSchedule taint on one Docker Desktop node. It includes no incumbent work, physical accelerator occupancy, queue competition, multi-unit placement, or multi-tenant pro-duction load. A Pod held pending by the taint represents a controlled unavailable-state action. It is not evidence of physical interference or a tenant policy violation. The functional block has four decisions per treatment on one shared path. It tests event coverage and audit consistency but cannot estimate policy effects, long-run admission, or retained admission under the prefix guard. Its guarded arms have zero allowance at every tested decision. No guarded Job or delayed guarded-feedback transition is exercised. Capacity advances in logical decision epochs after the cur-rent action outcome has been recorded. Treatments that create more Jobs therefore take more wall time. The reported g and 

ρ values are per decision epoch rather than cluster throughput. The NATS broker is reached through a local Kubernetes port forward, so the study does not estimate multi-host transport latency. The separate CPU request-accounting check uses a sleeping holder Pod on the same node. It verifies one pending-to-complete lifecycle transition under Kubernetes request ac-counting. It does not exercise NATS, an admission policy, or the prefix guard. The holder generates no synthetic CPU work, so the check does not measure utilization, interference, or physical load. The audit verifies identifier equality and event order as ob-served by one client. It treats resourceVersion as opaque and does not infer a global Kubernetes event order from object versions, creation timestamps, or condition transition timestamps [8]. IX. C ONCLUSION 

Timestamped capacity records make observation delay an explicit policy input. For one symmetric binary Markov unit, the stationary fixed-delay frontier gives an exact matched-budget benchmark. With finite transition data, a one-sided binomial endpoint gives the stated population confidence for each fitted policy under the model. A separate prefix guard bounds unavailable-state admissions at every prefix while truthful outcomes remain unresolved. The admission algo-rithm composes the finite-cell allocation with this guard. The completed functional block audits every delivered record and decision. Its unguarded positive actions connect the source sample, randomized decision, Job, Pod, and verified outcome with no audited mismatch. Its guarded arms cover only zero-allowance decisions. The repeated CPU request-accounting check preserves Job and Pod identity across the tested sched-uler transition.
