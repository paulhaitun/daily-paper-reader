# Capacitated Line Aggregation with Maximum Delay 

## Runtian Ren, Shengcai Liu Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of Brain-Inspired Intelligent Computation, Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Southern University of Science and Technology, Shenzhen 518055, China liusc3@sustech.edu.cn 

Abstract 

We study capacitated line aggregation with per-batch maximum delay. Requests arrive over time at locations on a rooted half-line and are served in instantaneous batches of at most k

requests. Each batch pays the distance to its farthest request plus the waiting time of its oldest request. The objective is to minimize the total cost. We give a deterministic online algorithm with competitive ratio 36 + 12 √3. The algorithm uses a Next Fit idea along paths of nested time intervals: it assigns a new group to an existing batch when a path-load test permits, and opens a new batch otherwise. The path load controls both capacity and the cost of opening new batches. Randomizing the common scale improves the expected competitive ratio to (18 + 6 √3) / ln 2 against an oblivious adversary. Both guarantees extend to arbitrary indivisible request sizes. We also study capacitated LAD in the learning-augmented model. Advice revealed at its service times allows (1+ ε)-consistency against every complete feasible advice schedule and O(ε−2)-robustness. The analysis charges repeated switches to successive decreases in the remaining advice cost. arXiv:2610.26497v1 [cs.DS] 19 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Sending an acknowledgment, replenishing several types of goods, and serving requests along a common route all allow several requests to share one service cost. Waiting for more requests can reduce that cost, but also delays the requests already present. This tradeoff has been studied in TCP acknowledgment [12, 33, 18], joint replenishment (JRP) [1, 15, 16, 10, 5], and control-message aggregation [19, 8, 6]. Multi-level aggregation (MLA) extends these problems to rooted trees, with algorithms for additive waiting costs and deadlines [3, 4, 9, 2, 28, 22] and a stochastic-arrival variant [27]. Under per-batch maximum delay , each service pays its service cost plus the waiting time of its oldest request, and the objective sums these costs over all services. Lu et al. [26] study this objective for uncapacitated multi-level aggregation on rooted trees. They obtain optimal competitive ratios of 2 deterministically and e/ (e − 1) against an oblivious adversary. We study what happens when a capacity constraint is added, focusing on line aggregation with delay (LAD). In capacitated LAD, requests arrive at locations on a rooted half-line. Each service completes at most k pending requests and pays the distance to the farthest one, together with their maximum waiting time. Besides choosing when to serve, the algorithm must choose which requests to serve together. Even on a line, these decisions are closely coupled: a distant request can pay for serving nearby requests, but each nearby request occupies a place that a later request might use. Under additive delay, every pending request contributes to the waiting cost. Under maximum delay, younger requests can join a batch without increasing its waiting cost. This sharing is useful only while the requests fit. If an uncapacitated service is split into several feasible batches, several of them may contain old requests, and each batch pays its own maximum waiting time. Conversely, serving each distance class independently avoids this splitting decision but loses opportunities to share waiting across classes. For LAD, this gives a logarithmic competitive ratio; see Section 3.1 for the algorithm and a matching lower-bound instance. For LAD, we avoid this logarithmic loss by combining requests across distance classes and obtain a constant-competitive online algorithm. The algorithm assigns requests to future batches through nested time intervals. Its admission rule controls both the capacity of a batch and the cost of opening a new batch when admission fails. Thus capacity does not force the logarithmic loss incurred by independent distance classes. 

Our contributions. We obtain the following main results in this paper. • For LAD, we give a deterministic online algorithm with competitive ratio 36 + 12 √3 < 56 .785 for capacity k. For radii on a fixed scaled dyadic grid, the competitive ratio is 18 + 6 √3. A random common scale offset gives an expected competitive ratio of (18 + 6 √3) / ln 2 < 40 .962 against an oblivious adversary. Both guarantees extend to arbitrary indivisible request sizes, as described in Section 3.9. • We also study capacitated LAD in the learning-augmented model. An algorithm receives advice only when a proposed service is due. We combine this advice with any deterministic R-competitive baseline. For 0 < ε ≤ 1, the resulting algorithm is (1+ ε)-consistent against every complete feasible advice stream and (32 /ε 2 + 72 /ε + 36) R-robust. Our LAD baseline gives O(ε−2)-robustness. At a single location, LAD reduces to capacitated TCP acknowledgment, whose optimal deter-ministic competitive ratio is two. This special case also gives a randomized lower bound of e/ (e − 1) on competitive ratios uniform in capacity. Appendix A gives the fixed-location algorithm and both lower-bound arguments. 1Our techniques. Our algorithms address two difficulties: assigning requests to future batches and switching between schedules that have served different requests. • A constant-competitive algorithm. We use Next Fit along paths of nested time intervals. Apath-load test accounts for group sizes and interval lengths, ensuring capacity and paying for new batches when admission fails. Next Fit prevents descendants from repeatedly charging the same ancestor. Section 3.2 develops the rule and its additive charges through a worked example. • Online advice. Switching schedules requires clearing requests that the new schedule may already have served. We separate the service and waiting costs of this clearance. Baseline budgets control service costs; successive decreases in the remaining advice cost pay waiting charges across switches without repeatedly charging an entire advice batch. 

## 1.1 Related work 

Aggregation with additive waiting and maximum delay. TCP acknowledgment, JRP, control-message aggregation, and MLA have been studied with additive waiting costs and deadlines [12, 18, 10, 5, 6, 3, 4, 9, 2, 28, 22]. Our direct predecessor is the work of Lu et al. [26] on uncapacitated aggregation with per-batch maximum delay. They show that an offline optimum serves consecutive arrival blocks. This yields a polynomial-time offline dynamic program, which also sets the deadlines of their online algorithm. With capacity, such a block may not fit in one service, and splitting it can charge the same waiting period to several batches. Lu et al. [24] extend the maximum-delay framework to monotone subadditive service costs. With exact batch optimization, they obtain optimal competitive ratios of 2 deterministically and 

e/ (e − 1) randomly against an oblivious adversary. Their framework requires every request set to be realizable by one service. In LAD, f (Q) = max r∈Q xr remains subadditive, but the union of two capacity-feasible batches need not fit in one service. Treating their partition as a single service would omit the separate maximum-delay charges, so this framework does not directly apply. 

Capacitated batching and capacity transfer. Lu et al. [23] study bin packing with per-bin maximum delay and arbitrary positive item sizes. This is the single-location special case of our size extension, with unit service cost. They obtain an expected competitive ratio of approximately 2.418 against an oblivious adversary in the ideal random-real model. Our setting allows requests at different distances to share a service. Offline capacitated LAD with deadlines is captured by Nonner’s capacitated max-batching model [30]. A request with window [ ar, b r] and radius xr corresponds to an interval of weight xr. A feasible batch contains at most k intervals with a common intersection and costs their maximum weight. The problem is strongly NP-hard already for k = 3 and admits a PTAS for every fixed k.For proper interval graphs, Correa and Megow [11] give a PTAS with arbitrary capacity. These results concern deadline feasibility and service cost, rather than per-batch maximum delay. A different connection concerns adding capacity to an uncapacitated schedule. Tour-partitioning bounds combine an uncapacitated service cost with a demand-weighted root-distance lower bound [7, 36]. Fukunaga et al. [14] use a reduction from capacitated to uncapacitated periodic inventory routing, where costs include inventory holding. For aggregation, splitting a service at its original time preserves additive waiting costs and deadline feasibility. Applying the partitioning bounds and a volume lower bound therefore increases the competitive ratio by only an additive constant; we give the derivation in Appendix C. This argument does not extend to maximum delay, since each resulting batch pays its own maximum waiting time. 2Dynamic bin packing and busy-time scheduling. MinUsageTime dynamic bin packing and busy-time scheduling group items or jobs under capacity constraints and charge the total time for which bins or machines are used [20, 17, 21, 35, 32, 13, 34, 29, 31]. There is a temporal resemblance to our objective: the maximum waiting time of a batch is the length of the union of its requests’ waiting intervals. In LAD, however, these intervals all end at a common service time chosen by the algorithm. Capacity bounds the total number of requests in the batch. In dynamic bin packing, departures free capacity that later arrivals can reuse. Thus the shared busy-time interpretation does not by itself transfer algorithms or lower bounds to LAD, which also charges a distance-dependent service cost. 

Learning-augmented LAD. Lu et al. [25] study uncapacitated LAD with additive waiting costs, including deterministic and randomized learning-augmented algorithms using online suggestions of service lengths. We follow their consistency benchmark of any feasible advised schedule. With capacity, our advice also specifies which requests a service completes. It is revealed only at the proposed service time. Our analysis must account for requests that remain pending when the algorithm switches schedules, even if the schedule it starts following has already served them. Section 4 gives the switching rule and the residual-advice argument that controls these costs. 

Organization. Section 2 introduces the model. Section 3 develops the constant-competitive LAD algorithm, from the failure of independent distance classes to the Next Fit construction, its analysis, and the arbitrary-size extension. Section 4 gives the online-advice algorithm and its consistency and robustness analysis. The appendices contain the fixed-location results, the baseline upper bound, capacity transfer, and supporting proofs for advice and arbitrary request sizes.

In the uncapacitated algorithm of Lu et al. [26], a dynamic program sets a deadline for the current pending block, which is then served in one batch. Its analysis uses the fact that batches with overlapping arrival spans can be merged without increasing cost. Capacity changes both steps: a pending block may contain more than k requests, and merging may be infeasible. Splitting a block at its deadline can repeat its maximum delay across several batches; serving only part of it leaves old requests for later services. Neither modification inherits the original cost bound. Before developing the new algorithm, we examine the loss incurred by serving distance classes independently. The instance below shows that capacity alone does not explain this loss: all its requests fit in one batch, but the algorithm fails to share their waiting costs.

We therefore control which groups can join a future batch while they wait. The challenge is to retain sharing across distances without making small groups wait too long for a distant request. Counting requests alone ensures that a batch fits, but does not pay for groups that must be served separately. Our path load accounts for both group size and waiting time scale. A failed admission test opens a new batch and provides a charge for its cost. Later groups inside that batch’s interval try to join it, rather than returning to its ancestors: this is the Next Fit idea along nested intervals. We explain the rule and its analysis through an example before giving the formal proofs.

Why count load rather than requests? Suppose a root has interval length R. A cohort of 

M requests with home length y reserves M R/k units of area under this interval. Spread this area uniformly over its own home. Its load there is 

M R ky = Mk · Ry .

The first factor is the fraction of capacity used; the second compares the owner’s time scale with the cohort’s own time scale. A short home therefore contributes more load than a long home with the same number of requests. We allow the assignment only if the total load stays at most one throughout the new home. The total area is then at most R. Since every assigned request contributes area R/k , at most k requests can be assigned to this root. When the test fails, the cohort opens its own root. Later cohorts inside this new interval test admission to the new root, rather than returning to its ancestors. This is the Next Fit idea along each path of nested intervals. Disjoint subintervals can still use the earlier root. This distinction will be essential when we account for the cost of rejected cohorts. Section 3.6 shows that trying older roots first can destroy the certificate used to compare these costs with offline batches.

What pays for opening another batch? The example has load 3 /4 already present throughout the rejected home [10 , 12). The area under this old load is 3 /2, enough to pay its length 2 within a constant factor. More generally, a failed test means that either the incoming cohort contributes at least 1 /2, or the old load exceeds 1 /2 on its home. In the first case, charge the new root’s length to the requests that created it. In the second, charge it to twice the old load integrated over its home. A root’s actual batch cost is at most three times its length, so it suffices to pay for these lengths. The second charge must not reuse the same area at every smaller scale. This is exactly why we use Next Fit: the first rejected home becomes responsible for later rejections inside it. The homes that charge a fixed parent root are disjoint. A request assigned to that parent therefore pays at most twice its reserved area in total, even if many smaller roots are subsequently opened. Descendants of 6a child use the child’s assigned requests, not the parent’s requests. 

Why are these charges lower bounds on the offline cost? We first charge online roots to requests and then bound these charges inside each feasible offline batch . The second comparison is necessary because a batch may combine requests from different roots without taking whole cohorts. Fix an offline batch whose largest radius is Y and whose arrival span is D. Its cost is at least 

Y + D. Requests with owners of length at most Y have small reserved-area charges, whose sum is controlled by the batch’s capacity. For larger owners, Next Fit gives a unique-owner property :inside each maximal home represented in the batch, all requests owned above that home share the same owner. Its load is at most one, so their area is bounded by the home length. These maximal homes are disjoint and lie in a time interval of length D + 2 Y .The own-cohort charges admit a similar division: maximal roots are controlled by disjoint intervals, and the remaining per-request charges by capacity. Both certificates are therefore bounded by a constant times the offline batch cost. Summing over its partition gives the desired comparison with OPT. # 4 Learning-augmented capacitated LAD 

Section 3 gives a constant-competitive algorithm without advice. We now seek to follow a good advised schedule more closely while retaining a worst-case guarantee. We use the online revelation of advice and the consistency benchmark of Lu et al. [25] for additive-delay LAD. With capacity, each suggestion must also specify which requests it serves: at time t, an advice event reveals a service ( Q, t ) and the identities of its already arrived requests. The algorithm sees no future advice events or total advice cost. The provider may know the full input; only when its advice is revealed is restricted. As in [25], consistency 1 + ε means ALG( I) ≤ (1 + ε) cost(ADV) for every complete, capacity-feasible advice stream ADV on I. Robustness R requires ALG( I) ≤ R OPT( I) even for inaccurate advice. Invalid references can be ignored and no physical action may violate capacity. Consistency compares with the supplied feasible schedule, even when it is far from optimal. The new difficulty is accounting for requests left pending when switching between advice and a robust baseline. Under additive delay, waiting costs are charged separately to requests. Under maximum delay, one advice batch may share a waiting cost that the algorithm pays across several batches. Capacity can also force a switch to clear old requests in several services. Charging each clearance to the original advice batch would count its cost repeatedly. The switching idea. The algorithm simulates the advice and the baseline throughout the input. Advice is preferred whenever its budget permits; a smaller budget for the baseline limits spending away from advice. At a switch, clearing all old pending requests ensures that the new schedule can serve every request still waiting for the algorithm. The main issue is paying for these clearances while keeping consistency close to one. When leaving advice, the waiting charge is paid by the decrease in its remaining cost after deleting the requests just cleared. Different switches use successive decreases, rather than repeatedly charging an entire advice batch. The baseline budget pays the additional service cost. When returning to advice, the algorithm also clears requests that advice served while the baseline was active; these are charged within the baseline block. This restores the residual-advice comparison at every return.

## 4.5 A lower bound on robustness 

The dependence on ε is necessary even on inputs that fit in one batch at one location. Since these are also LAD inputs, the following lower bound applies to the general advice problem.

# 5 Conclusion and future directions 

The constant competitive ratio for capacitated LAD leaves several questions open. What are the optimal deterministic and randomized competitive ratios? Our bounds show that capacity does not force a logarithmic loss, but a gap remains even against the lower bounds from a single location. For online advice, our robustness is O(ε−2), while the deterministic lower bound is Ω( ε−1) uniformly in capacity. Closing this gap is another natural question. Extending the constant guarantee beyond a line is a further direction. Does capacitated JRP with maximum delay admit a constant competitive ratio or a constant approximation ratio? For rooted-tree MLA, how should the competitive ratio depend on capacity and tree depth? The LAD admission rule uses nested intervals to control both capacity and the cost of opening batches. For branching service costs, requests can share some edges while paying separately for others; an extension must account for both forms of cost sharing. Capacity constraints also merit study in online service and facility-location problems with waiting costs. How does maximum delay interact with capacity that can be reused through an opened facility, rather than assigned to a single instantaneous service? 21 A Capacitated TCP acknowledgment with maximum delay

## D.3 Completion and implementation 

We finish the proof of Theorem 4.1 by verifying completion and online computation. On a finite input, CB is finite, and hence (4.14) permits only finitely many levels and source changes. A final 

B block completes because B does. A final A block with a request pending forever would have unbounded D(PA, t ), exhausting its finite cap. Hence all requests are eventually served without an end-of-input signal. The cost bounds can first be applied to every finite prefix of physical actions, and hence this completion argument does not assume either bound circularly. Both packing values and both cleanups require only sorting. For fixed PX , ages have a fixed order and D(PX , t ) is affine with slope ⌈| PX |/k ⌉. Timers therefore use only current requests. Discrete jumps use the newly revealed event and the current source states; intermediate ineligible dyadic levels can be skipped directly. Under rational input encoding, including ε, the number of levels and the arithmetic overhead are polynomial whenever B is polynomially simulable. Malformed events are charged to the advice input length; as usual, event streams are locally finite.
