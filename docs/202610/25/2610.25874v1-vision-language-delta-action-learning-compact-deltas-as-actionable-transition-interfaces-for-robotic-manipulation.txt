Harbin Institute of Technology, Shenzhen 2Baidu, Inc. 3Shenzhen Loop Area Institute Robotic manipulation is not merely observation-to-action generation, but the realization of task-specified state changes in the physical world. Existing vision-language-action (VLA) methods directly learn observation-to-action mappings, imposing a substantial learning burden on policies due to the large gap between semantic understanding and action execution. Although world-action models (WAMs) improve control through fu-ture prediction, they optimize the full future rather than task-relevant changes, wasting capacity on static ap-pearance or action-irrelevant abstractions. To address this issue, we propose Vision-Language-Delta-Action (VLDA) , a new paradigm that reformulates full future prediction as compact actionable transition modeling. Our core insight is to introduce a compact Delta as an intermediate decision variable that captures latent world-state changes, serving as a unified representational interface between vision-language understanding and action generation. GT-Anchor provides coarse future-level regularization, ensuring that Delta can explain the overall trend of future changes. Building on these, Delta-Driven Action Refinement (DDAR) further grounds Delta in the action manifold and injects it as a structured seed to guide action denoising. We implement VLDA within LoongForge, an open-source training framework for large-scale foundation and embodied models, leveraging its modular and distributed infrastructure for unified two-stage optimization. Experiments show that VLDA improves long-horizon consistency, robustness, and generalization, validating actionable Delta as a more prin-cipled alternative to both direct action prediction and full future-state modeling.

1 Introduction 

Similar to human actions, robotic manipulation is a process of controlled physical state transformation [49, 56]. As shown in Fig. 1 (top), conditioned on visual observations, a robot must not only recognize the current scene and interpret the task-relevant physical context around it [23, 27], but also determine which part of the arXiv:2610.25874v1 [cs.RO] 19 Oct 2026 scene should change and how such change can be realized through continuous action execution [1, 43]. This requirement fundamentally distinguishes robotic manipulation from generic vision-language understanding, as the critical decision variable is not the complete visual state itself, but the task-relevant transition that drives the current state toward the desired physical outcome. 

Figure 1: Motivation and paradigm comparison of VLDA. Robotic manipulation is governed by sparse task-relevant state changes that provide es-sential cues for action generation, while most vi-sual content remains static. Unlike existing asyn-chronous and synchronous paradigms that predict complete future states before or alongside actions, VLDA learns a compact Delta from current-to-future transitions and uses it as an actionable in-terface to guide action refinement, forming an understanding-transition-execution loop. However, existing VLA and WAM paradigms share a common limitation in lacking a dedicated, action-grounded representation of task-relevant phys-ical transitions. 1) Most VLA models still follow an observation-to-action formulation that entangles task-relevant object identification [4, 26, 53, 61], de-sired physical state-change inference, and executable action-chunk generation within a single policy. With-out dedicated modeling of task-relevant state changes, the policy must implicitly infer the intended physi-cal transition from action supervision alone, leaving a substantial representational gap between semantic understanding and action execution. A straightforward way to bridge semantic under-standing and action execution is future modeling, as illustrated in Fig. 1 (a) and (b). 2) WAMs predict fu-ture observations [7, 54, 59], visual latents [39, 44], or action-conditioned dynamics [2, 25] to provide tem-poral cues beyond the current observation. However, they typically represent the future as another com-plete state, causing task-relevant transition cues to re-main entangled with static scene content and action-irrelevant variations. Moreover, future prediction is often used only as an auxiliary objective, without pro-viding a structured intermediate variable that can di-rectly guide action generation. Robotic manipulation instead requires a compact transition variable that captures task-relevant manipulation-centric changes, remains consistent with the overall direction of future evolution, and is directly grounded in executable actions. Taken together, these limitations motivate a transition interface that is selective, predictive, and action-able. It should isolate task-relevant manipulation-centric changes, remain consistent with future evolution, and be directly consumable by the action generator. To this end, we propose Vision-Language-Delta-Action (VLDA) , a new paradigm that models actionable transitions through a low-dimensional Delta interface rather than predicting the complete future state. Specifically, VLDA instantiates the transition interface as a compact low-dimensional Delta that cap-tures the task-relevant transition implied by the task. To make Delta both selective and predictive, VLDA introduces a unified learning objective with two complementary constraints. 1) Local Transition Filter (LT-Filter) distills sparse, manipulation-centric transition evidence from dense current-to-future visual dif-ferences, suppressing static scene content and action-irrelevant variations. 2) Global Transition Anchor (GT-Anchor) regularizes Delta with a coarse summary of future evolution, preventing overly local tran-sition modeling and preserving the global direction of state change. Together, these constraints reconcile local transition selectivity with global predictive consistency, yielding a compact and future-grounded rep-resentation of task-relevant physical change. To make Delta directly actionable, VLDA further introduces 3) Delta-Driven Action Refinement (DDAR) , which grounds Delta in the action manifold through Delta-action 2alignment and injects it into the action flow as a structured transition seed. The action expert then refines this transition-informed seed into executable action chunks conditioned on the predicted transition. Rather than treating future prediction as auxiliary supervision, VLDA therefore establishes a closed understanding-transition-execution loop , where vision-language representations interpret the task context, Delta specifies the required state transition, and DDAR realizes that transition through continuous action generation. We evaluate VLDA on standard robotic manipulation benchmarks and challenging long-horizon tasks. Experimental results show that VLDA improves long-horizon consistency, robustness under distribution shifts, and generalization to complex manipulation scenarios. These results demonstrate that modeling actionable Delta provides a more effective alternative to both direct observation-to-action learning and full future-state prediction. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce Vision-Language-Delta-Action (VLDA) , a new robotic manipulation paradigm that replaces complete future-state prediction with a compact Delta serving as an actionable transition interface. • We design a unified Delta learning objective with Local Transition Filter and Global Transition Anchor , enabling Delta to capture sparse task-relevant changes while remaining consistent with future transition trends. • We develop Delta-Driven Action Refinement , which aligns Delta with the action manifold and uses it as a structured seed for action denoising, forming an explicit understanding-transition-execution loop. 

2 Related Work 

Observation-to-Action Learning in VLA Models. Existing VLA models primarily employ action-token prediction, implemented either autoregressively or in parallel [5, 20, 26, 27, 32, 41], and continuous action experts based on diffusion or flow matching [4, 11, 17, 23, 33, 34, 51]. Despite different action decoders, both paradigms directly supervise actions from observations, requiring a single policy to jointly resolve task grounding, intended physical change, and motor realization. This creates a substantial gap between high-level semantic observations and low-level executable actions. VLDA bridges this gap with a compact Delta that explicitly mediates what should change and how that change is executed. 

Predictive World-Action Modeling. Recent WAMs couple future modeling with robot control through cascaded future-to-action generation [30, 47, 62], joint world-action prediction [7, 16, 54, 63], and predictive latent dynamics [18, 21, 40]. In these formulations, future prediction typically serves as an upstream context, a jointly decoded target, or a regularizer for action learning, with its contribution to control determined by architecture-specific fusion mechanisms. VLDA instead reparameterizes the prediction-control connection through a compact Delta, whose control role is explicitly defined by transition learning and Delta-driven action refinement. 

Latent Action Representation Learning. Discrete approaches tokenize visually inferred motion patterns for scalable VLA pretraining [6, 9, 52], while continuous formulations learn smooth latent control variables that can be mapped to embodiment-specific actions with limited supervision [35, 45]. CLAP [57] and LARA [37] further improve physical grounding by aligning visually inferred representations with robot actions or proprioceptive dynamics. However, these representations mainly serve as pseudo-action labels, pretraining targets, or shared control spaces. They do not explicitly isolate task-relevant physical changes under complementary local and global constraints, ground such changes in the action manifold, and use them to directly guide action refinement. 3Figure 2: Overview of VLDA. Given the observation and instruction, the World Expert predicts an ac-tionable Delta under transition supervision. In training stage I, LT-Filter and GT-Anchor learn selective and consistent transition representations. 5 Training Strategy 

5.1 Training Objective 

Following V-JEPA 2 [2], VLDA employs an online encoder Eon for current observations and an EMA teacher 

Eema for future observations. Specifically, Eon is optimized by gradients, whereas Eema is updated solely through exponential moving average (see Appendix D.1 ). This asymmetric design prevents the Delta target from being constructed between two rapidly changing feature spaces, thereby providing stable supervision for future-transition modeling. VLDA is optimized in two stages to progressively establish transition modeling and action generation. In the first stage (transition pre-training), we learn the transition interface with the Local Transition Filter and Global Transition Anchor objectives, while keeping the action branch frozen: 

L (1) = LLTF + LGTA . (23) 8Figure 3: Representative executions on Ring Stacking, Conveyor Sorting, Table Sweeping, and Basket Relocation, including two OOD spatial configurations. 5.2 Training Framework 

We implement and train VLDA within LoongForge 1 [46], an open-source training framework for large-scale foundation and embodied models. Its PyTorch-native embodied-model subsystem provides modular model, data, and trainer abstractions, supporting distributed training with DDP, ZeRO-1, FSDP, and HSDP. Together with flexible component-wise parameter freezing, checkpoint management, and experiment logging, this in-frastructure facilitates the staged optimization of VLDA’s VLM backbone, World Expert, Action Expert, and transition modules. Building upon these capabilities, we implement VLDA-specific Delta target con-struction, LT-Filter and GT-Anchor objectives, Delta–action alignment, and Delta-driven action refinement, integrating transition learning and action generation within a unified training pipeline. During inference, VLDA first samples ˆ ∆t with the World Expert and then uses it as a structured transition prior to guide action denoising via the Action Expert. This inference procedure preserves the understanding– transition–execution decomposition, allowing the predicted Delta to mediate between multimodal task un-derstanding and executable action generation without requiring access to future observations.

6.3 Real-world Experimental Analysis 

Quantitative Results. As shown in Tab. 3, VLDA achieves the best real-world performance at 73.0% ,outperforming π0.5 by 17.0 points. Basket Relocation further tests spatial generalization using randomized mat posi-tions during data collection and OOD target locations at evaluation. While both baselines degrade sharply at the final placement stage, VLDA retains 72% success ( +24 ), showing robust adaptation to unseen layouts. 

Qualitative Visualization. Fig. 3 shows executions across four real-world tasks. The current-to-future tran-sitions correspond to manipulation actions, including precise placement, tool-mediated transfer, dynamic in-terception, and bimanual transport. In Basket Relocation, VLDA adapts to the configuration and two OOD mat locations with horizontal and vertical displacements. These results demonstrate that actionable Delta captures manipulation-relevant local changes while preserving the global direction of the transition.

Ablation on LT-Filter and GT-Anchor. As shown in Tab. 5, both modules improve performance in sim-ulation and real-world settings, with their combination achieving the best results throughout. LT-Filter improves performance by suppressing static and action-irrelevant content, while GT-Anchor provides com-plementary global transition guidance. The gains are especially substantial under lighting ( +6.3 ) and background ( +3.7 ) shifts, indicating that Man-ifold Grounding steers Delta toward action-relevant transitions while suppressing action-irrelevant visual variations.

7 Conclusion 

We propose Vision-Language-Delta-Action (VLDA), which models compact actionable transitions instead of direct actions or complete future states. LT-Filter captures manipulation-relevant changes; GT-Anchor 12 preserves global transition consistency; DDAR grounds Delta and guides action refinement. Experiments demonstrate improved long-horizon consistency, robustness, and spatial generalization, validating action-able Delta as an effective interface between vision-language understanding and action execution.

RoboTwin 2.0. RoboTwin 2.0 [8] is a simulation benchmark designed for generalizable bimanual manip-ulation. It provides 50 dual-arm tasks involving diverse objects, scene configurations, and coordinated ma-nipulation skills. In addition to clean environments, the benchmark supports domain-randomized evaluation with variations in scene clutter, illumination, background textures, and tabletop height. These variations jointly alter both visual appearance and object geometry, making the benchmark particularly suitable for evaluating whether a policy can preserve bimanual coordination and task execution under substantial distri-bution shifts. Under our clean-to-randomized protocol, performance therefore reflects the ability to transfer manipulation knowledge beyond the narrow visual and spatial distribution observed during training. 

LIBERO-Plus. LIBERO-Plus [13] extends the original LIBERO environments with controlled perturba-tions along seven dimensions: object layouts, camera viewpoints, robot initial states, language instructions, lighting conditions, background textures, and sensor noise. It contains 10,030 perturbed task instances or-ganized into five difficulty levels. In contrast to the broad scene randomization used in RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO-Plus enables a factor-wise diagnosis of model robustness by isolating individual sources of dis-tribution shift, while also supporting evaluation under combined perturbations. Since no LIBERO-Plus-specific adaptation is performed in our experiments, this benchmark measures whether the representations learned from standard LIBERO remain reliable under unseen changes in viewpoint, robot configuration, visual appearance, language, and observation quality. 19 Figure 5: GALAXEA R1 Lite (left) and AgileX Cobot Magic (right) platforms. 

A.2 Real-world Platforms and Tasks 

GALAXEA R1 Lite Platform. As shown on the left of Fig. 5, the GALAXEA R1 Lite [15] is a 23-DoF mobile bimanual manipulation platform, comprising a wheeled chassis, an articulated torso, and two 6-DoF robotic arms equipped with parallel grippers. The movable base and torso extend the effective workspace of the robot, while platform-level and wrist-mounted cameras provide complementary global and close-range visual observations. We employ this platform to evaluate VLDA under large-workspace and coordinated bimanual manipulation settings. 

AgileX Cobot Magic Platform. As shown on the right of Fig. 5, the AgileX Cobot Magic platform [14] is a low-cost, open-source bimanual system developed upon Stanford’s Mobile ALOHA project for teleoperated data collection and imitation learning. It integrates a Tracer differential-drive mobile base, two 6-DoF follower arms with parallel-jaw grippers, and RGB-D sensors. During demonstration collection, the follower arms are controlled through a pair of leader arms, while fixed and wrist-mounted RGB cameras provide multi-view observations of the workspace and end-effector interactions. Compared with R1 Lite, ALOHA is operated in a stationary tabletop configuration, emphasizing precise, contact-rich, and tightly coordinated bimanual manipulation. 

Real-world Task Details. We design four long-horizon real-world tasks covering sequential spatial align-ment, contact-rich tool use, dynamic target interception, and bimanual object relocation. We collected human-teleoperated demonstrations from individual platforms, with corresponding sample counts of 100, 125, 125, and 150 in order. OpenVLA-OFT, π0.5, and VLDA are trained using the same demonstrations, observations, robot states, and control frequency to ensure a fair comparison. The language instructions specify only the task objective and necessary semantic order, without expos-ing low-level actions or evaluation checkpoints. For Basket Relocation, the blue mat is randomly positioned during data collection, and multiple target locations are used during evaluation to assess spatial generaliza-tion. Each method is evaluated over 25 physical trials per task and platform. The instructions and progressive checkpoints are summarized in Tab. 7. 

Evaluation Protocol. For each method, task, and platform, we conduct 25 independent physical trials. At the beginning of each trial, task-relevant objects are randomly perturbed within predefined regions while 20 preserving the task semantics. For Conveyor Sorting, the conveyor speed and object release interval are fixed across methods, while the ordering and spacing of target and distractor objects are varied. For Basket Relocation, the blue mat is placed at multiple target locations to evaluate spatial generalization. Success is recorded at each progressive checkpoint. A trial reaches checkpoint Ck only if all preceding checkpoints are successfully completed, and complete-task success is defined by the final checkpoint. All results are reported as success rates over 25 trials, while the average success rate is computed from the complete-task success rates across the four tasks. 

A.3 Model and Training Details 

Architecture and Initialization. VLDA is implemented on top of π0.5, which consists of a PaliGemma-initialized 2B vision-language backbone and a Gemma-300M Action Expert. We additionally introduce a World Expert with the same transformer architecture and model capacity as the Action Expert. The World Expert is initialized independently from scratch rather than from the pretrained Action Expert. For visual transition modeling, we adopt the pretrained V-JEPA 2 ViT-g/16 encoder ( vjepa2_vit_giant ) [2]. The online and teacher encoders are initialized from the encoder and EMA target-encoder branches of the pretrained V-JEPA 2 checkpoint, respectively. Both encoders process the primary-camera image at a reso-lution of 256 × 256, producing P = 256 spatial tokens with dv = 1408. The primary-view restriction applies only to transition supervision, while the policy backbone continues to process all available camera views at 224 × 224 resolution. The online encoder is optimized by gradients, whereas the teacher encoder remains gradient-free and is updated exclusively through EMA with momentum m = 0.99925. This asymmetric design provides a slowly evolving future-feature target throughout training. 

Delta and Projection Modules. The World Expert predicts K = 64 Delta tokens with d∆ = 256. The down-projection g↓ and up-projection g↑ are implemented as two-layer MLPs with a hidden dimension of 1024. The action-space bias projection qb uses the same hidden dimension. Its output layer is zero-initialized, and the unconstrained learnable scale γ is initialized to 0 .01. The mean-pooled Delta is linearly projected into a 1024-dimensional prefix token for the Action Expert. For Delta-action alignment, q∆ and qact each consist of LayerNorm followed by a linear projection into a shared 256-dimensional contrastive space. 

Two-Stage Training. VLDA is optimized in two stages. During the first stage, termed transition pre-training, we freeze the vision-language backbone and Action Expert, while optimizing the online V-JEPA 2 encoder, World Expert, and transition projection modules. This stage is trained for 10K optimization steps with a global batch size of 48. The second stage is initialized from the transition-pretraining checkpoint. We unfreeze the vision-language backbone and Action Expert, and jointly optimize all gradient-enabled modules for an additional 60K steps with a global batch size of 128. The World Expert, online encoder, and projection modules remain trainable, while the teacher encoder continues to receive no gradient and is updated solely through EMA. The LT-Filter and GT-Anchor objectives are equally weighted. We set λ(1) = 1 and λDDAR = 0.1 in the second-stage objective. The Delta-token pooling temperature and contrastive temperature are set to τ∆ = 0.1and τc = 0.07, respectively. Both training stages are conducted on a single machine equipped with 8 NVIDIA A800-SXM4 GPUs, each with 80 GB of memory.

Complementary Transition Constraints. During Stage I, GT-Anchor requires the predicted compact Delta to explain the pooled transition in the high-dimensional visual feature space. Under the pretrained and non-degenerate visual feature space, these pooled transition anchors vary across samples and execution stages, thereby discouraging the predicted Delta from collapsing to a sample-independent constant repre-sentation. During Stage II, Delta-to-Action Manifold Grounding further requires Delta to distinguish actions in the current chunk from actions sampled from other temporal regions of the same trajectory. Since these negative actions share the same task, scene, and embodiment context but correspond to different execution stages, the alignment objective encourages Delta to retain phase-specific and action-relevant transition information. The action-generation loss also propagates through the Delta-induced noise bias and Delta prefix token, providing additional action-dependent supervision to the World Expert and the predicted Delta representa-tion. 

Transition Representation Stabilization. Pretrained initialization, asymmetric gradient flow, and slow EMA updates provide a stable and structured future reference for current-to-future transition learning. GT-Anchor encourages sample-dependent global transition information, while Delta-to-Action Manifold Grounding and the action-generation objective further constrain Delta to encode phase-specific and execution-relevant factors. Together, these complementary visual, temporal, and action-grounded constraints reduce rapid co-adaptation and help maintain an informative and actionable Delta space throughout optimization. 

D.2 Entropy-Regularized Transport Matching 

The token collections compared in LT-Filter and GT-Anchor are treated as unordered sets. Specifically, LT-Filter matches the predicted and target Delta-velocity tokens, while GT-Anchor matches the projected Delta tokens with the global transition anchors. D.3 Supplementary Details on Motivation 

This section further explains the design considerations behind actionable Delta modeling, including the choice of transition targets, the complementary supervision objectives, and the role of Delta in action gen-eration. 

Why an Actionable Transition Interface? Direct observation-to-action policies must implicitly infer the desired physical change and translate it into motor commands within a single representation. Complete future-state prediction introduces temporal structure, but its target contains both the state information al-ready present in the current observation and the comparatively sparse changes caused by manipulation. Consequently, a large portion of its learning capacity may be devoted to reconstructing static objects, back-ground appearance, and other information that does not determine the next action. Delta provides an intermediate alternative between these two formulations. Rather than reproducing the complete future state or mapping semantic observations directly to actions, it explicitly represents the tran-sition between the current and future states. Feature differencing suppresses much of the shared appearance content, while the compact token space further limits residual redundancy. Delta is therefore not intended to reconstruct every future detail; it serves as a control-oriented interface that conveys the physical transition most useful for subsequent action generation. 

Why Horizon-Aligned Future Supervision? The future observation at t + H is aligned with the action chunk At = [ at , . . . , at+H−1]. It records the state reached after executing the actions covered by the current prediction horizon, making the current-to-future difference a natural supervision signal for the physical effect of that chunk. A substantially shorter transition would describe only an early fraction of the predicted motion, whereas a substantially longer transition could include state changes beyond the temporal scope of the current action chunk. 

Why Combine Local and Global Transition Supervision? A useful transition representation must be selective without becoming myopic. LT-Filter addresses selectivity by retaining regions with large feature-transition magnitudes. This suppresses the many spatial tokens whose current and future representations remain nearly unchanged and emphasizes candidate interaction regions. However, magnitude-based selec-tion alone can overemphasize isolated local changes or discard distributed transitions, such as the overall displacement of a large object. GT-Anchor provides the complementary constraint. By pooling before computing the transition, it re-tains a coarse summary of the overall state evolution without requiring reconstruction of the complete fu-ture feature map. LT-Filter can therefore be viewed as preserving high-resolution local evidence, while GT-Anchor preserves low-resolution global consistency. Their combination encourages Delta to capture manipulation-centric details while remaining compatible with the broader direction of future evolution. 

Why Ground Delta in the Action Manifold? Visual transition supervision alone does not guarantee action relevance. Feature differences may also reflect illumination changes, background motion, or object changes that are visually salient but unrelated to the demonstrated behavior. Delta-to-Action Manifold Grounding addresses this ambiguity by comparing every action in the current chunk with the predicted Delta-token set. 32 Actions from the corresponding chunk serve as positives, while actions from non-overlapping regions of the same trajectory serve as negatives. Sampling negatives from the same trajectory is important because it controls for much of the shared scene, task, and embodiment context. The alignment objective must therefore distinguish the action asso-ciated with the current local transition from other individually plausible actions in the same demonstration. As detailed in Appendix D.4, this action-wise InfoNCE objective admits a conditional mutual-information interpretation. It encourages Delta to retain transition factors that are informative about all actions in the current chunk rather than merely encoding visually predictive appearance changes. 

Why Two-Stage Optimization? The World Expert and the Delta pathway are newly introduced compo-nents, whereas the VLM and Action Expert inherit strong pretrained representations. If all objectives are optimized jointly from the beginning, the pretrained action pathway can reduce the action loss without con-sistently relying on the initially unstructured Delta representation. This can weaken the role of Delta as an explicit transition interface. The first stage therefore optimizes LT-Filter and GT-Anchor before joint action training, allowing the World Expert to establish a stable and future-grounded transition space. The second stage then introduces action generation and Delta–Action alignment, grounding this learned transition space in executable be-havior. The observed degradation under one-stage training further supports the benefit of establishing the transition interface before joint action refinement.

D.5 Limitations and Future Work 

While Vision-Language-Delta-Action (VLDA) provides a principled transition interface that improves ma-nipulation performance, robustness, and long-horizon consistency, several limitations remain to be addressed in future work: 

Sensitivity to Unrelated Visual Dynamics. The Local Transition Filter (LT-Filter) selects task-relevant changes based on the feature difference magnitude between current and future observations. Although the rich semantic priors from the V-JEPA 2 encoder significantly suppress pixel-level high-frequency noise, intense background motion (e.g., dynamic screens, moving bystanders) could occasionally pollute the top-K

selection. Future extensions could incorporate manipulation-centric visual grounding or instruction-attentive filters to explicitly isolate robot- and manipulation-centric regions from background clutter. 

Fixed Horizon State Transition Modeling. Currently, VLDA models state transitions over a fixed tem-poral horizon H, dictated by the action chunking size. However, complex real-world tasks often involve variable-speed trajectories or multi-rate execution where state transitions naturally span non-uniform tem-poral spans. Extending the compact Delta interface to hierarchical or adaptively spaced temporal horizons represents a promising avenue for flexible long-horizon execution. 

Cross-Embodiment Transition Transfer. Because the compact Delta representation focuses on physical world state changes rather than robot-specific joint configurations, it holds strong potential as an embodiment-agnostic control interface. Future work will investigate leveraging Delta targets for cross-robot skill transfer, where a transition model learned on one physical arm guides policy execution across diverse robotic plat-forms with minimal adaptation. 37
