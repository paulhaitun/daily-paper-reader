ABSTRACT
Large-scale action pretraining equips Embodied Foundation Models (EFMs) with
strong manipulation capabilities, with particular strengths in generalization and
closed-loop robustness, but reproducing such pretraining requires substantial
robot data and computation. To transfer these capabilities from pretrained EFMs
to policies without action pretraining, we introduce TILDE (Teacher-In-the-
Loop Distillation from EFMs), which synergizes on-policy distillation at student-
induced states with learning from teacher-executed recovery trajectories. We fur-
ther propose AutoDAgger, an autonomous data aggregation pipeline in which a
robotic reward model is utilized to monitor task progress and trigger teacher in-
tervention, thereby collecting recovery experience without human monitoring or
manual control. AutoDAgger accelerates recovery-trajectory collection by more

INTRODUCTION
Embodied Foundation Models (EFMs), including Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (Zhang
et al., 2025a; Shao et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2024; Sapkota et al., 2025), have demonstrated remarkable
manipulation capabilities and generalization. However, these capabilities often rely on action pre-
training on large-scale robot datasets (Bu et al., 2025; Padalkar et al., 2024; Walke et al., 2023; Khaz-
atsky et al., 2024), making it costly to endow new policy architectures, particularly efficient models,
with comparable capabilities (Yu et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025). Reproducing foundation-scale pre-
training requires access to sufficiently diverse, high-quality robot data and substantial computational
resources, which is prohibitively expensive in many research and deployment settings.
Policies without large-scale action pretraining (Lin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a; Commu-
nity, 2026) can still achieve competitive performance on standard simulation benchmarks such as
LIBERO-Spatial and LIBERO-Object (Liu et al., 2023). However, our pilot study suggests that this
apparent parity does not consistently extend to more challenging settings. As shown in Figure 1, sub-
stantially larger gaps emerge on long-horizon tasks in LIBERO-10, under visual and robot-initial-
state perturbations (Fei et al., 2025), and in real-world tasks. These observations are consistent
with action pretraining providing benefits beyond nominal action prediction, particularly improved
closed-loop robustness under challenging and out-of-distribution conditions. This motivates a natu-
ral question: Can such capabilities be transferred from a pretrained EFM to a student policy without
repeating expensive action pretraining?
Policy distillation offers an intuitive route by transferring teacher knowledge in a task-specific do-
main (Rusu et al., 2015; Dong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). However, for closed-loop policies,
effective distillation depends not only on what actions are supervised, but also on where supervision
is provided. When distillation is restricted to a fixed demonstration dataset (Figure 1(a)), supervision
remains concentrated on the nominal distribution. During closed-loop deployment, compounding
student errors and environmental stochasticity can drive the policy into out-of-distribution states.
This distribution mismatch between training and inference leaves the student insufficiently prepared
to handle execution deviations (Yan et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026).
Interactive imitation learning mitigates this mismatch by querying expert supervision at states in-
duced by the learner (Ross et al., 2011). In On-Policy Distillation (OPD) (Agarwal et al., 2024;
2023), the student interacts with the environment while the teacher provides action targets at student-
visited states (Figure 1(b)). This expands supervision to student-induced rollout states, but the
queried teacher actions are not executed (Zhong et al., 2026; Wan et al., 2026). Consequently,
OPD does not observe the subsequent states that would arise following the teacher’s correction.
Human-in-the-loop approaches such as HG-DAgger (Kelly et al., 2019) (Figure 1(c)) can collect
recovery trajectories but require continuous human monitoring and teleoperated takeover (Murray
et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025), severely limiting data scalability.
To address these limitations, we introduce TILDE (Teacher-In-the-Loop Distillation from EFMs),
a policy distillation framework that transfers teacher knowledge over three complementary data dis-
tributions: human demonstration, student-induced rollouts, and recovery trajectories (Figure 1(d)).
Central to TILDE is AutoDAgger, an autonomous teacher-in-the-loop data aggregation pipeline. An
off-the-shelf robotic reward model monitors student execution, while the execution manager uses
its feedback to trigger intervention, after which the EFM teacher directly takes control and executes
a recovery trajectory toward task completion. Automated progress monitoring removes the need
for expensive human oversight, while teacher policy takeover avoids the reaction and teleoperation
overhead of human intervention, substantially enhancing recovery-data collection throughput.
Given the aggregated data, TILDE applies supervision according to the sample source. Offline
demonstrations and teacher recovery trajectories provide both behavior-cloning targets from exe-
cuted actions and teacher distillation targets, while student rollout states use on-policy teacher su-
pervision only. Importantly, all distillation is performed directly in the common action space and

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
1. We formulate policy distillation from a distributional perspective and propose the TILDE frame-
work, which jointly transfers teacher knowledge over offline demonstrations, student-induced
rollouts, and teacher-generated recovery experience.
2. We introduce AutoDAgger, an autonomous teacher-in-the-loop data aggregation pipeline that ex-
pands the training distribution through automatic task monitoring and teacher takeover, enabling
efficient recovery-data collection while bypassing expensive human involvement.
3. Extensive experiments on real-world manipulation tasks demonstrate that TILDE improves stu-
dent performance by up to 4.1× over behavior cloning, consistently outperforms offline and
on-policy distillation, and enables effective cross-architecture capability transfer.

RELATED WORK
Embodied Foundation Models.
Recent advances in large-scale robot learning have given rise
to Embodied Foundation Models (EFMs) that integrate visual perception, language understanding,
and low-level control, showing impressive performance across a broad spectrum of dexterous tasks
and improved generalization to diverse environments (Zhang et al., 2025a; Shao et al., 2025; Ma
et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024b). Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models directly map multimodal
observations and language instructions to robot actions (Intelligence et al., 2025a; 2026; Bjorck
et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2026; Fan et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026). An emerging alternative is the
World Action Model (WAM), which jointly models future world states and robot actions, rather
than relying solely on reactive observation-to-action prediction (Ye et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026;
Yuan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). Despite their strong capabilities, training such foundation
models from scratch requires substantial data and computational resources. Our work instead studies
how the capabilities of a pretrained EFM can be transferred to a student policy without repeating
foundation-scale pretraining.
Policy Distillation.
Knowledge Distillation (KD) transfers predictions or representations from a
teacher to a smaller student and is widely used for model compression and capability transfer (Hinton
et al., 2015; Buciluˇa et al., 2006). Policy distillation extends this paradigm to robot learning (Rusu
et al., 2015; Torne et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025b), with recent
advances leveraging foundation models as teachers (J¨ulg et al., 2025). Existing methods perform
distillation through model-level alignment (Jeon et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2025) or teacher-generated
data and supervision (Shi et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2024a). On-Policy Distillation
(OPD) further queries teacher supervision on student-induced states rather than relying solely on
a fixed offline dataset (Agarwal et al., 2024; 2023), providing corrective signals where the student
tends to encounter uncertainty or make mistakes (Zhong et al., 2026; Wan et al., 2026). Our method
extends OPD by executing teacher takeover after intervention, thereby collecting the subsequent re-
covery trajectory and providing direct supervision for returning from off-nominal states to successful
task execution.
Interactive Imitation Learning.
Imitation learning suffers from distribution shift when the
learned policy encounters states absent from expert demonstrations. Interactive imitation learn-
ing mitigates this issue by querying an expert on learner-induced states and iteratively aggregating
the resulting supervision (Ross et al., 2011). Subsequent methods reduce expert queries using pre-
dicted novice–expert discrepancy (Hoque et al., 2021b), policy uncertainty (Menda et al., 2019;
Kelly et al., 2019; Zurbr¨ugg et al., 2026), or estimates of state novelty and task risk (Cronrath et al.,
2018; Hoque et al., 2021a). However, these approaches generally depend on policy-specific tech-
niques or continuous human oversight, and still request human teleoperation for corrective takeover
on physical robots (Murray et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025). In contrast, we propose a novel data ag-
gregation framework that automatically detects intervention needs and executes corrective actions,
substantially reducing the human-effort bottleneck in interactive data collection.

METHODOLOGY
3.1
PRELIMINARIES
Task Formulation.
We consider standard language-conditioned robotic manipulation with action-
chunking policies (Chi et al., 2023). Given an observation state s comprising RGB images and robot
proprioception, together with a language instruction l, a policy π predicts an action chunk:
π(s, l) = A = [a1, . . . , aH],
(1)
where H denotes the policy’s prediction horizon and ah is the h-th action in the chunk. During
closed-loop execution, the policy is repeatedly queried using updated observations.
DAgger.
Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) (Ross et al., 2011) is an interactive imitation learning al-
gorithm that addresses the distribution shift between offline demonstrations Ddemo and learner roll-
outs Droll by collecting expert action labels at learner-induced states. In the representative human-
in-the-loop variants, the human expert monitors the execution progress and takes control when the
learner’s behavior requires correction by providing recovery actions a⋆at the encountered observa-
tion s. The resulting labeled samples (s, l, a⋆) are added to an aggregated dataset Drec, which is
used to update the learner through behavior cloning.
3.2
OVERVIEW
TILDE transfers task-relevant capabilities from a capable and robust teacher policy πT to a student
policy πS. With a base human demonstration dataset Ddemo, the teacher policy is finetuned from
the pretrained EFM on the dataset to adapt to specific embodiment and target task, and remains
frozen in the framework. The student policy is initialized from a pretrained backbone (e.g., VLM
in a VLA model) but receives no robot action pretraining, and is trained on Ddemo using standard
behavior cloning. TILDE consists of two stages: (1) In the AutoDAgger data collection, the student
executes the task while a task monitor estimates progress and an execution manager triggers teacher
intervention when needed. The teacher takes over to perform recovery, and only episodes with con-
firmed teacher recovery are combined with the student rollout and added to the aggregated dataset.
(2) During action distillation, the student learns from teacher supervision on student-visited states,
together with corrective trajectories and existing demonstrations, combining knowledge distillation
with imitation learning of recorded expert behaviors.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Tasks and Evaluation.
We conduct real-world experiments with 7-DoF AgileX PiperX robot arms
on three manipulation tasks: (1) “Fold Dishcloth”: folding a dishcloth in half twice and placing it
at a target location toward the front left, testing deformable object manipulation; (2) “Pick Pen”:
picking up one to three pens and placing them in a cup, testing precise manipulation across object
locations; and (3) “Pick Beverage”: picking up a beverage and placing it in a paper box, focusing on
generalization across objects and locations. We collect 200 human demonstration episodes for Fold
Dishcloth and 100 for each of the other two tasks.
We evaluate each policy on the same predefined test cases, with fixed object types, positions, and
orientations: 12 trials for Fold Dishcloth, 27 for Pick Pen, and 30 for Pick Beverage. We define
fine-grained evaluation metrics to enable a more precise assessment of policy performance, with
the detailed scoring criteria provided in Appendix A.2. Scores are averaged across sub-steps and
evaluation trials, while the success rate (SR), which measures complete task success, is reported as
a complementary metric.
Implementation Details.
We obtain the teacher by finetuning the pretrained π0.5 VLA model (In-
telligence et al., 2025b) on the human demonstrations for each task. Unless otherwise specified, the
student employs the same architecture as π0.5 but receives no action pretraining: its VLM backbone
is initialized from PaliGemma (Beyer et al., 2024), while the action expert is trained from scratch.
The task monitor in AutoDAgger leverages the off-the-shelf robotic reward model Robometer (Liang
et al., 2026) without task-specific finetuning.

Baselines and Training Protocol.
We compare TILDE with three training paradigms: standard
behavior cloning (BC) (Bain & Sammut, 1995; Pomerleau, 1988), offline distillation, and On-Policy
Distillation (OPD). BC learns from recorded human actions, offline distillation additionally uses
teacher action targets on demonstration states, and OPD further incorporates teacher supervision at
student-rollout states. TILDE unifies learning from three complementary data distributions. Un-
less otherwise stated, comparisons use a shared pool of collected AutoDAgger data, containing 30
successfully recovered and retained episodes per task.
4.2
MAIN RESULTS
Figure 3 shows that offline distillation yields only modest improvements over BC, as teacher super-
vision remains confined to the demonstration distribution. OPD yields further gains by mitigating
distribution mismatch through adding teacher supervision at student-rollout states. TILDE achieves
scores of 63.9, 80.6, and 69.2 on Fold Dishcloth, Pick Pen, and Pick Beverage, respectively, out-
performing all evaluated baselines on each task. These scores are approximately 3.07×, 2.18×, and
4.14× those of the BC baseline, substantially narrowing the observed student–teacher performance
gap. These consistent improvements support the value of learning from recovery data in addition to
teacher action targets at student-induced states.
4.3
ANALYSIS
Ablation Study.
We first examine the contributions of student-rollout supervision and teacher-
executed recovery trajectories using offline distillation as the common baseline. As shown in Fig-
ure 4 (left), adding either source improves performance on all three tasks, while combining both
produces the highest scores. These results indicate that each source improves performance when the
other is already present, supporting their complementary roles in transferring teacher behavior.
Figure 4 (right) further analyzes the supervision used on demonstrations and recovery trajectories.
Recovery BC adds imitation of the teacher’s recorded actions to conventional BC and improves
performance on all three tasks. Recovery Distill additionally applies knowledge distillation to both
demonstration and recovery samples, achieving further advantages.

Recovery from Failures.
To directly assess whether recovery trajectories from teacher policy im-
prove closed-loop correction behavior, we construct a standardized recovery-state evaluation with a
common set of 12 distinct error states on Fold Dishcloth. Each state deviates from nominal execu-
tion and requires corrective behavior before the task can be completed. As shown in Table 1, the
BC baseline succeeds from only 4 out of 12 states, while offline distillation and OPD improve this
result slightly to 5/12. TILDE succeeds in 8 cases, compared with 9/12 for the pretrained teacher.
These results support the value of learning from the subsequent states generated by executing teacher
corrections, where TILDE transfers substantially stronger recovery capability.
Scaling with AutoDAgger Data.
Table 2 studies how performance scales with the amount of
AutoDAgger data. Starting from a score of 27.8 with offline distillation on the human demonstration
dataset, the student improves to 63.9, 65.3, and 77.8 after aggregating 30, 60, and 90 AutoDAgger
episodes, respectively. The corresponding success rates exhibit a similar upward trend, indicating
consistent improvement. Notably, with 90 AutoDAgger episodes, the student reaches a score of
77.8, narrowing the gap to the teacher score of 87.5. These results suggest that TILDE could benefit
further from continued scaling of interactive recovery data.
Quality and Efficiency of AutoDAgger Data.

with the same number of additional human teleoperation episodes or AutoDAgger episodes per task.
Under BC, AutoDAgger data yield scores of 48.6, 63.0, 50.0 on Fold Dishcloth, Pick Pen, and Pick

We further compare AutoDAgger with HG-DAgger (Kelly et al., 2019), a representative human-
in-the-loop data aggregation method, in terms of downstream policy performance, data collection
efficiency, and intervention success. As shown in Table 4, AutoDAgger substantially reduces the
time required to collect recovery trajectories across all three tasks. This improvement primarily
stems from direct takeover by the teacher policy. Once an intervention is triggered, the teacher
can immediately generate and execute corrective actions from the current state, whereas human
teleoperation incurs additional reaction and control overhead.
Despite replacing human intervention with automated teacher takeover, AutoDAgger achieves re-
covery success rates comparable to those of HG-DAgger. Recovery is not always successful for
either method, as some failure states are inherently difficult to recover from. Importantly, HG-
DAgger requires a human operator to continuously monitor execution and manually take over when
intervention is needed, whereas AutoDAgger automates both processes. By eliminating human in-
volvement during data collection while substantially increasing collection throughput, AutoDAgger
provides a more scalable alternative to human-in-the-loop data aggregation.
Faster recovery offers an opportunity to close the small performance gap through additional au-
tonomous data collection. On Fold Dishcloth, increasing the AutoDAgger dataset to 60 episodes
raises the score to 65.3 (Table 2), matching HG-DAgger with 30 episodes. Combined with the
2.53× recovery-time speedup, this result suggests that AutoDAgger can use its collection efficiency
to improve downstream performance within a comparable time budget.
Cross-architecture Distillation.
We investigate whether TILDE can transfer capabilities across
heterogeneous model architectures. Since our approach performs distillation purely through action-
space alignment rather than intermediate feature alignment, the teacher and student can employ
different architectures and action-generation mechanisms. We use SmolVLA (Shukor et al., 2025)
without action pretraining as an efficient student policy and distill knowledge from the π0.5 teacher
on the LIBERO benchmark. As shown in Table 5, TILDE consistently improves the student and
outperforms conventional offline distillation. On the long-horizon LIBERO-10 suite, success in-
creases from 64.6% to 79.6%, consistent with our motivation to transfer capabilities needed in more
challenging settings. These results validate the broader applicability of our framework and suggest
that pretrained EFMs can serve as reusable teachers for new policy architectures.
Validation of Task Monitor.
Since AutoDAgger triggers interventions based on task execution
estimates from a task monitor implemented with the Robometer reward model, we further evaluate
its ability to distinguish successful and failed rollouts on the LIBERO benchmark. We collect 200
SmolVLA rollout episodes on each suite and compare the model’s success predictions with the
ground-truth outcomes provided by the simulator. As shown in Table 6, Robometer achieves strong
performance across the four suites, although recall decreases on the more challenging LIBERO-
10 tasks. These results support its use as an off-the-shelf, policy-independent task monitor for
AutoDAgger without task-specific finetuning.

CONCLUSION
In this work, we study how capabilities acquired through large-scale action pretraining can be trans-
ferred from pretrained EFMs to new student policies without repeating foundation-scale pretrain-
ing. TILDE combines teacher supervision at student-induced states with teacher-executed recovery
trajectories, covering both corrective actions and the subsequent recovery-state distribution. Au-

for subsequent policy training. Episodes successfully completed by the student are also discarded,

vary across tasks to account for differences in execution dynamics and expected duration. For exam-

valid execution, whereas picking tasks typically involve more temporally compact and visually ap-
parent progress.
A.2
REAL-WORLD EXPERIMENTS
Hardware Setup. We conduct our real-robot experiments on an AgileX PiperX dual-arm robot plat-
form, as illustrated in Figure 5, following the leader-follower teleoperation design of ALOHA-style
systems (Zhao et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024). The platform consists of four 7-DoF (6 joints + 1 grip-
per) PiperX arms: two leader arms operated by a human demonstrator and two corresponding fol-
lower arms that reproduce the leader commands during data collection and execute policy-generated
actions during autonomous inference. The visual sensing system comprises one front-facing In-
tel RealSense D455 camera and two Intel RealSense D435 wrist cameras, with one wrist camera
mounted on each follower arm.
Tasks. We evaluate our method on three real-robot manipulation tasks: “Fold Dishcloth”, “Pick
Pen”, and “Pick Beverage”. These tasks cover deformable-object manipulation, precision grasping,
and object relocation. Illustrations of the three tasks are provided in Figure 6.
The language instructions for the tasks are:
• Fold Dishcloth: “Fold the dishcloth in half twice, then place it in the position slightly to the front
and left.”
• Pick Pen: “Pick up the scattered pens one by one and place them in the yellow cup.”

Evaluation Protocol.
Each method is evaluated using a fixed number of real-robot rollouts per
task: 12 trials for Fold Dishcloth, 27 trials for Pick Pen, and 30 trials for Pick Beverage. We use
predefined test configurations, including fixed object categories and controlled variations in object
position and orientation, and keep these configurations as consistent as practically possible across
all evaluated methods. A rollout is considered successful only if the complete task is accomplished
within the maximum episode length without human intervention.
In addition to binary task success, we report a fine-grained success score to capture both partial task
completion and execution quality under a limited real-robot evaluation budget. Each task is decom-
posed into semantically meaningful stages. Each stage receives a score of 0, 0.5, or 1, corresponding
to failure, partial completion or successful completion after repeated attempts, and successful com-
pletion in a single attempt, including immediate recovery from minor execution issues, respectively.
The task-level score is computed by averaging over all stages and evaluation rollouts, and then mul-
tiplied by 100 for better display. The scoring criteria are as follows:
1. Fold Dishcloth
• Stage 1: Folding the dishcloth in half from left to right
– 0: The robot fails to complete the fold after multiple attempts.
– 0.5: The robot completes the fold after several attempts or demonstrates partial recovery
from an execution error during the process.
– 1: The robot completes the fold in a single attempt, including cases in which it immedi-
ately recovers from minor issues such as a flipped corner.
• Stage 2: Folding the dishcloth in half a second time, from bottom to top
– 0: The robot fails to complete the second fold after multiple attempts.
– 0.5: The robot completes the second fold after several attempts or demonstrates partial
recovery from an execution error during the process.
– 1: The robot completes the second fold in a single attempt, including cases in which it
immediately recovers from minor issues such as a flipped corner.
• Stage 3: Picking up the folded dishcloth and placing it in the front-left target region
– 0: The robot fails to pick up and place the folded dishcloth after multiple attempts.
– 0.5: The robot completes the pick-and-place operation after several attempts or demon-
strates partial recovery from an execution error during the process.
– 1: The robot completes the pick-and-place operation in a single attempt.
2. Pick Pen
• Stage 1: Grasping the pen
– 0: The robot fails to grasp the pen.
– 0.5: The robot grasps the pen after repeated attempts or recovers from an unsuccessful
grasp.
– 1: The robot grasps the pen correctly on the first attempt.
• Stage 2: Placing the pen into the cup
– 0: The pen is not placed into the cup or is dropped outside it.

– 0.5: The pen is placed into the cup after a recovery attempt or with substantial contact

A.3
SIMULATION BENCHMARKS
LIBERO.
The LIBERO benchmark (Liu et al., 2023) has four task suites—Spatial, Object, Goal,
and 10 (Long)—each emphasizing different manipulation capabilities. We train a single policy
jointly across all four suites. For π0.5, VLA-Adapter-Pro, and SmolVLA, we use the dataset released
by OpenVLA1 (Kim et al., 2024) and convert it to the LeRobot format (Cadene et al., 2026). For
X-VLA, we instead use the HDF5-format dataset released by the X-VLA authors2, which represents
actions in the EE6D action space. Each suite consists of 10 tasks, and each task is evaluated over 50
rollouts.
LIBERO-Plus.
LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2025) extends LIBERO with seven dimensions of visual
and configuration perturbations. In our pilot study (Figure 1), we evaluate the “Background” setting,
which perturbs scene and surface appearance, and the “Robot” setting, which varies the manipula-
tor’s initial pose. These settings contain 1,076 and 1,550 task instances, respectively. The evalu-
ations are performed using checkpoints trained exclusively on the original LIBERO data, thereby
measuring out-of-distribution generalization under environmental and embodiment perturbations.
A.4
TRAINING SETUP
We train π0.53, X-VLA4, VLA-Adapter5, and SmolVLA6 based on their official codebases, with
modifications to support our training pipeline and data formats. For the action-pretraining-free vari-
ants of π0.5 and X-VLA, we initialize the model from VLM backbones: PaliGemma (Beyer et al.,
2024) for π0.5, and Florence-2-large (Xiao et al., 2024) for X-VLA, while training the action experts
from scratch.
We follow the default or recommended training configurations for each model, with the key hyper-
parameters summarized in Table 8 and Table 9. During training, samples are drawn randomly from
the pooled training datasets without additional dataset balancing, and the training loss for each sam-
ple is determined by its data source. The supervision applied to each training variant is summarized
in Table 10. We set the distillation loss weight λKD = 1 for all experiments. For real-robot experi-
ments, demonstrations are recorded in absolute joint space at 30FPS and converted to relative joint
actions by subtracting the corresponding proprioceptive states if needed.
For AutoDAgger data collection, the student collection policy is first trained on Ddemo using be-
havior cloning only. We then perform AutoDAgger collection using this fixed BC baseline. After
collection, we reinitialize the student and train on the aggregated dataset.
During distillation, when both the teacher and student use the π0.5 architecture, we compute the dis-
tillation loss LKD(s, l) by directly matching their flow-matching velocity predictions using shared
noise samples. When the student uses a different architecture, such as SmolVLA, we instead use the
teacher’s fully-denoised action predictions as supervision targets and train the student with the same
imitation learning objective used for ground-truth actions.
All models are trained on 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs with 80GB VRAM. Training takes approximately
1–2 days for π0.5 and less than one day for the other models.
A.5
ROBOT DEPLOYMENT SETUP
We deploy the policy and task-monitor models on a workstation equipped with NVIDIA RTX 4090
GPU. The inference latency is 80.0±1.6ms for π0.5 and 266.9±0.5ms for the task monitor Robome-
ter. The robot-side client runs on ROS at a fixed control frequency of 30Hz. We employ asyn-
chronous inference together with temporal ensembling to enable continuous control and smooth the
action trajectories.

In Table 11, Positive denotes that the monitoring system predicts task success, while Negative de-

respectively. The monitoring system correctly identifies 8 of the 10 successful trajectories and 9 of

real-robot experiments, these results indicate that the task monitor provides a practical signal for
triggering teacher takeover and verifying recovery outcomes.

B.3
VISUALIZATION OF AUTODAGGER COLLECTION
We visualize representative AutoDAgger collection trajectories for Fold Dishcloth, Pick Pen, and
Pick Beverage tasks in Figure 7. Before intervention, the predicted task progress initially increases
during student execution but subsequently stagnates, while the success probability remains well be-
low θs = 0.5. The execution manager consequently detects the combination of sustained progress
stagnation and low success probability, triggering teacher takeover at the red-bordered frames. Fol-
lowing takeover, the predicted progress may decrease temporarily as the teacher corrects the student-
induced state. It subsequently resumes increasing as recovery proceeds, reaching 0.869, 0.814, and
0.847 for the three tasks. Meanwhile, the success probability remains low during most of the re-
covery process and rises above θs only near task completion, reaching 0.844, 0.582, and 0.926,
respectively.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Despite the improved student performance demonstrated by TILDE, several limitations remain.
First, TILDE is evaluated with a single round of AutoDAgger collection followed by student train-
ing, rather than iterative data aggregation. Extending the experiments to alternate between policy
updates and subsequent rounds of AutoDAgger could progressively expose the student to increas-
21


Preprint
ingly relevant failure and recovery states. Second, AutoDAgger relies on the accuracy of the task
monitor and the recoverability of the teacher policy; incorrect intervention decisions or failure states
beyond the teacher’s capability may reduce the quality or efficiency of collected data. More robust
task monitoring and adaptive intervention strategies are therefore promising directions. Finally, our
experimental evaluation focuses on a limited set of real-world tasks, simulation benchmarks, and
teacher–student architectures. Future work will scale TILDE to broader tasks, embodiments, and
heterogeneous policy families, and investigate how closely student policies can approach pretrained
EFMs with continued autonomous data aggregation and distillation.
