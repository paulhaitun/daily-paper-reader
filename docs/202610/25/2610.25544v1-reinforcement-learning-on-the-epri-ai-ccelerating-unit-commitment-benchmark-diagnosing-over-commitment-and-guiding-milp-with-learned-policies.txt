# Reinforcement learning on the EPRI AI-ccelerating Unit Commitment benchmark: diagnosing over-commitment and guiding MILP with learned policies 

Maharani Devira Pramita a, Aidan O’Sullivan a,*  

> a

UCL Energy Institute, Bartlett School of Environment, Energy and Resources, University College London, Central House, 14 Upper Woburn Place, London WC1H 0NN, United Kingdom * Corresponding author. E-mail address: aidan.osullivan@ucl.ac.uk 

Highlights 

• First RL study on the EPRI benchmark: agents commit twice the optimal fleet. • Spare units insure against exploration: injected noise enlarges the fleet. • Imitation and conservative fine-tuning give the lowest penalised cost tested. • Fixing the fine-tuned policy’s confident decisions speeds the MILP 3.8–8.5-fold. • A scope-matched optimum and a simple rule are needed to judge learned schedulers. 

Abstract 

Reinforcement learning (RL) is increasingly proposed as a fast solver for unit commitment (UC), but standalone agents remain far more expensive than mixed-integer linear programming (MILP). In the first study of RL on the 51-generator, 72-hour benchmark of the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) AI-ccelerating Unit Commitment competition, we trace this gap to the agents’ own exploration, close most of it, and show that the resulting policy can accelerate an exact solver. In every configuration tested, Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) and an action-branching Deep Q-Network (DQN) trained from scratch committed about twice the units of a thermal-only MILP optimum, at 39–64% higher cost, whereas a merit-order rule came within 1.6–7% (10–24% when unserved energy is priced). When each unit’s decision is sampled independently, spare units insure against random de-commitments. Consistent with this, injecting exploration noise during fine-tuning enlarged a lean policy’s fleet, a reward without shaping terms did not remove the over-commitment, and under their training noise the agents outperformed the rule subjected to comparable noise. Imitating the MILP solutions and fine-tuning conservatively brought PPO within 2.6% of the optimum, and these policies had the lowest penalised cost of any evaluated (about 5%). Fixing the fine-tuned policy’s confident decisions made the MILP 3.8–8.5 times faster within 0.10–0.25% of the best solution, with a smaller worst case than an imitation-only policy fixing as many variables (1.1% against 8.9%). Because dispatch method and benchmark scope alone shift the reported optimality ratio by up to 3.6% and 9%, we propose a reporting protocol. 2

Keywords: Unit commitment; Reinforcement learning; Exploration; Imitation learning; Benchmarking; Power system scheduling

1. Introduction 

This section sets out why the study was undertaken and what it contributes. Section 1.1 describes the computational burden of unit commitment and the gap between standalone RL and MILP, and Section 1.2 states the contributions. 

1.1. Motivation 

The unit commitment (UC) problem determines which thermal generating units to bring online over a multi-hour horizon so that demand is met at minimum cost, subject to minimum stable output, ramping, and minimum up- and down-time constraints [1,2]. The binary commitment decisions are coupled across units and across time, and the problem is strongly NP-hard [3]. Mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) is the industry standard, and tight formulations now solve large instances to small optimality gaps [4,5]. Its limitation is computational: each scenario requires a new combinatorial solve, and variable renewable generation increases how often schedules must be recomputed [6,7]. This computational burden has motivated a growing literature on learned approximate schedulers that move effort into an offline training phase and produce schedules in seconds at inference [8–10]. Reinforcement learning (RL) is a natural fit because commitment is sequential: a decision taken now constrains the choices available in later hours [11,12]. 3Model-free RL has been applied to UC at increasing scale [13–18], and hybrid methods that combine a learned policy with search or optimisation achieve cost close to MILP [19–22]. Standalone model-free policies remain more expensive than mathematical programming, and the gap is usually attributed to the intrinsic difficulty of coordinating many binary decisions [20,22]. Understanding why they fall short is the step that would let them close the gap. Learned schedulers, whether trained by RL or supervised learning, are usually evaluated by an optimality ratio against a MILP benchmark and a feasibility or fulfilment rate [9,13,16,17,20,21], which shows how far a schedule is from the benchmark but not what kind of schedule it is. Two further diagnostics would add to these: whether a policy has optimised its own training reward, and whether a simple rule would do as well. Both echo a broader literature on evaluation practice in deep RL, which has shown that conclusions drawn from few random seeds and point estimates are frequently unreliable [23–25]. 

1.2. Contributions 

This paper presents, to the authors’ knowledge, the first study of RL on the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) AI-ccelerating Unit Commitment benchmark [9,10,26]. It explains why standalone RL over-commits on this benchmark, a single-bus representation of the Irish test system with 51 thermal units and a 72-hour horizon, and shows how most of the gap to the optimum can be closed. The main contributions of this paper are that it: 1. establishes the first RL baselines on the benchmark, comparing Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) and an action-branching Deep Q-Network (DQN) with three references (a scope-matched MILP optimum, a merit-order rule and a static-fleet control) on cost and on the agents’ own training reward, and finding that agents trained from scratch commit about twice the optimal fleet in every reward, masking and hyperparameter setting tested; 2. identifies a mechanism for the over-commitment and tests it directly: with factorised per-unit actions, spare capacity is reward-optimal insurance against the agent’s own exploration. Injecting noise into a lean policy during fine-tuning enlarges its fleet, a reward without shaping terms leaves the over-commitment in place, the agents outperform the rule under comparable noise, and fine-tuned DQN’s fleet increases with its exploration rate; 3. demonstrates that a lean PPO policy initialised by imitation drifts back towards over-commitment under standard but not conservative fine-tuning, and that DQN shows an additional, noise-independent drift; 4. finds that imitation of the MILP solutions followed by conservative fine-tuning, with a reward equal to the MILP objective and a look-ahead dispatcher, gives the lowest penalised cost of any policy evaluated; 5. shows that the fine-tuned policy can accelerate an exact solver: fixing its confident decisions gives a several-fold speed-up at a small cost, with a smaller worst case than an imitation-only policy fixing as many variables and a much smaller error than a merit-order heuristic; 6. quantifies how dispatch, unserved energy and benchmark scope change the reported optimality ratio, and sets out a reporting protocol for learned UC schedulers. 4

2. Literature review 

This section reviews the work on which the study builds. Section 2.1 covers optimisation-based UC and the learned methods that support a MILP solver, Section 2.2 reinforcement learning for UC, and Section 2.3 imitation learning and fine-tuning. Section 2.4 identifies the gap this paper addresses. 

2.1. Optimisation-based unit commitment 

UC combines binary commitment with continuous dispatch decisions and inter-temporal constraints [1]. Lagrangian relaxation, dynamic programming and metaheuristics were historically prominent [2,27], but MILP became dominant once solvers and formulations matured, because it represents constraints exactly and provides optimality bounds [4,5]. Its persistent limitation is not solution quality but the cost of repeated solves, which has motivated learning methods that predict commitments or warm-start the solver [8–10], a use of learned predictions to fix variables that is also established for general mixed-integer programmes [28]. Two such methods have been reported on the EPRI benchmark, both supervised and both retaining a MILP solver at inference. The competition winner predicts commitment probabilities with a nearest-neighbour classifier and fixes confidently predicted variables, with generator-specific thresholds tuned on validation instances, reporting a mean optimality gap of 0.48% at a mean speed-up of 20.8 times [10]. The second combines a Transformer-based commitment predictor with deterministic feasibility repair and a warm-started MILP; the raw prediction is feasible in 31.6% of cases, while the full pipeline reaches 100% feasibility at a median optimality ratio of 1.0007 [9]. 

2.2. Reinforcement learning for unit commitment 

Dalal and Mannor [13] formulated UC as a Markov decision process on a 12-generator system and obtained schedules faster than conventional search. Subsequent work applied tabular and distributed Q-learning [14,17], fuzzy RL [15], deep policy-gradient methods under load and wind uncertainty [16], PPO with separate dispatch optimisation [18], and quantum RL within a two-stage framework [29]. Reviews of RL in power systems identify scalability, constraint satisfaction and evaluation against optimisation baselines as recurring open problems [12,30,31]. Because standalone policies remain more expensive than MILP and the gap widens with system size [22], higher-performing approaches restrict or refine the learned policy: guided tree search over a policy-pruned action space [19,20,32], or optimisation-assisted candidate actions [21]. Related supervised approaches replace the RL policy with a commitment predictor that warm-starts or fixes variables in a MILP solver [9,10]; to our knowledge, whether an RL policy can serve the same purpose has not been tested. Most RL studies of UC consider 5 to 30 generators [13,20,21]; the 51-unit EPRI system is larger than most. Because the joint action space grows as 2|𝒢| , standalone agents usually factorise the action into per-generator decisions that share a system reward, either as independent Bernoulli outputs of a policy or as separate value branches [33]. This resembles independent multi-agent learning [34] and value decomposition [35], but additivity does not hold in UC, because the value of committing one unit depends on which others are online. Independent learners in cooperative settings are known to prefer actions that are robust to their teammates’ 5exploration over jointly optimal ones, a pathology termed relative overgeneralisation [36]. Masking invalid actions before sampling, rather than projecting them afterwards, yields a valid policy gradient [37,38]. Reward shaping preserves the optimal policy only when the shaping terms take a specific potential-based form [39,40]. That exploration shapes what an agent learns is well known in principle. In the cliff-walking example of Sutton and Barto [11], on-policy SARSA learns a longer, safer path than Q-learning because it evaluates the policy it follows, including its exploratory steps, rather than the greedy policy. Joint or autoregressive action heads, which condition each sub-action on those already chosen [41,42], are one way to avoid independent sampling across action dimensions. What has not been examined is how strongly this effect shapes the schedules of factorised RL agents for UC, where many binary decisions jointly secure a heavily penalised constraint. 

2.3. Imitation and fine-tuning 

When a competent policy or expert is available, behaviour cloning trains a policy by supervised learning on expert decisions [43]. Cloned policies are vulnerable to compounding errors once they leave the states the expert visited, which interactive methods such as Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) address by querying the expert on the learner’s own states [44]. Demonstrations can also initialise RL: DQN from demonstrations combines temporal-difference learning with a large-margin loss on expert actions [45], and policy-gradient methods can be pre-trained on demonstrations and then fine-tuned [46,47]. A recurring observation is that online fine-tuning can erode the initial policy before the value estimates are reliable, which motivates conservative or constrained updates in offline-to-online RL [48– 50]. In UC, the optimal MILP solutions supplied with a benchmark are a natural source of demonstrations, and the learned predictors that warm-start MILP solvers [8–10] are imitation learners in this sense. 

2.4. Research gap 

RL-for-UC studies usually report aggregate cost and feasibility against a MILP benchmark. Three diagnostics are not standard in this literature: comparison with a simple rule and with an optimum solved for exactly the problem the agent faces, a test of whether the agent has optimised its own training objective, and an explanation of the characteristic behaviour of policies that fall short of the benchmark. Published work on the EPRI benchmark has so far combined supervised prediction with a MILP solver [9,10]. This paper provides the first RL study on it, applies all three diagnostics, identifies the role of exploration in the gap, tests imitation-based remedies against the same optimum, and tests whether the resulting policy can guide an exact solver. 

3. Problem setup 

Here we provide an overview of the EPRI competition framework and describe key characteristics of the problem including; operational constraints, reward formulation, state representation and the economic dispatch approaches used. 6

3.1. Benchmark and data 

We use the EPRI AI-ccelerating Unit Commitment competition dataset [26], a single-bus representation of the Irish test system also used by Za’ter et al. [9] and Fritz et al. [10], with instances generated in UnitCommitment.jl [51]. It contains 2,621 operating instances labelled 2021–2029 across all four quarters, the count also reported by Fritz et al. [10]. Each instance defines a 72-hour horizon at hourly resolution, with demand, wind and solar profiles, the initial status of each unit, and the technical and cost characteristics of 51 thermal generators with a combined capacity of 8,515 MW. The unit data are identical across instances; only the profiles and initial conditions vary. Twenty-four medium units (100–500 MW) provide 82.9% of capacity. Production cost curves are convex and piecewise linear with one to four segments, start-up costs reach about $46,000, and several units with high start-up costs have low marginal costs, which creates the usual trade-off between cycling and running. We restrict the problem to the thermal fleet, the dominant flexible resource and the source of every binary commitment decision. This is the simplest version of the problem that retains its combinatorial core, which makes the behaviour of the learned policies easier to diagnose: the hydro, battery and pumped-storage assets of the full competition system would add continuous decisions that carry state between hours and could absorb or mask the effects studied here. Extending the analysis to the full system is left to future work (Section 7). Perfect foresight of demand, wind and solar is assumed, consistent with the competition formulation, so results characterise deterministic scheduling. The training set comprises 300 instances drawn at random from all quarters except the fourth quarters of 2026–2029; the validation set comprises 25 instances from those fourth quarters; and 100 test instances were drawn at random from the instances not used for training or validation. Each instance also carries the benchmark solution of the full competition system.

Constraint handling. A unit that has not completed its minimum up-time cannot be switched off, and a unit that has not completed its minimum down-time cannot be switched on. A unit whose minimum output exceeds its ramp-down limit cannot be switched off at all, which applies to two units. The benchmark’s shutdown limits are otherwise not modelled, so other units may shut down from any output level (Section 6.4). A unit that none of these rules binds is termed free. 3.5. Economic dispatch 

Economic dispatch is resolved after commitment, following the standard two-stage decomposition in which the agent addresses the combinatorial commitment problem and a deterministic method allocates output [1,19,22]. Three methods are used, each respecting ramp limits relative to the previous hour’s dispatch. Proportional loading , used with R2 unless stated, sets each unit at minimum output and distributes the residual in proportion to committed headroom, approximating practice in which reserve is spread across the fleet [53], and snaps outputs to discrete levels of 5% of 𝑃 (. Economic dispatch fills the residual from the cheapest cost-curve segments of the committed units, which is exact within each hour for convex piecewise-linear costs and reproduces the MILP dispatch cost for a given commitment to within 0.01%. Both are myopic: the agent observes the full forecast but controls only commitment, and an hourly dispatcher cannot raise output ahead of a steep rise in net demand. Look-ahead dispatch solves a linear programme over the next four hours with the current commitment held fixed, the known net demand and the penalties of Eq. (1), and applies the first hour. On the MILP’s own schedules for the 100 test instances, the hourly economic dispatcher leaves 5.8 MWh of unserved energy per instance and is feasible on 80% of instances; the look-ahead dispatcher leaves less than 0.01 MWh and is feasible on all of them. These comparisons use the MILP’s schedules on the test instances, not those of any learned policy, so they do not favour any of the policies compared. A four-hour window performed better than six hours, because a longer window extends the assumption that the current commitment persists; the dispatcher is therefore an approximation to the joint 9commitment and dispatch of the MILP. All results in Section 5 use look-ahead dispatch unless stated.

4.2. Reference policies 

Four reference policies are evaluated with the same dispatch and cost pipeline as the agents. 

Thermal-only MILP optimum. Eq. (1) is solved for all 100 test instances with a 100 s limit and a 0.1% relative gap on one CPU core; 77 were solved to proven optimality, the largest remaining gap was 0.46% and the median solve time was 52 s. We refer to this solution as the optimum; where optimality was not proven, it lies within 0.46% of the bound. The same model was solved for all 300 training and 25 validation instances with a 40 s limit (median gap 0.11%, 95th percentile 0.78%), to provide demonstrations and relative-reward baselines. Its commitment is replayed through the environment and the evaluation dispatcher, so that its results include any effect of the dispatcher. 

Merit-order rule. At each hour, the units the environment forces online are kept, and further units are added in order of full-load average cost, 𝐶 ('𝑃 (*/𝑃 (, skipping units locked offline, until committed capacity reaches 𝑚 times net demand. The margin 𝑚 ∈ {1.0,1.05,1.1,1.2} is the rule’s only parameter. 

Static-fleet control. To bound what any adaptation contributes, the 𝑘 cheapest units are committed for all 72 hours of every instance, for 𝑘 from 8 to 51 and under both full-load and first-segment cost orderings. The lower envelope of optimality ratio against fulfilment over all fleets defines the frontier attainable without any scheduling behaviour; policies are compared with it at equal fulfilment by linear interpolation. 

Benchmark commitment. The thermal commitment of the benchmark solution is replayed through the thermal-only environment. Because the benchmark also uses hydro and storage, its thermal commitment does not meet the thermal-only demand in full. 

4.3. Imitation and fine-tuning 

For imitation, the schedules of a teacher (the merit-order rule or the MILP) on the 300 training instances are converted into state–action pairs under the agent’s own observation. For the rule, a further 300 episodes are generated in which 10% of free units are flipped at random when executed but labelled with the rule’s decision, so that the policy also sees states off the rule’s path; for the MILP this is not possible without re-solving from each perturbed state, so only its own trajectories are used. The policy network is trained by masked cross-entropy for 100 epochs, and the value network is pre-trained on the discounted returns of the teacher’s trajectories (or set to zero for the relative reward). For DQN, the branching network is first trained by masked cross-entropy and then by temporal-difference learning with a large-margin loss on the teacher’s decisions [45], and the replay buffer is seeded with the teacher’s transitions. A pre-trained policy that is not fine-tuned is termed imitation-only. The pre-trained policies are then fine-tuned online with the settings in Table 3. 11 

4.4. Training protocol 

Each PPO configuration is trained for 10 6 environment steps (about 13,900 episodes) with three seeds (42, 43 and 44), except six of the seven tuning variants of Section 4.7 (all but the zero-entropy variant), which use one seed. DQN is trained for 5 × 10 5 steps from scratch with one seed per exploration rate, and for 3 × 10 5 steps from a pre-trained network with three seeds. Checkpoints are evaluated every 25,000–100,000 steps on the 25 validation instances, and the checkpoint with the lowest median penalised optimality ratio (Section 4.5) on validation is reported on the 100 test instances. The exceptions, reported at their final checkpoint, are DQN trained from scratch and the standard fine-tuning runs B1 and B3 of Section 5.4, whose drift is the quantity of interest. Training runs on a single CPU core per run and takes about 15–20 min for 10 6 PPO steps, excluding validation evaluations. A trained policy produces a 72-hour schedule in about 0.07 s, or 0.12 s including look-ahead dispatch, against a median MILP solve time of 52 s. Results are reported as the mean and standard deviation over seeds of the per-seed statistics. The code uses Python 3.10, Stable-Baselines3 2.4.1 with sb3-contrib 2.4.0 and PyTorch 2.5.1, and HiGHS 1.15.1, and was run on a four-core Linux virtual machine (aarch64).

Penalised optimality ratio. The numerator of Eq. (3) is replaced by the full objective of Eq. (1), including unserved energy and spill priced at 𝜆 +-* and 𝜆 *. . Spill includes renewable surplus in hours of negative net demand, which no thermal schedule can avoid and which is common to all policies. 

Fulfilment and feasibility. Demand fulfilment is the share of positive net demand served over the horizon. An instance is feasible if its total unserved energy is below 1 MWh, and the feasibility rate is the share of feasible instances. 

Fleet size and responsiveness. Mean committed units 𝑁 ) is reported for every policy. Demand responsiveness 𝜌 is the within-episode Pearson correlation of 𝑁 ) with net demand, averaged over instances.

4.6. Learned policies as solver warm starts 

A policy’s commitment schedule is passed to the MILP of Eq. (1) in two ways. As a warm start, the schedule, completed by the dispatch for that commitment, is given to HiGHS as an initial solution. With variable fixing, the commitment of unit-hours with a policy commitment probability below 𝜏 ∈ {0.001,0.02,0.05} is also fixed at zero, following Fritz et al. [10]; decisions to commit are not fixed, because in a pilot on the first 20 of the 100 test instances fixing them cost 1.6% on average; the choice applies equally to every policy, so it does not favour any of them. The warm starts compared are no warm start, the merit-order rule ( 𝑚 = 1.1 ), the imitation-only policy of the MILP, the final fine-tuned PPO configuration and the default masked PPO agent trained from scratch; fixing is applied with three seeds of the imitation-only policy (two further seeds were trained for this purpose) and of the fine-tuned PPO. Two further controls test whether the gains need a learned policy at all. A heuristic fixes every unit-hour left off by the merit-order rule with a generous margin 𝑚 ∈ {1.3,1.5,2.0} , warm-started from that rule’s schedule. To separate how many variables a policy fixes from which ones it chooses, the imitation-only policy is also made to fix, on each instance, exactly as many unit-hours as the fine-tuned policy of the same seed index at 𝜏 = 0.001 and 0.02 , choosing those it is most confident should be offline. Each solve uses one thread, a 100 s limit and a 0.1% relative gap, on all 100 test instances. Quality is the cost above the best solution found for the instance by any unrestricted solve, and speed-up is the ratio to the solve time without warm start on the same instance and machine (solves were split over two machines by instance); it is a lower bound, because that solve reached the time limit on 42% of instances. 

4.7. Experiments 

Seven experiments are reported (Table 4). E1 evaluates the reference policies. E2 trains standard PPO under R0, R1 and R2 with the basic observation (spill penalty 1,000 $/MWh) and the balance-aware observation (3,000 $/MWh), and DQN from scratch with final exploration rates of 0.02, 0.05 and 0.1. E3 trains masked PPO (balance-aware, 3,000 $/MWh, R2) with the default settings, with seven variants (observation and reward normalisation, a 256 × 256 network with eight parallel environments, a lower learning rate, entropy coefficients of 0 and 0.03, a clip range of 0.1, and learning-rate decay), and with the clean reward. E4 measures the agents’ exploration noise, simulates the reward-optimal fleet under noise, compares the agents with the rule under matched noise, and fine-tunes a lean policy with injected noise. E5 fine-tunes policies that imitate the merit-order rule: PPO with standard (B1), conservative (B2) and standard settings without an entropy bonus (B3), and DQN with constant exploration. E6 fine-tunes conservatively from imitation of the rule or the MILP under progressively refined rewards and dispatch. E7 uses the policies as warm starts and variable-fixing guides for the MILP (Section 4.6).

5. Results 

This section first establishes the reference points (Section 5.1), then examines the over-commitment of agents trained from scratch and its link to exploration (Sections 5.2–5.4). Sections 5.5 and 5.6 report the imitation-based remedies and the use of the resulting policy within the MILP, and Section 5.7 the sensitivity of reported results to evaluation choices. Results are reported mainly as two gaps to the thermal-only MILP optimum on the same instances: the gap in production and start-up cost, and the penalised gap, which also prices unserved energy and spill. A policy can score well on the first by leaving demand unserved, so the two should be read together. The O.R. against the competition benchmark is given for comparability with other work; because the benchmark also uses hydro and storage, the optimum itself scores 1.090 on it. 

5.1. Reference points

A standalone thermal scheduler should therefore be judged against 1.090, not 1.0.

5.3. Exploration noise as insurance 

Why would an agent settle on a policy its reward rates poorly? During training, each unit’s decision is sampled independently, so committed units are switched off at random. Switching off a needed unit risks unserved energy priced at 12,533 $/MWh, whereas keeping a spare unit online costs its no-load and minimum-output fuel, typically $1,000–3,000 per hour. Under noise, spare capacity is insurance, and a policy that is optimal for the noisy behaviour policy over-commits when executed greedily. We tested this by injecting the same kind of noise into the merit-order rule: at every hour, each free unit’s decision was flipped with probability 𝜀 , and the rule was scored on a training reward over 50 training instances for a range of margins. Under R2, the reward-optimal fleet rises from 12.5 units without noise to 19.5 at 𝜀 = 0.1 and 26.2 at 𝜀 = 0.3 . Under the clean reward, used for the DQN agents (Fig. 3a), it is 14.7 units without noise ( 𝑚 = 1.2 ) and 15.1, 16.9, 18.0, 22.4 and 27.8 units at 𝜀 = 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2. At high noise the cost is also nearly flat over a wide range of fleet sizes: at 𝜀 = 0.1 , fleets of 19–28 units cost within about 5% (R2) to 7% (clean reward) of each other, so the reward gives little signal to shrink the fleet.

Most trained agents lie close to the curve (Fig. 3b). The masked PPO agents of Section 5.2 had a per-unit noise of 0.27–0.43 and held 24–31 units; under R2 the reward-optimal fleet is 26.2 units at ε = 0.3, the highest noise simulated. Because the cost is nearly flat at these noise levels, this agreement is a weak test on its own, which motivates the direct test below. The agents’ noise stays high because of the entropy bonus: the variant without it ended training with a noise of only 0.07. That variant does not fit the curve, however: it kept 26–27 units against a predicted 18–19. The imitation-initialised PPO agents of Section 5.4 lie near the curve at much lower noise; the DQN agents at the lowest noise lie above it (Section 5.4). 18 If over-commitment were simply a failure to optimise, the agents’ stochastic policies should also lose to the rule when both act with the agents’ training noise; they do not. On the R2 training reward over 30 training instances, the stochastic policies of all twelve masked agents cost 16–63% less than the best merit-order rule with the same average per-unit noise. Matching the noise separately for committed and uncommitted units gave the same result. The agents therefore perform well on the problem they face during training, which includes their own exploration, and poorly only when that policy is executed greedily. The comparison is approximate: the agents’ noise is concentrated on units with commitment probabilities near one half, whereas the injected noise is spread uniformly and is costlier, so it favours the agents, and the simulated curve models their noise only approximately. The account also makes a direct prediction: adding exploration noise to a lean policy should enlarge its fleet. We tested it by fine-tuning the final configuration of Section 5.5 from its imitation-only starting policy, with each free unit’s executed decision flipped with probability ε during training only, as environment noise (Fig. 4, Table D1). The greedy fleet rose from 15.0 units without injected noise to 17.0–19.1 units at ε = 0.02–0.2, and the penalised gap from 4.5% to 13–62%. Up to ε = 0.05 the fleets lay within about one unit of the reward-optimal fleet of the noisy rule. At higher noise they fell well short of it (17.8 against 22.4 units at ε = 0.1, and 19.1 against 27.8 at ε = 0.2); at ε = 0.2 the fleet was still rising steeply when training ended, which suggests that the conservative step size limits how far the policy moves, but at ε = 0.1 it had nearly levelled off. Exploration noise therefore causes the fleet to grow, by about the predicted amount at low noise, though not to the predicted optimum at high noise within this budget.

5.4. Drift towards over-commitment from a lean start 

If over-commitment is a response to exploration, a lean initial policy should drift towards it when it explores. We initialised PPO by imitation of the merit-order rule ( 𝑚 = 1.1 ) and fine-tuned it under R2 with three settings (Fig. 5a, Table 7). With the standard settings of Section 19 5.2 (B1), the fleet rose steadily from 13.8 to 22.6–24.9 units over 10 6 steps, with a final per-unit noise of 0.25–0.27. Removing the entropy bonus but keeping the larger steps (B3) slowed the drift to 15.8–18.8 units after 10 6 steps, with a noise of 0.008–0.019. With conservative settings (B2; Table 3), the fleet stayed at 13.6–14.3 units for the full 10 6 steps, with noise of about 0.005, and the policy improved slightly on its teacher (Table 7). The B1 and B2 fleets lie close to the reward-optimal fleet at their measured noise (Fig. 3b); B3 lies 1– 4 units above it. Because B2 changes the learning rate, the clip range and the entropy bonus together, it does not separate smaller steps from lower noise; B3 removes the entropy bonus alone and drifts less than B1.

The DQN experiment varied the noise directly. Initialised by imitation of the rule ( 𝑚 = 1.0 )and fine-tuned with constant per-unit noise, its executed fleet over the last third of training increased with 𝜀 : 20.5, 26.0 and 27.3 units at 𝜀 = 0.005, 0.02 and 0.1, an increase of about seven units, close to the predicted increase between the lowest and highest noise levels. The shape differs, however: most of the observed increase occurred between the two lowest levels, where the simulated curve predicts only about two units. Even the almost noise-free arm rose from 12.7 to about 19–22 units, and the greedy fleet varied between 14 and 48 units from one checkpoint to the next. DQN therefore shows two components: a noise-dependent increase consistent with the insurance account, and a noise-independent drift that places even the lowest-noise arm about five units above the predicted 15 units. The latter is consistent with relative overgeneralisation in independent learners [36] (Section 6.1). 

5.5. Imitation and conservative fine-tuning

Fixing the confident decisions of the fine-tuned policy gave a controlled trade-off: 3.8 times faster at 0.10% above the best solution, 6.6 times at 0.19% and 8.5 times at 0.25%, with a seed-to-seed standard deviation of 0.03 percentage points. The imitation-only policy fixed more variables at each threshold and was faster, but its confident decisions were less reliable: at 𝜏 = 0.001 it was 5.7 times faster at 0.23% on average, and its mean cost rose steeply at higher thresholds (Table 8). At comparable speed-ups (5.7 and 6.6 times), the worst case was 27.9% for the imitation-only policy against 4.5% for the fine-tuned policy. The imitation-only policy was trained only to reproduce the MILP’s decisions and can be confident in states its teacher never visited; fine-tuning on the cost of its own decisions is consistent with making the policy’s confidence a better guide to which units can stay offline. Two controls support the view that the learned policy matters. The merit-order heuristic was 4–13 times faster but 2.9–4.7% more expensive on average, with worst cases of 26–37%, so a good schedule alone is not enough to choose which variables to fix. With matched counts (Section 4.6), the imitation-only policy remained faster. At the lower level it was indistinguishable in two of three seeds, with one seed reaching 8.9% on an instance; at the higher level its mean cost was three times larger (0.60% against 0.19%) and its worst case was 15.7–27.9% in every seed, against 0.9–4.5% for the fine-tuned policy. The two policies therefore trade speed against reliability: the imitation-only policy’s choice of variables makes the restricted problem easier, while the fine-tuned policy’s choice makes it safer, with the difference showing mainly in the tail. The over-committing agent trained from scratch was confident about only 5% of decisions, and fixing those made the solve slower and 2.1% more expensive. 25 The speed-ups reported by Fritz et al. [10] (Section 2.1) were obtained on the full system, with a different solver and per-generator thresholds tuned on validation; the speed-ups here use a single threshold for all units, and the comparison is between policies within one pipeline. 

5.7. Sensitivity to evaluation choices 

The dispatch method used for evaluation changes the reported O.R. by amounts comparable to the differences between methods. Benchmark scope and the exclusion of unserved energy from the O.R. have similar effects, discussed in Sections 5.1 and 5.5. Proportional loading spreads load across all committed units, including expensive ones. It raised the O.R. of the MILP commitment from 1.090 under look-ahead dispatch to 1.130 (+3.6%), and that of the rules by 0.9–3.0%, so it also changes the gaps between policies. Its discrete output levels leave shortfalls even when capacity is ample (459 MWh per instance on the MILP’s own schedules), so that neither the MILP schedule nor any rule met the 1 MWh feasibility threshold on any instance. Hourly economic dispatch gives the same O.R. as look-ahead dispatch but lower feasibility (Section 3.5). Feasibility rates should therefore be reported with the dispatcher. 

6. Discussion 

This section draws the results together. Section 6.1 discusses why factorised agents over-commit, Section 6.2 the implications for learned schedulers, Section 6.3 a recommended reporting protocol, and Section 6.4 the limitations of the study. Fig. 7 summarises our results visually, placing the main policies by their mean fleet and penalised cost relative to the optimum. Agents trained from scratch form one cluster at about twice the optimal fleet and 1.5–8 times the optimal penalised cost, whatever their algorithm, reward or settings. The merit-order rules and the conservatively fine-tuned policies sit close to the optimum, the latter within 5–14%, while standard fine-tuning moves a lean policy back towards the over-committed cluster.

6.1. Why factorised agents over-commit 

The results support a specific mechanism. With a factorised action space, the policy optimised during training is a noisy one in which each unit’s decision is sampled independently, and for that noisy policy spare commitment is a good answer: it insures against random de-commitments, which the unserved-energy penalty prices heavily. Evaluated greedily, the same policy over-commits. Four results support this account: injected exploration noise enlarged a lean policy’s fleet, as exploration did for DQN; a reward without shaping terms left the over-commitment in place; under their own noise the agents outperformed the rule; and most PPO fleets lay close to the reward-optimal fleet at their measured noise. The fleet at high injected noise and the zero-entropy variant fit less well (Section 5.3). Joint or autoregressive action heads, which would remove the factorisation itself, were not tested. The account is incomplete for DQN, whose fleet also drifted upwards with almost no noise and whose greedy policy was unstable between checkpoints. Independent value branches that each average over the others’ behaviour favour actions that are safe individually over those that are jointly optimal [36], and heavy-tailed penalties make commitment such an action; we state this as a hypothesis. More generally, the effect resembles the preference of on-policy methods for safe behaviour under exploration [11], and may arise wherever many binary decisions jointly secure a constraint whose violation is expensive. 

6.2. Implications for learned schedulers 

Four practical implications follow. First, a learned scheduler should be compared with a simple rule and with an optimum for exactly the problem it solves; on this benchmark a one-parameter rule is within 1.6–7% of the optimum and sets the minimum bar a learned scheduler should clear. Second, because the gap is largely a response to exploration, remedies that reduce the need for it are effective, as imitation followed by conservative fine-tuning showed (Section 5.5). These comparisons are made under perfect foresight, where the MILP solves exactly the problem being scored; the setting therefore favours mathematical programming and gives no credit for the sequential, reactive decision-making that motivates RL. Under forecast uncertainty some spare commitment acts as reserve, so the cost of the behaviour diagnosed here may be smaller, a hypothesis that the extension to uncertain conditions in Section 7 would test. Third, the training reward and dispatch should match the evaluation objective. Fourth, an RL policy and an exact solver are complementary. As a warm start, the fine-tuned policy made the MILP result more reliable, as did the merit-order rule; as a guide for variable fixing it gave a several-fold speed-up at a cost within a few tenths of a percent, with a smaller worst case than an imitation-only policy fixing as many variables. An over-committing policy trained from scratch is of little use for fixing; for hybrid pipelines that warm-start or repair a learned proposal [9,10,21], the results suggest that the calibration of the policy’s confidence 27 matters as much as its accuracy, which makes a conservatively fine-tuned RL policy a natural partner for an exact solver. 

6.3. Recommended reporting protocol 

On the basis of these results, we recommend that evaluations of learned UC schedulers report the following: 1. the optimality ratio with its distribution across instances, and a penalised ratio that prices unserved energy and spill; 2. demand fulfilment and feasibility rate adjacent to the ratio, with the dispatch method stated; 3. the gap to an optimum solved for exactly the problem the agent faces, where the benchmark’s scope differs; 4. a simple rule, such as a merit-order rule with a capacity margin, evaluated with the same dispatch and cost pipeline; 5. mean committed fleet size, spill and unserved energy, and, for stochastic policies, the exploration noise at the reported checkpoint; 6. the agent’s performance on its own training reward relative to the rule; 7. all metrics across at least three, and preferably five or more, independently seeded training runs [24], with checkpoints selected on validation instances. Items 4–6 can be computed retrospectively for any study that saved its trained policies. 

6.4. Limitations 

Several limitations bound these conclusions. 1. Deterministic setting. All results assume perfect foresight of demand, wind and solar, so every policy is compared with a MILP that solves exactly the problem being scored. The study therefore does not measure how RL policies, or the over-commitment diagnosed here, perform under forecast uncertainty, where that optimum is not attainable and some spare commitment has value as reserve (Section 6.2). 2. Optimum. The model does not enforce the benchmark’s shutdown limits, which would bind for 12 of the 51 units; enforcing them changes the optimum by less than 0.3% on average, but individual schedules would need adjusting to honour them. 3. Warm-start study. Solves used the open-source HiGHS solver on one thread, the thermal-only problem and a common threshold for all generators; speed-ups are lower bounds because the reference solve often reached its time limit, and the runs were split between two machines (each instance on one machine). 

7. Conclusion 

This first study of RL on the 51-unit EPRI benchmark found that PPO and DQN trained from scratch committed about twice as many units as a thermal-only MILP optimum. With factorised actions, spare units insure the agents against their own exploration: injecting exploration noise enlarged a lean policy’s fleet, and removing the reward’s shaping terms did not remove the over-commitment. Removing the need for that exploration closed most of the 28 gap: imitation of the MILP solutions followed by conservative fine-tuning, with a reward equal to the MILP objective and look-ahead dispatch, came within 2.6% of the optimum and gave the lowest penalised cost of any policy evaluated. The same policy made an exact solver 3.8–8.5 times faster at a cost within 0.10–0.25% of the best solution, which suggests that RL policies for UC are most useful alongside, rather than instead of, mathematical programming. Future work should address the remaining gap with interactive imitation, which relabels the states the policy visits with the expert’s decisions [44], test joint or autoregressive action models, tune fixing thresholds per generator, and extend the analysis to the full system with storage, network constraints and forecast uncertainty.
