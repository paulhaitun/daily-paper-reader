Abstract 

We study makespan minimization on unrelated machines with the additional goal of constructing diverse schedules: Instead of outputting a single schedule, we seek a collection of schedules with provable makespan guarantees and substantially different job assignments. This setting is motivated by practical applications in which committing to a single schedule in advance may be undesirable. Processing times may be uncertain, and additional constraints or domain knowledge may become available only later, so providing a set of k schedules of similar quality gives the scheduler meaningful alternatives. We measure pairwise diversity by the weighted Hamming distance between job assignments and study the two standard objectives in the diverse-solutions literature: max-sum diversity, which maximizes the total pairwise distance between schedules, and max-min diversity, which maximizes the smallest pairwise distance. For the max-sum objective, we show that optimal diversity can be achieved in polynomial time without sacrificing the classic makespan approximation factor. For every feasible makespan target T , we can compute k schedules of makespan at most 2 T whose max-sum diversity is optimal among all k schedules of makespan at most T . Therefore, we match the classic factor-2 makespan guarantee of Lenstra, Shmoys, and Tardos ( Math. Prog. , 1990) and preserve optimal diversity. For the max-min objective, we show that the diversity problem is inherently harder. Namely, we show that, even without makespan constraints, deciding whether three schedules of given pairwise diversity exist is NP -hard. We complete this with two complementary bicriteria approximation algorithms for every fixed k and ε ∈ (0 , 1). The first preserves almost all of the optimal max-min diversity with makespan at most (3 ⌈12 /ε ⌉ + 1) T . The second keeps the makespan arbitrarily close to the classic 2 guarantee while achieving a 12 (1 − ε)fraction of the optimal max-min diversity.

Makespan minimization on unrelated machines is one of the most classic problems in scheduling. Given a set M of m machines and a set J of n jobs, where each job j ∈ J has a machine-dependent processing time pij on machine i ∈ M , one seeks an assignment, or schedule, σ : J → M that minimizes the makespan : for a schedule σ, the load of machine i is Li(σ) = P  

> j∈J:σ(j)= i

pij , and the makespan is Cmax (σ) = max i∈M Li(σ). The problem is denoted by R∥Cmax [13] and is well-known to be NP -hard. This hardness motivated the design of approximation algorithms, which compute in polynomial time a schedule σ whose makespan is within a factor α of the optimum. One of the most classic approximation algorithms in scheduling is the factor-2 approximation for R∥Cmax due to [23]. Moreover, the same work shows that the problem is hard to approximate within any factor strictly smaller than 32 , unless P = NP [23]. Since its conception in 1990, no approximation factor strictly below 2 has been obtained for general unrelated machines. Our main research question is whether one can retain such a guarantee when the output is required to contain not just one schedule, but several substantially different schedules. Our motivation arises from settings in which committing to a single schedule in advance is undesirable. Processing times may be known only approximately, while additional constraints or application-specific information may become available only after candidate schedules have been computed. We therefore seek to preserve flexibility at the optimization stage by producing multiple schedules that are substantially different from one another. Such diversity enables a decision maker to incorporate information that was unavailable or unmodeled during optimization when selecting a final schedule. In this sense, diverse solution sets provide a natural mechanism for increasing robustness to uncertainty and unmodeled constraints. This point of view is consistent with the broader diverse-solutions paradigm in combinatorial optimization. A growing body of work studies how to compute multiple solutions that are not only good with respect to the original objective, but also far apart according to an appropriate distance measure [2, 9, 15]. A similar perspective also arises in patient admission scheduling, where evolutionary diversity optimization has been used to compute diverse sets of high-quality schedules that provide alternatives under imperfect modeling [24]. In this paper, we measure diversity at the level of job assignments: two schedules are far apart when many important jobs are assigned to different machines. For example, a decision maker may later learn that certain job–machine assignments are desirable or undesirable and then choose the candidate schedule that best matches these preferences. Optimizing this diversity creates a specific difficulty for unrelated-machine scheduling. Each schedule must satisfy its own load constraints, but the diversity objective couples all schedules through job assignments. A change in one schedule may improve its distance from some schedules, decrease its distance from others, and at the same time violate a machine-load constraint. Thus, diversity has to be built into the scheduling problem itself. We study the following bicriteria problem. Given a makespan target T and an integer k, the goal is to compute k schedules with makespan close to T and large job-assignment diversity. Our results separate the two standard diversity objectives: max-sum diversity maximizes the total pairwise distance in the collection, while max-min diversity maximizes the smallest pairwise distance. For max-sum diversity, we retain the classic factor-2 makespan guarantee and achieve the optimal diversity among k schedules of makespan at most T . For max-min diversity, we show that the problem is computationally harder and design two complementary bicriteria algorithms. 2m1m2σ1σ2σ3Figure 1: Three schedules σ1, σ2, and σ3 of seven jobs on two machines m1 and m2. The max-sum objective measures the total spread of the collection of schedules, and an optimal solution might include identical schedules or consist of a few well-separated clusters of nearly identical schedules. To counter such behavior, the max-min objective is stricter and requires every pair of schedules to be far apart. An example for both objectives is depicted in Figure 1. Diverse scheduling has two competing objectives, makespan and diversity, and our results approximate their Pareto tradeoff. We state the guarantees for a makespan target T , but they also admit the dual view of fixing a diversity target and minimizing the required makespan. Given a target makespan T , all our guarantees compare the diversity of the output schedules with the best diversity attainable by k schedules of makespan at most the original target T ,even though the output schedules may have larger makespan (see the discussion at the end of Section 2). Max-sum diversity. Our first result is proved in Section 4. We begin in Section 4.1 by introducing a natural labeled k-tuple LP formulation, in which each of the k schedules has a label and each variable assigns a job to a k-tuple of machines. This formulation can be viewed as a special case of the k-sided placement problem of [19], and already gives optimal max-sum diversity with makespan ( k +1) T in polynomial time for fixed k. Our main result significantly improves the makespan guarantee and matches the best-known factor-2 approximation for R∥Cmax [23], while preserving exact max-sum diversity and running in time polynomial in the input size and k. To do this, we rewrite the max-sum objective in terms of multiplicities. Occurrence-count arguments for weighted Hamming distance have already appeared in the diverse-solutions literature [15]. In our work, however, multiplicities count job–machine assignments with unrelated processing 3times, so they must satisfy load constraints and be decomposed into actual schedules. We formulate a multiplicity LP in Section 4.3. It tracks how many schedules assign each job to each machine, uses ranked copies to linearize the same-machine penalty, and keeps aggregate load constraints over the k schedules. We round this LP by iterative relaxation [22] in Section 4.4, obtaining integral multiplicities with a small aggregate-load violation. The remaining challenge is that this formulation controls only the aggregate load over all k schedules, and an arbitrary realization of the rounded multiplicities could concentrate several large jobs in the same schedule. In Section 4.5, we address this by decomposing the rounded multiplicities into labeled schedules, adapting the slot construction by Shmoys and Tardos [27] and combining it with bipartite edge coloring. The resulting decomposition, together with an averaging argument that exploits the multiplicity structure, yields the per-schedule makespan guarantee 2 T .

Theorem 4 (Informal). One can obtain k schedules of makespan at most 2T with optimal max-sum diversity, in time polynomial in the input size and in k.

Max-min diversity. We next turn to max-min diversity. Unlike max-sum diversity, this objective must guarantee diversity for every pair of schedules. Thus, the multiplicity view is no longer enough: we need to know which pairs of schedules differ on each job. This obstruction is not only an artifact of our approach. Lemma 2 in Appendix C.5 shows that, given a diversity value Q, it is NP -hard to decide whether there exist three schedules with max-min diversity at least Q, even when the makespan constraint is ignored. For fixed k, we therefore give two complementary algorithms, one that preserves almost all of the optimum diversity, and one that keeps the makespan close to the factor-2 guarantee for R∥Cmax .

Near-optimal max-min diversity. In Section 5.1, we give our first algorithm for max-min diversity. It keeps the labeled k-tuple LP and aims for near-optimal diversity. Assume that the diversity target is known through guessing. For every pair of schedules, we guess the largest positive contributors to their pairwise distance (called heavy contributors ), together with their full k-tuple assignments. After guessing the diversity target and heavy contributors for each pair of schedules, the algorithm solves a residual k-tuple LP and iteratively rounds it. For each remaining job, we restrict the allowed tuples to use at most O(1 /ε ) distinct machines. The main technical step is to prove that the residual LP remains feasible despite this restriction. The proof proceeds by contradiction, considering an LP point (a fractional assignment of jobs to k-tuple of machines) that minimizes the sum of squared diversity shortfalls subject to the job and load constraints. Here, a pair’s shortfall is the amount by which its diversity falls below its target, or zero if the target is met. If some diversity target is unmet, we construct a point y satisfying the job and load constraints. The crucial idea is that, to construct y, the simultaneous pairwise diversity requirements can be reduced to a single weighted diversity target, using pairwise shortfalls as weights. Meeting this weighted target ensures that moving slightly toward y decreases the sum of squared shortfalls, contradicting minimality. We then round the residual LP by iterative relaxation. 

Theorem 8 (Informal). For fixed k ≥ 2 and ε ∈ (0 , 1) , one can obtain k schedules of makespan at most (3 ⌈12 /ε ⌉ + 1) T with (1 − ε)-approximate max-min diversity, in polynomial time.

1.2 Related work 

There is a long history of works that study combinatorial optimization problems with the goal of finding multiple “diverse” solutions: For example, Krarup [20] introduced the Peripatetic Salesman problem, where multiple edge-disjoint Hamilton cycles in a graph need to be found that have minimum weight in sum. Erkut et al. [8] survey problems where facility locations need to be computed that are maximally far apart. Kuo et al. [21] consider the Maximum Diversity problem, where a number of elements from a given set need to be selected such that the sum of their pairwise diversity coefficients is maximized, and show it to be NP -hard. The computational intractability that is common for these diversity problems (see, e.g., by Hanaka et al. [15]) has motivated approaches via parameterized algorithms as well as approaches via approximation algorithms. With a focus on the former approaches, Fellows and Rosamond [9] recently introduced the 

Diverse X paradigm. Here, X can be any algorithmic problem and the proposal is to study “diverse” versions of X, possibly with varying diversity measures, with the number of desired solutions as a parameter. To give only a few examples, results of the study of parameterized algorithms for diverse problems encompass a framework for low-treewidth graphs [2], a framework using odd sieving [7], and an algorithmic meta-theorem using cliquewidth [5]. As Fellows and Rosamond [9] also point out, in applied settings, optimality is not so essential. In line with this motivation, there is a growing body of literature on approximately solving diverse problems, including multiple frameworks [1, 10, 11, 15], and on selecting approximately diverse subsets of a given set [3]. While these have yielded approximation algorithms for diverse versions of, for example, Shortest Path [11], Interval Scheduling [15], Knapsack [10], and 

Interval Vertex Deletion [1], the diverse version of makespan minimization on unrelated machines has not been studied. To our knowledge, prior work on diverse scheduling problems thus far covers variants of Diverse Interval Scheduling [15], including Diverse Job 

5Selection [14], and heuristics for hybrid flow-shop scheduling [12]. In Interval Scheduling ,a maximum-cardinality subset of non-overlapping intervals needs to be selected from a given set of intervals, and so the problem differs substantially from makespan minimization on unrelated machines. G¨ oren and Pierreval [12] propose a heuristic framework for diverse scheduling and evaluate it on hybrid flow-shop makespan instances, without worst-case guarantees. Our algorithms for unrelated machines provide approximation guarantees for both makespan and diversity.

# 3 Hardness 

Since makespan minimization is NP -hard even when producing a single schedule, it is not surprising that hardness persists when producing multiple diverse schedules. However, we prove that producing multiple diverse schedules is hard even in settings where the makespan target is essentially irrelevant. In Section 3.1, we show that computing two schedules of makespan at most T with optimal diversity at target T is NP -hard even when finding non-diverse schedules of makespan at most T

6is trivial. The same hardness applies to both max-sum and max-min diversity, since for k = 2 the two objectives coincide. In Section 3.2, we show that computing three schedules of optimal max-min diversity is 

NP -hard even in the absence of a makespan target. Further, we observe that the related problem of selecting k maximally diverse schedules from a given set of schedules is W[1]-hard with respect to k, for both max-sum and max-min diversity. 

3.1 Hardness at a fixed makespan target 

We give a simple reduction showing that optimizing diversity at a fixed makespan target is 

NP -hard. Our hardness result holds even when all diversity weights are equal to 1 and every job is eligible on only two machines. Each job has the same processing time on both eligible machines, and so we establish hardness even in the restricted-assignment machine model (the graph-balancing machine model [6] on stars, in fact). Moreover, finding two distinct feasible schedules of makespan at most the target is trivial for the instances produced by the reduction. Thus, the hardness comes from the diversity objective. 

Lemma 1. Given a makespan target T and a diversity value Q, it is NP -hard to decide whether there exist two schedules σ1, σ 2 such that Cmax (σ1), C max (σ2) ≤ T and d(σ1, σ 2) ≥ Q. The same hardness applies to both max-sum and max-min diversity, since for k = 2 the two objectives coincide. 

The reduction is from Partition and establishes weak NP -hardness; see Appendix A for the proof. 

3.2 Further hardness results 

We give an additional hardness result specifically for max-min diversity. In particular, for this objective, producing three maximally diverse schedules is NP -hard even without a makespan constraint. 

Lemma 2. Given a diversity value Q, it is NP -hard to decide whether there exist three schedules 

σ1, σ 2, σ 3 such that dmin (σ1, σ 2, σ 3) ≥ Q, even if the makespan constraint is ignored. 

The reduction is again from Partition and establishes weak NP -hardness. We refer to Appendix C.5 for the details. Note that this result separates max-min diversity from max-sum diversity in terms of hardness. This is because max-sum diversity for three schedules without makespan constraints can be formulated as a linear objective subject to assignment constraints and thus solved in polynomial time. Also, even when a set of schedules is already given , deciding whether a sufficiently diverse fixed-size subset exists is hard for both max-sum and max-min diversity: With an observation similar to that of Hanaka et al. [15, Observation 2], the problem of selecting a size-k subset of maximum diversity from a given set of schedules can be shown to be W[1]-hard with respect to k by a reduction from Set Packing , even with unit jobs on two machines. Intuitively, the input set of schedules can omit easy solutions, making the problem hard. 

# 4 Max-sum diversity 

In this section, we present our bicriteria approximation algorithm for max-sum diversity. We begin in Section 4.1 with the natural labeled k-tuple formulation, which keeps track of the machine chosen by each job in each of the k schedules and yields a ( k + 1)-makespan guarantee with 7optimal diversity. Our main result, in Section 4.2, shows that this labeled view is unnecessarily detailed: the max-sum diversity objective depends only on the multiplicities with which each job is assigned to each machine across the k schedules, not on which schedule makes each assignment. We exploit this observation through a multiplicity LP, introduced in Section 4.3, which decides only how many of the schedules assign each job to each machine. The LP uses ranked copies to linearize the convex same-machine penalty and keeps only aggregate-load constraints summed over all schedules. In Section 4.4, we round this LP by iterative rounding, obtaining integral multiplicities with a small aggregate-load violation. Finally, in Section 4.5, we decompose the multiplicities into k schedules using a slot construction, bipartite edge coloring, and an averaging argument to bound the load of each output schedule. 4.5 Decomposing multiplicities into schedules 

We now turn the integer multiplicities μij into k schedules. We build a bipartite multigraph between jobs and so-called “machine slots”, properly edge-color it to obtain the schedules, and use an averaging argument over slots to bound the makespan of each schedule. For every eligible pair ( i, j ) with μij > 0, we create μij copies of the pair ( i, j ), each of processing time pij . For each machine i, we sort all copies involving i in nonincreasing order of processing time and cut this list into consecutive slots of k copies each, with the last slot possibly smaller. The ordering is inspired by the slot construction in the generalized-assignment rounding of Shmoys and Tardos [27]. In our setting, the slots are used to decompose integral multiplicities into k schedules via edge coloring, and the resulting load bound exploits this additional structure. We construct a bipartite multigraph H with a vertex for each job on the left and a vertex for each machine slot on the right, adding one edge between job j and a slot of machine i for every copy of ( i, j ) in that slot. Each job j has degree P 

> i∈Γ( j)

μij = k, and each machine slot has degree at most k, as a slot holds at most k copies, so H has maximum degree k.By K˝ onig’s edge-coloring theorem, H admits a proper k-edge-coloring [18, 26] computable in polynomial time. Each color defines one schedule. If an edge of color a ∈ [k] is incident to job j and a slot of machine i, then we set σa(j) = i. Since the coloring is proper and every job has degree exactly k, each job has exactly one edge of each color, so every σa schedules all jobs. Moreover, for every pair ( i, j ), there are exactly μij edges from job j to slots of machine i, and hence exactly μij colors assign j to i. Thus, job j is scheduled on machine i in exactly μij of the schedules. Therefore, σ1, . . . , σ k realize the multiplicities μ. The diversity contribution of each job in the max-sum objective from the preceding section depends only on how often it is assigned to each machine across the k schedules, not on the labels of the schedules. Hence, we optimized aggregate multiplicities and recovered the labeled schedules only at the end. In contrast, for max-min diversity we must guarantee diversity for every pair of schedules. Thus, we must know which pairs of schedules differ on each job. The max-min objective is computationally harder than the max-sum objective because, given a diversity target Q, it is already NP -hard to decide whether there exist three schedules with 

dmin (σ1, σ 2, σ 3) ≥ Q, even when dropping the makespan constraints (Lemma 2, see Appendix C.5). Therefore, in this section, we give two complementary bicriteria approximation algorithms for max-min diversity. First, in Section 5.1, we achieve near-optimal diversity. For fixed k and ε > 0, our labeled 

k-tuple algorithm achieves a (1 − ε)-approximation to the optimum max-min diversity at the original target T , while increasing the makespan of the computed schedules to at most (3 ⌈12 /ε ⌉ + 1) T . After guessing the diversity target and heavy contributors to the diversity for each pair of schedules, the algorithm solves a residual k-tuple LP and iteratively rounds it. For each remaining job, we restrict the allowed tuples to use at most O(1 /ε ) distinct machines, and count the processing time of the job once on each machine used by its tuple. The main technical step is to prove that the residual LP remains feasible despite this restriction. Second, in Section 5.2, we achieve the classic factor-2 makespan guarantee approximately. The main idea is to build the schedules sequentially. Given the schedules already constructed, we view the construction of the next schedule as an unrelated-machine assignment problem with covering constraints, one per previously constructed schedule, each requiring the new schedule to be sufficiently diverse from it. For every fixed k ∈ N and ε > 0, we round this assignment problem and combine it with a metric argument to obtain schedules of makespan at most (2+ ε)T

and a 12 (1 − ε)-approximation to the optimum max-min diversity.

# 6 Future work 

In this work, we studied bicriteria approximation algorithms for scheduling on unrelated machines under the max-sum and max-min diversity objectives. The main open question that remains is whether max-min diversity admits a polynomial-time algorithm with constant approximation guarantees for both makespan and diversity when k is part of the input. Further, future work can go beyond the standard notions of max-sum and max-min diversity. For example, schedules could instead be compared using coverage diversity , absolute-difference diversity , or 

Venn diversity [4, 5]. Although unrelated machines generalize models such as restricted assignment, related ma-chines, and identical machines, from a practical perspective, other notions of diversity may be of interest especially for identical machines. In this special case, our notion of diversity can consider two schedules maximally diverse even when only the labels of machines differ but the two schedules partition the jobs in the same way. Thus, it is of interest to study notions of diversity that are invariant under machine relabeling.
