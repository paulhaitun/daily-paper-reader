Abstract: Multimodal reasoning provides robotic foundation models with predictive context needed for reliable action generation. However, language-based reasoning captures task semantics but struggles to express fine-grained physical dynamics, while latent reasoning models future state evolution but offers limited structure for organizing these dynamics into reusable reasoning primitives. To bridge this gap, we introduce PhysWord , a reasoning-before-acting foundation model built on Physical Words , which use atomic action verbs as shared semantic anchors for continuous representations of physical dynamics. Specifically, PhysWord predicts upcoming atomic action words and directly aligns their contextual hidden states with latent targets derived from the corresponding future observations. Building on these primitives, we construct a temporally structured PhysWord space with visual and optional tactile variants of each action word. Autoregressively predicting Physical Words across future timesteps captures transitions between execution stages, providing structured multimodal context for action generation. PhysWord achieves state-of-the-art success rates of 90.3% on visuotactile UniVTAC and 99.6% on vision-based LIBERO-Long, and outperforms competing methods on real-world tasks using bimanual grippers and dexterous hands. Further evaluations demonstrate that our structured latent reasoning framework generalizes to unseen scenarios and enables long-horizon manipulation. 

# 1. Introduction 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have emerged as a promising foundation for general-purpose robotic manipula-tion [1, 2], integrating the semantic knowledge of pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) [3–5] with robot action learning. Reliable manipulation, however, requires anticipating how the physical environment evolves as the robot acts.

This need has motivated reasoning-before-acting VLA approaches [6, 7], which generate intermediate task plans or predictive context to guide action generation. World Action Models (WAMs) further integrate future world prediction with action generation, enabling policies to leverage dynamics knowledge learned from video and manipulation data. These advances highlight the value of multimodal reasoning in providing predictive context that aligns robot actions with evolving environments. Existing reasoning approaches differ in how they represent predictive context. On the one hand, some VLA models adopt the reasoning paradigm of pretrained VLMs, using language to decompose tasks and express task-level action semantics [8–10]. However, linguistic descriptions remain physically coarse, as a verb such as “rotate” identifies an operation without fully conveying its fine-grained dynamics. On the other hand, WAMs [11, 12] and latent reasoning methods [7, 13, 14] model future state evolution, providing rich predictive representations of interaction dynamics. Yet these approaches do not necessarily organize their representations around shared action semantics. Since the same manipulation skill can be applied across different objects and scenes, a useful reasoning representation should preserve shared semantics while capturing context-specific physical dynamics. This raises a central question: Can atomic action semantics organize physical latent representations into reusable reasoning primitives? To this end, we introduce PhysWord, a reasoning-before-acting foundation model built on Physical Words, which use atomic action verbs as semantic anchors for continuous latent dynamics. We assemble a vocabulary of atomic action verbs, such as “pick,” “rotate,” and “push,” based on the action taxonomy of prior work [15, 16]. As shown in Figure 1(a), PhysWord predicts upcoming atomic action words from the current observation context and directly aligns their contextual hidden states with latent targets derived from the corresponding future observations. Joint semantic and latent supervision encourages these representations to capture shared action semantics while retaining action-related physical dynamics. To capture how these dynamics evolve during execution, we organize Physical Words at multiple future timesteps within the action-chunk horizon, forming a temporally structured PhysWord space. Within this space, we use separate visual and tactile variants of each action word to capture complementary motion and contact dynamics, including tactile variants only for tactile-enabled tasks. By autoregressively predicting these Physical Words, PhysWord captures changes in action semantics and physical dynamics across execution stages, providing structured multimodal predictive context for long-horizon manipulation. This token interface adapts to the available sensory modalities, bringing demonstrations with and without tactile observations into a unified training formulation. This flexibility enables us to jointly pretrain PhysWord on approximately 7,000 hours of real-robot and human-hand demonstrations. Across this corpus, we use a VLM [17] to automatically segment trajectories into atomic action stages and align the resulting atomic verbs with the corresponding observations. As shown in Figure 1(b), PhysWord achieves success rates of 90.3% on visuotactile UniVTAC and 99.6% on vision-only LIBERO-Long, outperforming prior SOTA methods. Real-world experiments on bimanual gripper and dexterous-hand platforms with visual and tactile sensing further demonstrate its effectiveness in contact-rich, precise, and long-horizon manipulation. To assess reusability, we evaluate both unseen scene configurations and extended task sequences absent from training. PhysWord outperforms competing methods under unseen backgrounds, object instances, and object positions on Powered Screw Driving and Pick A4 Paper , and successfully executes extended manipulation sequences, supporting reuse across both scenarios and task stages. To assess the predictive value of its tactile representations, we replace measured tactile inputs with predicted latents at inference. Remarkably, the resulting policies retain strong performance with only modest degradation, suggesting that semantic structuring preserves contact information needed for control and enables deployment without online tactile sensing. Our contributions: 

• We introduce PhysWord, a reasoning-before-acting foundation model built on Physical Words, which use atomic action verbs as semantic anchors for continuous physical dynamics by directly supervising their contextual hidden states with future latent targets. • We construct a temporally structured PhysWord space with visual and tactile variants of atomic action words, capturing evolving motion and contact dynamics for action generation. • PhysWord achieves 90.3% on UniVTAC and 99.6% on LIBERO-Long, outperforming prior SOTA methods. Experi-ments on real-world tasks demonstrate superior performance, stronger generalization, and long-horizon manipulation through sequential reuse of Physical Words. 

# 2. Related Work 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models. VLA models [1, 2, 18–21] adapt pretrained VLMs to generate robot actions from visual observations and language instructions. Reasoning-before-acting VLAs introduce intermediate reasoning

to anticipate task progression. Language-based approaches, including subtask prediction, embodied chain-of-thought (CoT), and motion descriptions [8–10, 22], convey task semantics and subtask plans but struggle to express fine-grained dynamics. Beyond language, recent methods predict future visual observations [23–28] or incorporate anticipated signals from multiple modalities [29–31]. 

Latent Reasoning and World Action Models (WAMs). Latent CoT represents intermediate reasoning through continuous latent states in LLMs and VLMs [32–35]. ATLAS [36] further highlights the importance of LLMs’ native language-based CoT for general reasoning tasks. In robotics, recent VLA models adopt latent reasoning to guide control using compact representations of future physical dynamics [6, 37, 38]. Subsequent work incorporates reinforcement learning [39] and latent alignment between human and robot demonstrations [40]. Related WAMs jointly model video and action sequences [11, 12, 41, 42]. Several approaches condition action generation on latent representations of predicted future states [43–47]. Although these approaches capture rich future dynamics, their latent representations are not structured around shared action semantics. PhysWord introduces this structure by anchoring future dynamics to atomic action verbs, forming reusable reasoning primitives that provide predictive context for action generation. 

# 3. Methodology 

In this section, we present PhysWord for structured latent reasoning in robotic manipulation, including its architecture (Section 3.2), Physical Word formulation (Section 3.3), temporally structured PhysWord space (Section 3.4), and training objectives (Section 3.5). 

3.1. Preliminaries Problem Formulation. We consider language-conditioned robotic manipulation with visual observations and optional tactile sensing. At time t, the robot receives a language instruction ℓ, multi-view visual observations Vt , and, when available, tactile observations Tt . We denote the current observations and their temporal history by Ot . Conditioned on this context, the policy predicts an action chunk: At = ( at+1, . . . , at+H ) ∼ πθ (· | Ot , ℓ ), where H denotes the action prediction horizon and at is an embodiment-specific control vector. For dual-arm gripper platforms, each action is 14-DOF, comprising a 3-DOF relative translation, a 3-DOF Euler-angle rotation, and a binary gripper command for each arm. For dexterous-hand platforms, each action is 56-DOF, comprising 12 end-effector pose components across the two arms and 44 joint commands across the two Sharpa hands. 

3.2. PhysWord Model Architecture 

PhysWord adopts a mixture-of-transformers (MoT) architecture with a temporal perception branch initialized from Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B [48] and a reasoning-and-action branch initialized from Qwen3-VL-2B [4]. As shown in Figure 2 (a), the temporal branch encodes visual history and anticipated scene evolution, while the reasoning-and-action branch generates Physical Words followed by robot actions. 

Multimodal inputs. The temporal perception branch receives five head-view observations from t − 4 to t, each at a resolution of 256 × 256 , and predicts the next H frames. The reasoning-and-action branch processes the remaining camera views and the language instruction ℓ, retaining adaptive-resolution image processing. Both visual encoders are frozen, while all other components remain trainable. For tactile-enabled tasks, we use the T3 tactile encoder [49], initialized from FTP-1 pretraining, to extract a 512-dimensional feature from each 3D visuotactile observation. We aggregate these features according to the end-effector configuration. For each two-finger gripper, we concatenate its two sensor features into a 1024-dimensional vector per timestep, then concatenate the vectors at t and t − 1 to form a 2048-dimensional tactile token. For dexterous hands, we concatenate five fingertip features into a 2560-dimensional vector and apply channel-wise pooling to obtain the 2048-dimensional token. Both configurations yield one tactile token per end-effector, matching the LLM’s word embedding dimension and facilitating joint pretraining across grippers and dexterous hands. Bimanual tasks use two tactile input tokens. Tactile features and language instructions condition the temporal perception branch through Cosmos’s cross-attention and the reasoning-and-action branch through input tokens in the LLM’s embedding space. 

Shared attention and action generation. The two branches retain independent QKV projections and FFNs, along with their original pretrained positional encodings. After projection, their query, key, and value features are concatenated along the token dimension for shared attention. At inference, Cosmos performs a single denoising step with full attention to obtain predictive features, whose keys and values are cached for subsequent token generation. As shown in Figure 2,

3.3. Physical Words 

A Physical Word is a reasoning primitive that couples an atomic action word label with a contextual hidden state representing future visual or tactile dynamics. 

Atomic action vocabulary. Large-scale language pretraining provides LLMs with rich semantic knowledge, while native next-token prediction offers a natural interface for expressing intermediate reasoning [36]. In robotic manipulation, continuous latents compactly encode multimodal context, but representations of the same action across scenarios may also capture incidental visual variations. Therefore, we introduce Physical Words to organize latent reasoning around atomic action semantics, preserving shared skill semantics while capturing context-dependent details. Based on prior action taxonomies [15, 16, 50], we define 16 action labels as semantic anchors for continuous latent representations. Additional action words can be incorporated by initializing their Physical Word variants from the corresponding pretrained LLM embeddings. 

Atomic Action Vocabulary 

Each label has a visual variant ( _v ) and a tactile variant ( _t ), e.g., pick_v and pick_t .

Movement: Approach, Move. Manipulation: Pick, Place, Flip, Rotate, Toggle, Open, Close, Push, Pull, Insert, Press, Click, Strike, Handover. 

From action words to latent representations. Each Physical Word associates an action-word label w with a contextual hidden state z from which the label is predicted. Let e(w) denote the trainable vocabulary embedding of w

and g the corresponding future sensory latent target used to supervise z. To capture future physical dynamics, we predict both visual dynamics and tactile contact states. Since visual and tactile targets occupy distinct latent spaces and may

impose conflicting supervision on a single hidden state, we instantiate separate visual and tactile variants of each action word. Both are initialized from the same pretrained embedding [4] while maintaining independent trainable vocabulary entries. This preserves a common semantic initialization while allowing modality-specific hidden states to represent future dynamics. For vision-only tasks, we omit tactile inputs and generate only visual Physical Words, such as pick_v .As shown in Figure 2 (b), the word prediction head maps z to vocabulary logits, supervised by cross-entropy ( LCE )against w. The same z is directly aligned with g using cosine distance ( Llatent ). This joint supervision couples action semantics with future physical dynamics within a shared contextual representation. PhysWord is trained with blockwise teacher forcing and generates Physical Words autoregressively over future timestep blocks at inference. Compared with latent supervision alone, Physical Words yield lower intra-class distances among latent representations of the same action, and more focused attention on action-relevant regions, as shown in Section 4.2 and Appendix D. Appendix A.3 details our automatic atomic-action annotation procedure. 

Visual and tactile latent targets. Given action-word labels paired with future sensory observations, we construct visual latent targets following prior latent VLA methods [7]. At each target timestep, we encode the future head-view image using Qwen3-VL’s visual encoder and pool its final-layer spatial tokens into a single latent target for the corresponding visual Physical Words. All tactile observations are derived from visuotactile signals. We construct future tactile targets separately for each end-effector using the same T3 encoder and feature aggregation as the current tactile inputs. These targets supervise tactile Physical Words to anticipate future contact information. Future observations serve only as training targets and are not used at inference. 

3.4. Temporally Structured PhysWord Space 

Manipulation unfolds continuously as action semantics and contact conditions evolve. A single future Physical Word may miss intermediate transitions within an action chunk, such as approaching an object before picking it up. We therefore organize Physical Words over multiple future timesteps to provide action generation with temporally structured predictive context. 

Temporal organization. To reduce the inference latency of dense Physical Word prediction, we predict Physical Words every s steps over the horizon H, yielding K = H/s future prediction times: t + s,t + 2s, . . . , t + H, where t is the current timestep. 3.5. Training Objectives 

We train PhysWord in two stages. In Stage 1, we pretrain the model on approximately 7,000 hours of human-hand and robot demonstrations with and without tactile observations. Details of the pretraining data and configuration are provided in Appendix A. In Stage 2, we fine-tune the PhysWord on task-specific demonstrations for downstream manipulation. For each stage, we jointly optimize four objectives: the Cosmos flow-matching loss Lvideo , Physical Word cross-entropy loss LCE , future latent prediction loss Llatent , and action loss Lact . # 4. Experiments 

We compare PhysWord with baselines on two simulation benchmarks (Section 4.1), assess component contributions through ablations (Section 4.2), and evaluate real-world performance and Physical Word reuse across unseen configura-tions and long-horizon tasks (Section 4.3). 

4.1. Simulation Experiments Benchmarks and Protocol. We evaluate PhysWord on two benchmarks: UniVTAC for contact-rich manipulation with visuotactile sensing and LIBERO-Long for long-horizon manipulation. UniVTAC [51] comprises eight tasks spanning lifting, classification, and insertion. LIBERO-Long [52] comprises ten long-horizon tasks. For both benchmarks, we use 50 training demonstrations per task and follow the official success criteria. We evaluate PhysWord over three runs of 100 rollouts per task and compute each task’s standard deviation across runs. 

Baselines and Implementation details. On UniVTAC, we compare PhysWord with two categories of baselines: (1) vision-based models , including π0.5 [8], StarVLA-α [53], and InternVLA-A1 [54], and (2) tactile-enabled policies ,including VITaL [55], ACT Tac with the UniVTAC Encoder, FTP-1 [56], N0-VTLA [57], T-Rex [58], and TacDyn-WAM [46]. On LIBERO-Long, we compare PhysWord with two categories of baselines: (1) VLA models , including OpenVLA [1], π0.5, OpenVLA-OFT [59], GR00T-N1.7 [20], InternVLA-A1, and LaST 0 [7], and (2) WAMs , including Motus [13], Fast-WAM [44], and UniWAM [47]. Across both benchmarks, we report official baseline results or reproduce them through full fine-tuning following each method’s official setup. On UniVTAC, we train a separate policy for each task, following FTP-1. On LIBERO-Long, we jointly train a single policy on all tasks. Detailed implementation settings for PhysWord and the baselines are provided in Appendix F. 

Results on UniVTAC. PhysWord achieves a mean success rate of 90.3% (average per-task standard deviation (SD): 1.5 percentage points), surpassing the strongest tactile baseline, N0-VTLA, by 7.2 percentage points and the strongest vision-based VLA baseline, InternVLA-A1, by 23.2 percentage points. Gains are particularly pronounced on Put Bottle in Shelf and Lift Can , suggesting benefits for sustained motion coordination and contact-sensitive manipulation. We further evaluate PhysWord-feedback , which replaces measured tactile inputs with predicted tactile latents. It achieves 89% mean success, only 1.3 percentage points below the full model, suggesting that the predicted tactile latents faithfully capture contact information needed for control. These results indicate that atomic action semantics can anchor future dynamics while preserving the fidelity of tactile predictions. Per-task standard deviations for PhysWord are reported in Appendix C.1, while detailed experiments on predictive tactile feedback in simulation and the real world are detailed in Appendix C.4. 

Results on LIBERO-Long. We also evaluate PhysWord on the vision-only benchmark, where it predicts only visual Physical Words. As shown in Table 1, PhysWord achieves a mean success rate of 99.6%, with an average per-task SD of 0.3 percentage points. 4.2.1. Effectiveness of Physical Word Latent Reasoning 

We compare multimodal structures and supervision objectives to assess their contributions to Physical Word latent reasoning. Figure 3 (left) summarizes three groups of ablations. Our PhysWord achieves the best overall performance on both visuotactile UniVTAC and vision-only LIBERO-Long. All variants with temporal prediction use the best-performing sampling interval of s = 4. 

Group 1: Individual supervision signals. We first evaluate visual latent supervision alone ( O), tactile latent supervision alone ( T ), and atomic-word prediction alone ( W ). For the latent-only variants, we follow LaST 0 and autoregressively predict latent tokens using the same temporal organization as PhysWord. These variants underperform joint semantic and latent supervision, suggesting that action semantics and future dynamics provide complementary guidance for latent reasoning. 

Group 2: Joint semantic and latent supervision. Combining atomic-word prediction with visual ( WO ) or tactile (W T ) latent supervision improves performance over the corresponding single-objective variants in Group 1. These gains suggest that shared action labels serve as semantic anchors for latent learning, helping the model capture common action semantics while retaining context-specific physical details. We further evaluate WO MLP (82% on Put Bottle and 78% on Lift Can) and W T MLP (80% on Put Bottle and 70% on Lift Can), both of which apply a learnable MLP to the contextual hidden states before aligning them with future latent targets. Both variants perform worse than direct hidden-state supervision. These results suggest that directly supervising the word hidden states more effectively couples future physical dynamics with the action semantics.

Both orders outperform the unimodal variants, suggesting that visual and tactile Physical Words provide complementary predictive context. Meanwhile, Figure 4(b) compares latent-to-image attention maps for O/W /WO and T /W T . As highlighted by the red boxes, joint word and latent supervision concentrates attention on manipulated objects and task-relevant regions. Together, these findings suggest that semantic anchoring promotes consistent action-related representations across contexts while reducing sensitivity to incidental scene variations. Appendix D provides detailed intra-class distance results and t-SNE visualizations (Figure 6). 

4.2.2. Temporal Prediction Interval 

We evaluate Physical Word prediction intervals of 2, 4, 8, and 16 with a fixed action horizon of H = 16. For example, an interval of 2 yields predictions at t + 2,t + 4, . . . , t + 16. As shown in Figure 3, shorter intervals generally improve performance, suggesting that denser predictions capture intermediate action transitions and changes in physical dynamics that coarser sampling may miss. We select an interval of 4 to balance manipulation success and inference efficiency, preserving informative intermediate predictions while limiting reasoning overhead.

# 5. Conclusion and Limitation 

In this paper, we presented PhysWord, a reasoning-before-acting foundation model that uses atomic action semantics to structure latent representations of future physical dynamics. Its temporally organized visual and tactile Physical Words capture evolving motion and contact dynamics, providing predictive context for action generation. This temporal structure also supports the reuse of action primitives across task stages. Experiments on simulation benchmarks and real-world platforms demonstrate strong manipulation performance, generalization to unseen configurations, and effective execution of long-horizon tasks. Our current action vocabulary does not cover whole-body control, such as climbing stairs, which we leave for future work.

# A. Pretraining Data and Details 

A.1. Data Sources and Composition 

We construct the pretraining corpus using 2,000 hours of human demonstrations from Egoscale [60], the full public releases of DROID [61] and OpenNeoData [57], and real-world Sharpa demonstrations from T-Rex [58]. Each training example pairs observation context with action targets, atomic action labels, and future sensory targets according to the available modalities. Future observations serve only as training targets and are not provided as policy inputs at inference. Note that our pretraining focuses on establishing Physical Word reasoning capabilities. We deliberately use a smaller manipulation corpus than those used by our main baselines, e.g., π0.5, N0-VTLA, and T-Rex, to evaluate our approach without an advantage in pretraining data volume. A.2. Data Preprocessing Visual and action quality filtering. Visual filtering identifies black or near-uniform frames, severe underexposure or overexposure, motion blur, and prolonged periods when the manipulating hand or gripper and relevant objects are not visible in any available view. For action quality, we discard records with invalid values or malformed entries and detect abnormal translation or rotation jumps, unusually short or long trajectories, frozen motion, and excessive proportions of stationary steps. Filtering thresholds are calibrated using training data only. We group trajectories by embodiment, action representation, and control frequency, use the 1st and 99th percentiles ( Q01 and Q99 ) as initial references, and manually adjust the thresholds for each filtering criterion. 

Trajectory diversity filtering. To reduce redundant demonstrations, we compare trajectories within each dataset that share the same task and scene. We extract keyframes following the keyframe-discovery procedure of PerAct [62] and compare wrist positions in the world coordinate frame at corresponding keyframes. Trajectories whose corresponding wrist positions differ by less than 2 cm are treated as near-duplicates. We retain at most two demonstrations per group and discard the remainder to reduce motion redundancy that may limit spatial generalization. 

Action representations and retargeting. Robot demonstrations retain their original action spaces. For human-hand data, we follow EgoScale’s processing protocol [60], retaining wrist poses and retargeting finger motions to a 22-DoF dexterous hand. This retains the demonstrated wrist motion while providing hand-action targets compatible with the target hand kinematics. 

A.3. Atomic Action Annotation Annotation method. We use GPT [17] to segment processed demonstrations into atomic action stages and assign labels from our action vocabulary. For bimanual demonstrations, the left and right end-effectors are annotated independently, 

> 14 Physical Words: A Vocabulary for Structuring Latent Reasoning in Robot Manipulation

allowing simultaneous actions to receive distinct labels. We record each stage’s temporal boundaries and align its label with the corresponding observations, providing cross-entropy targets paired with future sensory supervision. Visual and tactile Physical Words use modality-specific variants of the same action label, with tactile variants and their latent supervision included only when tactile observations are available. 

Annotation quality assessment. We randomly sample 500 examples from the pretraining corpus and manually annotate their atomic actions. GPT-generated labels achieve 95% accuracy against these human annotations. Errors primarily involve ambiguous or incidental movements. For example, finger tapping on a table during pauses in manipulation may be mislabeled as click , despite the absence of a button press. These cases highlight the difficulty of distinguishing intentional manipulation from incidental motion. Nevertheless, VLM-based video annotation provides a practical, scalable pipeline for fine-grained atomic-action labeling. PhysWord maintains stable task execution despite these occasional ambiguities, suggesting robustness to limited annotation noise. 

A.4. Pretraining Configuration 

We pretrain PhysWord for two epochs using the same optimization configuration as downstream fine-tuning (Appendix F). We use AdamW with zero weight decay and a per-GPU batch size of 6. The base learning rate is 10 −4, with separate rates of 2 × 10 −6 for the Cosmos backbone and 5 × 10 −5 for the T3 tactile encoder. Training uses BF16 mixed precision and linear warmup over the first 5% of optimizer steps, followed by cosine learning-rate decay to zero.

# E. Failure Case Analysis 

Figure 7 illustrates two failures in dexterous manipulation: paper slippage and insufficient finger closure. 

Paper slippage. Because the paper is thin, grasping too close to its edge provides limited contact area, allowing it to slip between the fingers during lifting. Future data collection could emphasize more stable grasp locations farther from the paper edge. 

Insufficient finger closure. During shuttlecock grasping, the thumb contacts the object before the index finger achieves sufficient closure. The resulting tactile feedback appears to trigger lifting prematurely, before a stable grasp is established. Despite the initial failure, PhysWord detects that the shuttlecock remains unlifted, predicts the Physical Word approach again, and initiates another grasping attempt. This behavior demonstrates its ability to reuse action primitives for recovery based on execution feedback.
