ABSTRACT
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have demonstrated promising rea-
soning capabilities, but deploying them for physical interaction requires reliable
planning that accounts for when actions are executable and how they change the
world. These preconditions and effects are often environment-dependent and are
not fully captured by MLLMs, making adaptation to new environments challeng-
ing. Yet existing adaptation approaches either update model parameters through
costly environment-specific training or rely on unstructured interaction experience
that is difficult to ground and reuse. To address this challenge, we introduce Caus-
Ground, a non-parametric causal interaction grounding framework that distills in-
teraction experience into reusable action-centered causal knowledge. CausGround
learns from experience by identifying action preconditions and effects, and orga-
nizes them in a factorized world-state representation that jointly captures object
attributes and inter-object relations. At planning time, task-relevant causal depen-
dencies are grounded in the current observation, supporting backward reasoning
over action preconditions and forward reasoning over action effects for action
selection and multi-step causal planning. We demonstrate the effectiveness of
CausGround across diverse long-horizon embodied settings spanning simulated
high-level planning, interaction reasoning, and real-world robot manipulation.
Evaluated on the adapted VirtualHome and RoboCasa benchmarks, CausGround
consistently improves vision-based high-level planning across MLLM backbones,
improving average task success from 24.9% to 53.3% on VirtualHome and closed-
loop planning success from 43.0% to 57.0% on RoboCasa. Consistent gains are
also observed on SWITCH benchmark, with average interaction reasoning ac-
curacy improving from 25.8% to 31.9%, indicating stronger reasoning over ac-
tion selection, recovery, and verification. Moreover, real-world experiments fur-
ther show that CausGround can reason backward from MLLM-interpreted goal
states to derive executable high-level action sequences for sequential manipula-
tion. These results demonstrate that explicit action preconditions and effects pro-
vide a reusable causal grounding mechanism for long-horizon embodied planning
across diverse environments without parameter updates.

INTRODUCTION

promising reasoning capabilities for high-level planning (Chen et al., 2025; Driess et al., 2023;
Feng et al., 2025b). Yet successful embodied interaction requires more than knowing what to
do: agents must know when an action is executable and how it changes the world. Such inter-
action knowledge is often environment-dependent, involving available actions, object states, action
preconditions, and effects (Kim et al., 2025; Han et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). As illustrated in

accumulate over long-horizon execution, leading to invalid actions, repeated attempts, and costly
trial-and-error (Raman et al., 2024; Zhi et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2025). Effective embodied plan-
ning therefore requires agents to account for task semantics while adapting their planning to the
interaction dynamics of the environment in which the task is executed.
Existing approaches still fall short in enabling efficient environment adaptation. A direct strategy is
to specialize embodied agents through fine-tuning or reinforcement learning (Xu et al., 2026; Zhai
et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025a), but such parametric adaptation requires substantial environment-
specific interaction data and repeated optimization, making it costly to deploy across diverse envi-
ronments, particularly in real-world settings. Non-parametric approaches instead incorporate inter-
action feedback or episodic memory at inference time (Anwar et al., 2025), but such experience is
often retained as unstructured trajectories or task-level memories (Ginting et al., 2025; Buoso et al.,
2025). This makes it difficult to identify which parts of an experience are causally relevant rather
than incidental, reuse them across tasks, or ground them in the current visual scene. Moreover,
existing representations do not always jointly capture the fine-grained object attributes and inter-
object relations required to determine whether an action’s preconditions are satisfied (Chen et al.,
2025; Shen et al., 2026). This raises a central question: Can interaction experience be distilled into
reusable causal knowledge that tells an embodied agent both what must be true before an action and
what changes as a result?
To this end, we introduce CausGround, a non-parametric causal interaction grounding framework for
adaptive embodied planning. As illustrated in Fig. 1, CausGround abstracts interaction experience
into action-centered causal knowledge, representing each action through its relevant preconditions
and resulting state effects. Rather than storing complete trajectories, it organizes reusable action-
centered causal knowledge over a factorized world-state representation that jointly captures object
attributes and inter-object relations. At planning time, the current observation and task are grounded
into the same representation to construct a task-relevant local causal graph from reusable causal
knowledge. This allows the planner to reason backward over action preconditions to determine
what must happen before an action becomes executable, and forward over state effects to anticipate
how the world should change after execution. Composing such causal dependencies provides a
structured reference for multi-step planning without updating model parameters.

We evaluate CausGround across heterogeneous long-horizon embodied settings. In simulation, we
adapt VirtualHome (Puig et al., 2018) and RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2026) for visually grounded
high-level planning and observe consistent improvements under both one-shot and closed-loop plan-
ning, including an increase in average task success from 24.9% to 53.3% on VirtualHome and from
43.0% to 57.0% under closed-loop planning on RoboCasa. Beyond simulation, we evaluate inter-
action reasoning on SWITCH (Cheng et al., 2025), a real-world egocentric long-horizon interaction
benchmark, where CausGround improves average accuracy from 25.8% to 31.9%. We further val-
idate the framework on real-robot manipulation, where CausGround reasons backward from inter-
preted goal states to derive executable high-level action sequences and successfully complete multi-
step interactions. Together, these results show that explicit causal interaction knowledge provides a
reusable grounding substrate for long-horizon embodied reasoning across diverse environments.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We formulate embodied interaction knowledge around action preconditions and state ef-
fects, and introduce a factorized world-state representation that jointly captures object at-
tributes and inter-object relations, enabling interaction knowledge to be grounded in current
visual observations.
• We introduce CausGround, a non-parametric framework that distills interaction trajecto-
ries into reusable action-centered causal knowledge and dynamically grounds task-relevant
causal dependencies for planning. This enables reasoning over both what must hold before
an action and how the world changes after it without parameter updates.
• We evaluate CausGround across long-horizon embodied settings spanning VirtualHome
and RoboCasa simulations, the real-world egocentric SWITCH benchmark, and real-robot
manipulation, demonstrating consistent gains in planning and interaction reasoning across
environments and MLLM backbones.

RELATED WORKS
Environment Adaptation for Embodied Agents. Embodied agents deployed in new environ-
ments often need to adjust their behavior based on newly acquired interaction experience. Earlier
approaches typically absorb environment-specific experience into model parameters through fine-
tuning, imitation learning, or reinforcement learning (Kim et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2025; Tian et al.,
2026). With the growing capabilities of foundation models, recent work has increasingly explored
adaptation without continual parameter updates, using execution feedback (Wang et al., 2023), past
trajectories (Zhao et al., 2024), or external memory (Sohn et al., 2026) to provide environment-
specific information during deployment.
This shifts environment adaptation from updating the
model itself toward leveraging external environment knowledge. Therefore, effective adaptation
depends not only on how experience is acquired and accumulated, but also on how the underlying
world state is represented.
World State Representations for Embodied Planning. Existing world-state representations vary
substantially in their level of abstraction. Free-form textual representations can flexibly summarize
observations and state changes, but often entangle multiple entities and dynamic properties within
a shared description (Hao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025). Object-centric representations reduce
this entanglement by organizing state information around individual entities (Yoneda et al., 2024;
Feng et al., 2026), while more factorized representations further separate each object into explicit
attribute-value pairs, enabling finer-grained state tracking (Shen et al., 2026; Rozanov & Rei, 2025).
Yet embodied interaction depends not only on the state of individual objects, but also on relations
among them (Ju et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2024). Therefore, a world-state representation for embodied
planning should jointly capture fine-grained object attributes and explicit inter-object relations, pro-
viding a shared basis for grounding observations and environment-specific interaction knowledge.
Interaction-Grounded High-Level Planning. A structured world state provides a basis for ground-
ing environment-specific knowledge, but effective planning further requires turning such knowledge
into actionable guidance. MLLM-based planners can generate actions or action sequences from
current observations and task descriptions (Lv et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025b), leveraging strong
pretrained priors to guide task completion. Before committing actions to the environment, world-
model-based planners further predict action-conditioned state changes (Li et al., 2026; Song et al.,

METHODOLOGY
We aim to adapt embodied planners to environment-specific interaction dynamics without updating
model parameters. To this end, we introduce CausGround, which distills interaction experience into
reusable action-centered causal knowledge that captures both action preconditions and state effects.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, CausGround consists of two main stages: causal interaction knowledge
construction and task-grounded planning. Specifically, we first define a factorized world-state repre-
sentation that serves as a shared interface between current visual observations and causal interaction
knowledge (Sec. 3.1). We then construct reusable action-centered causal knowledge over this rep-
resentation by extracting action preconditions and effects from environment interactions (Sec. 3.2).
Finally, we ground task-relevant causal knowledge in the current task and world state to support
planning with a frozen planner (Sec. 3.3).
3.1
FACTORIZED WORLD-STATE REPRESENTATION
To bridge causal knowledge construction and task-time grounding, CausGround represents the world
state extracted from current observations and the preconditions and effects captured by causal knowl-
edge using the same factorized schema (Fig. 2). Given an RGB observation It, a frozen MLLM
extracts the corresponding factorized world state St, exposing explicit state factors for matching the
action preconditions and effects stored in CausGround. Specifically, we represent the world state at
time t as:

both within a unified world state.
Since observations and causal knowledge share the same factorized schema, action preconditions
and effects can be directly matched against the current world state. Meanwhile, the factorized rep-
resentation makes individual state factors explicitly identifiable, allowing CausGround to identify
individual factors involved in action execution and state transitions.

CausGround abstracts action preconditions and effects from accumulated action-level interaction
trajectories. Each pre-action state provides a set of candidate state factors. More specifically, each
object attribute-value pair or inter-object relation is treated as an atomic candidate precondition.
For each grounded action a(x), CausGround aggregates its corresponding trajectories in Ta, identi-
fies pre-action state factors whose satisfaction consistently distinguishes successful executions from
failures, and retains them in the precondition set C(a(x)), while incidental factors are discarded.
For action effects, CausGround compares the corresponding pre and post-action states of success-
ful executions. State changes that consistently follow action execution are retained in the effect set
E(a(x)), while incidental changes are discarded. Together, C(a(x)) and E(a(x)) characterize what
must hold before the grounded action can be executed and what state changes follow from its exe-
cution. When an effect of one action satisfies a precondition of another, CausGround connects the
two actions through a causal dependency, allowing multi-step interactions to be composed through
such dependencies.
Moreover, CausGround organizes the extracted precondition and effect sets according to their level
of specificity.
Across grounded instances of the same action family a (e.g., OPEN(fridge)
and OPEN(microwave)), CausGround compares their precondition and effect sets to identify
causal regularities that generalize across instances. Preconditions and effects that are consistently
shared across multiple instances are abstracted into generic action knowledge Kgen(a), while regu-
larities that remain valid only for particular objects are preserved as instance-specific knowledge
Kspec(a(x)).
For example, OPEN(fridge) and OPEN(microwave) may share the effect
that openness becomes open, while requiring different object-specific preconditions for success-
ful execution. In this way, generic knowledge captures reusable causal regularities across grounded
instances, while instance-specific knowledge preserves valid object-specific variations. As shown in
the left part of Fig. 2, these two levels together form the reusable action-centered causal knowledge
used for task-time grounding.

EXPERIMENTS
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Benchmarks and Evaluation Protocols. We evaluate CausGround from two complementary per-
spectives: visual high-level planning and interaction reasoning. As illustrated in Fig. 3, we use Vir-
tualHome (Puig et al., 2018) and RoboCasa (Nasiriany et al., 2024; 2026) to evaluate long-horizon
planning from visual observations, and SWITCH (Cheng et al., 2025) to assess fine-grained rea-
soning over states, actions, and interaction outcomes. Beyond benchmark evaluation, we further
validate CausGround on real-world sequential manipulation with a physical robot.
For VirtualHome, a simulated household environment with diverse multi-step activities, we focus
on the image-based planning setting rather than its text-based counterpart. We correct rendered
observations that are inconsistent with the underlying environment state using an image-editing
model, with details provided in Appendix A.1. We report success rate (SR) and task progress, where
progress measures partial completion of the task-specific semantic objectives at episode termination.
To examine performance across planning horizons, we further partition tasks into short, medium, and
long-horizon subsets according to the number of ground-truth high-level actions.
For RoboCasa, a simulation benchmark for household manipulation, we adapt its tasks to high-
level visual planning by treating the officially annotated skill stages as atomic actions and using the
corresponding simulator observations as intermediate states. We report task success rate under both
one-shot planning and closed-loop replanning.
For SWITCH, which evaluates a broader range of embodied interaction reasoning capabilities, we
focus on four categories that most directly probe action-level state-transition reasoning: Action, Re-
covery, Verification State, and Verification Action. Together, these categories cover action selection,

Implementation Details. For each comparison, all methods are evaluated under the same planner
backbone, visual observations, task descriptions, and high-level action space. In our main experi-
ments, we instantiate the planner with Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.5-27B, and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, while
additional model families are considered in Sec. 4.5. We compare CausGround against a Vanilla
planner and two latest methods, SelfReVision (Park et al., 2025) and LookPlanGraph (On-
ishchenko et al., 2025). SelfReVision iteratively critiques, revises, and verifies generated procedural
plans, while LookPlanGraph maintains a dynamically updated scene-memory graph from visual ob-
servations to ground planning. We adapt both methods to the same visual observations and high-level
action space used by CausGround. All planner parameters remain frozen throughout evaluation. Ad-
ditional implementation details, prompts, and baseline adaptations are provided in Appendix C.
Overview. Our experiments progressively examine CausGround from effectiveness to generaliza-
tion and analysis. We first evaluate whether CausGround improves visual high-level planning across
different task horizons on VirtualHome and RoboCasa (Sec. 4.2). Apart from the vanilla planning
performance, we evaluate interaction reasoning on SWITCH (Sec. 4.3) and generalization across dif-
ferent model families and scales (Sec. 4.5). Next, we isolate the contributions of the local world-state
graph and causal interaction knowledge through component analysis (Sec. 4.4). Finally, we qualita-
tively analyze how CausGround changes planning behavior in VirtualHome and investigate whether
simulation-acquired interaction knowledge can support real-world robot planning (Sec. 4.6).

CONCLUSION
We introduce CausGround, a causal interaction grounding framework for adapting MLLM-based
embodied planners to environment-specific interaction dynamics without parameter updates. Instead
of storing interaction experience as unstructured trajectories, CausGround distills reusable action-
centered causal knowledge by identifying action preconditions and effects, expressed under a shared
factorized world-state schema that jointly captures object attributes and inter-object relations. At
planning time, CausGround grounds task-relevant causal knowledge with the current task and world
state, supporting backward reasoning over action preconditions, forward reasoning over action ef-
fects, and long-horizon planning. These results highlight explicit causal interaction knowledge as a
reusable grounding mechanism for long-horizon embodied planning across diverse environments.

A.1.3
OBSERVATION REPAIR
Although VirtualHome maintains an explicit simulator state for interaction execution, its native RGB
renderer can occasionally produce observations that are visually inconsistent with the underlying
state after certain interactions. Such inconsistencies are particularly problematic in our vision-based
setting, since the planner must infer the current world state from RGB observations rather than
privileged symbolic information. We therefore introduce an observation-repair procedure that cor-
rects state-inconsistent visual content while preserving the original camera viewpoint, scene layout,
object identities, and unrelated scene content.
For each inconsistent observation, the repair procedure takes the native rendered frame together with
the executed action and the corresponding simulator-derived state transition as input. We use Qwen-
Image-Edit-2511 (Wu et al., 2025) to generate a corrected observation under a constrained editing
prompt. The edit is restricted to visual changes implied by the executed action, while the camera
viewpoint, scene geometry, object identities, appearance, and unrelated scene content are preserved.
The repair is not allowed to introduce future states, hallucinate occluded or out-of-view objects, or
modify objects unrelated to the current transition.
Each repaired observation is checked against the resulting simulator state using deterministic con-
sistency rules and manual visual inspection. Only validated observations are retained for evaluation.
The privileged simulator state used during this repair and validation process is never exposed to the
planner and serves solely to ensure the RGB observation is consistent with the actual environment.
Repair Prompt. For reproducibility, we summarize the core prompt used for observation repair.

{action}

1. Objective. Edit the native observation only when necessary so that the visible scene is consistent with
the actual post-action simulator state. The executed action and state transition specify what has changed;
they must not be interpreted as instructions to modify unrelated content.
2. Apply only the current state change. Modify only visual content directly implied by the executed ac-
tion and its resulting state transition. Do not introduce effects from future actions, previous unexecuted
actions, or hypothetical task progress.
3. Preserve unaffected scene content. Keep the camera viewpoint, framing, scene layout, character
identity, object identities, geometry, materials, scale, lighting, and all unrelated objects unchanged.
4. Maintain interaction consistency. The edited image must reflect the resulting interaction state. For
example, after a successful GRAB(X), the object should be held by the actor and should no longer re-
main at its source location; after a successful placement action, the object should appear at the specified
destination and should no longer be held.
5. Respect visibility. Do not hallucinate objects that are occluded, inside a closed container, or outside the
current camera view. Do not expose hidden simulator information merely because it is available in the
symbolic state.
6. Avoid unrelated edits. Do not add, remove, replace, recolor, relocate, resize, or alter any entity unre-
lated to the current state transition. Do not change the task layout, camera pose, character appearance,
or background scene.
7. Output requirement. Return one repaired RGB observation that is visually consistent with the post-
action simulator state while remaining maximally faithful to the native render.

A.1.4
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Each task is associated with a private semantic completion specification defined by its target out-
come and required interaction events, with ordering constraints included when necessary. We report
Success Rate (SR) as the percentage of episodes that satisfy the complete task specification within
the interaction budget. Progress (P) measures partial task completion according to the same seman-
tic specification at the end of an episode.
Importantly, evaluation does not require the planner to reproduce the reference action sequence. The
reference programs are used only for benchmark construction, difficulty stratification, and private
evaluation, allowing alternative valid action sequences to receive full credit when they achieve the
required task outcome. An episode terminates once the task is completed or the maximum number
of action attempts is reached. The interaction budgets are 20, 40, and 60 attempts for Short, Medium,
and Long tasks, respectively, and unsuccessful action attempts also consume the budget.

A.2
ROBOCASA
A.2.1
BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION AND TASK SETUP
RoboCasa provides long-horizon household manipulation tasks together with structured task anno-
tations, successful trajectories, and simulator observations. While the original benchmark focuses
on robotic manipulation at the execution level, we adapt its Target Composite tasks to evaluate visual
high-level planning. Specifically, we convert the annotated manipulation trajectories into high-level
planning sequences by abstracting low-level robot control into task-level skill stages and using the
corresponding simulator observations as intermediate visual states.
A.2.2
PLANNING INTERFACE AND EXECUTION PROTOCOL
We abstract RoboCasa’s low-level robot control into a unified high-level action interface consist-
ing of eight task-level APIs: NAVIGATE, PICK, PLACE, OPEN, CLOSE, PRESS, ROTATE, and
TILT. These actions abstract away low-level motion and manipulation details, allowing the plan-
ner to operate directly over task-level manipulation steps. All compared methods receive the same
task description, RGB observation, public object and fixture vocabulary, high-level action API, and
executed-action history, while hidden simulator states and reference action sequences are not ex-
posed.
We evaluate two complementary planning protocols: one-shot planning and closed-loop replan-
ning. In the one-shot setting, the planner generates a complete sequence of high-level actions from
the initial observation and task description before execution. In the replanning setting, the planner
predicts one high-level action at a time. After execution, the resulting RGB observation and SUC-
CESS/FAILURE feedback are returned to the planner for the next decision. This protocol allows the
planner to adapt its subsequent actions to the actual state reached during execution.
A.2.3
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
We evaluate predicted high-level plans by replaying them against the official RoboCasa action
boundaries while preserving the corresponding simulator states and task goal checker. At each
boundary, a predicted action is considered SUCCESS if it matches a valid recorded transition from
the current state and advances execution to the corresponding next state. A well-formed action
that is not applicable at the current boundary is marked as FAILURE. If a predicted action cannot
be resolved because the available official trajectory does not contain the corresponding branch, we
mark it as UNKNOWN rather than treating it as an ordinary planning failure. A task is counted as
successful only when execution reaches the official task goal condition.
We begin with a fixed set of 100 held-out test cases. For each planning protocol, we evaluate all
methods on the shared subset of cases for which no method encounters an UNKNOWN transition.
For one-shot planning, the generated plan is limited to at most 40 high-level actions. For closed-loop
replanning, the planner is allowed at most 25 environment interaction attempts, with each subsequent
decision conditioned on the latest RGB observation and execution history.

B.2
ACTION-CENTERED CAUSAL KNOWLEDGE CONSTRUCTION
CausGround constructs reusable causal knowledge at the level of individual actions.
For each
grounded action, we collect interaction instances with different pre-action world states and compare
their execution outcomes. As illustrated in Fig. 11, factors whose variation is consistently associated
with whether the action can be successfully executed are retained as action-relevant preconditions,
while factors that vary without affecting executability are treated as incidental and discarded. For
example, PUTIN(bread,fridge) fails when the fridge is closed but succeeds when it is open,
indicating that the fridge state is relevant to action execution. In contrast, changing unrelated prop-
erties such as the fridge color does not affect the outcome and therefore should not be retained as a
precondition.
Action effects are extracted by comparing the pre and post-action world states of successful exe-
cutions. State changes that consistently follow an action are retained as its effects, while unrelated
or non-repeatable changes are removed. Together, the extracted preconditions and effects describe
both what must hold before an action can be executed and how the world is expected to change after
execution.

We further organize this knowledge at two levels of specificity. Across grounded instances of the
same action family, preconditions and effects that remain valid across different objects are abstracted
as generic knowledge, while regularities that are specific to particular objects or fixtures are retained
as instance-specific knowledge. For example, OPEN(fridge) and OPEN(microwave) may
share the generic effect that the target becomes open, while preserving different object-specific re-
quirements when necessary.
Finally, CausGround connects individual actions through effect–precondition dependencies. When
an effect produced by one action satisfies a precondition required by another action, a directed
dependency is introduced between them. Such dependencies allow reusable single-action knowledge
to be composed into multi-step causal structures, which are subsequently retrieved and grounded
according to the current task in Sec. B.3.
B.3
TASK-GROUNDED PLANNING
Figure 12 illustrates the task-grounded planning procedure in CausGround. Given a task description,
CausGround first retrieves task-relevant causal knowledge from the interaction library and organizes
it into a task-level causal graph. At each decision step, the current visual observation is converted
into a factorized world state, which is then used to ground the retrieved causal knowledge in the
current environment state before being passed to the planner. The planner predicts the next high-
level action, the environment executes it, and the resulting observation is fed back for the next
planning step.
More concretely, the retrieved task knowledge contains actions, preconditions, effects, and effect–
precondition dependencies that are potentially useful for achieving the task goal. The current world
state is used to identify which retrieved factors are already satisfied, which are currently violated,
and which candidate actions are executable under the present state. This grounded task knowledge
is provided together with the current observation to support state-aware high-level planning.

C.3
CAUSGROUND IMPLEMENTATION
C.3.1
CAUSGROUND LIBRARY CONSTRUCTION
We construct the CausGround library from environment interaction experience, using different
causal identification protocols according to the intervention capability of each environment.
For VirtualHome, we conduct controlled interaction experiments for each action by actively varying
candidate pre-action state factors. A factor is retained as a precondition only when violating it
prevents successful execution, while action effects are identified from state changes that consistently
follow successful executions. This controlled setting allows us to directly test which state factors are
necessary for an action and which changes reliably result from its execution. The resulting global
VirtualHome library contains 58,458 semantic action-hyperedge records, which are consolidated
into 5,614 normalized action rules covering 40 action families. These rules span 193 semantic node
types, 28 state-attribute types, and six explicit relation types, and contain 113,847 required-condition
records and 154,597 effect records.
For environments where controlled interventions are less practical (e.g., RoboCasa and SWITCH),
we instead identify causal regularities under a strict consistency criterion from repeated official
interaction trajectories. A candidate precondition is retained only when it is consistently supported
across successful executions; if the action succeeds even once without that factor, the candidate
is excluded. Candidate effects are filtered similarly, retaining only state changes that consistently
follow successful executions. The resulting RoboCasa CausGround library contains 98 grounded
action mechanisms covering 8 high-level action families and 225 semantic object and fixture types,
with 254 condition records and 416 effect records. For SWITCH, we construct the causal knowledge
from its interaction trajectories using the same consistency-based extraction procedure and apply the
resulting knowledge to the selected interaction-reasoning subtasks.
C.3.2
TASK-TIME RETRIEVAL AND GROUNDING
Given a task description, CausGround identifies the corresponding goal conditions and required in-
teraction events, and retrieves a task-relevant causal subgraph from the environment-specific library.
The retrieved knowledge contains relevant action conditions, effects, and producer dependencies,
and is loaded once at the beginning of each episode rather than reconstructed at every planning step.
During execution, the retrieved causal knowledge is repeatedly grounded in the current environ-
ment state. At each step, the current RGB observation is converted into the factorized local world
state, which is used to determine which retrieved conditions are already satisfied and which pre-
requisite actions remain relevant. The planner first predicts a semantic high-level action, such as
OPEN(fridge). Its semantic arguments are then matched to the corresponding object instances

new observation and public success/failure history update the local world state for the next decision,
while the retrieved causal subgraph itself remains fixed.
