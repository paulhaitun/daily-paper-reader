ABSTRACT 

World Action Models (WAMs) typically represent the world through latent em-beddings from pretrained video models, which entangle heterogeneous aspects of the world, from how it looks (e.g., texture and color) to how it is physically configured (e.g., occupancy and pose). This raises an underexplored question: 

what should constitute the state of a WAM? We argue that the world state should separate how the world appears from how the world evolves under action : the former captures semantic context , while the latter is governed by physical state .To this end, we propose PrismWAM , which decouples physics from semantics 

while jointly modeling state and action. PrismWAM factorizes an embodied task into a Semantic Planning → Physical Rollout → Executable Action pipeline: given only the initial RGB observation, semantic planning learns to predict the final goal image, while physical rollout predicts the fine-grained evolution toward this goal in depth space; executable actions are then generated from the depth rollout. Without any embodied action pretraining or additional data augmenta-tion, PrismWAM achieves strong manipulation performance in both simulation and the real world. Notably, PrismWAM even surpasses a broad range of models with large-scale embodied action pretraining (e.g., OpenWAM-α). More broadly, we reframe World Action Models from modeling how the world looks to how it responds to action , paving the way for action-centric world models grounded in occupancy, flow, tactile sensing, proprioception, and beyond. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

World models have emerged as a promising paradigm for enabling autonomous agents to predict the consequences of their actions (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; LeCun, 2022). By learning how the world evolves over time, these models aim to provide a foundation for planning and action (Hafner et al., 2019). Recent World Action Models (WAMs) extend this paradigm by jointly modeling video and action, often relying on pretrained video backbones to capture the rich visual dynamics of the physical world (Ye et al., 2026b; Li et al., 2026b; Ye et al., 2026a). Yet, a key question remains underexplored: are video latents truly the most appropriate representations for world states? 

Our key observation is that video latents capture a mixture of factors that are not equally relevant to action generation (NVIDIA et al., 2025a). While appearance and texture provide rich semantic context for understanding the scene, they are often invariant to the actions being modeled. For example, when an agent moves or grasps an object, its geometry and pose may change, while its color, texture, and intrinsic appearance typically remain unchanged. Therefore, video latents which entangles the physical state with the semantic context can make the representation substantially richer than what is necessary for modeling how the world evolves under action. This motivates a simple principle: a good state representation in WAMs should distinguish between physical state and semantic context. The distinction is not between information that is useful and useless; rather, it decouples what actions can change from what helps us understand what is being acted upon .Therefore, we propose PrismWAM to separate semantic context from physical dynamics through a 

Semantic Planning → Physical Rollout → Executable Action workflow (see Fig. 1 (b)), rather than treating a single video latent as a monolithic state. Semantic planning infers what should hap-pen in the end from RGB observations, physical rollout models how the world physically evolves to-ward the goal, and executable action determines how to make it happen . We instantiate this principle with a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture built upon a pretrained video backbone (Team Wan et al., 2025), as shown in Fig. 2. During training, the semantic planner predicts the target from the initial RGB observation and transfers its task-level knowledge to the physical rollout via atten-tion layers, while the action branch learns to map the predicted physical evolution to continuous actions. We use depth as the physical representation for its accessibility, physical grounding, and spatial alignment with RGB. Notably, the semantic planner serves only as a training-time teacher, with action inference relying solely on the initial RGB observation and learned depth rollout. Extensive experiments across diverse simulation and real-world benchmarks validate the effective-ness and efficiency of PrismWAM. (1) Without action pretraining, PrismWAM substantially out-performs representative VLAs and WAMs under the same setting, improving the success rate over FastWAM by 43.82 percentage points ( 83 .67 vs. 39 .85 ) on RoboTwin-C2R and the score over starVLA by 29.83 points ( 36 .23 vs. 6.40 ) on RoboDojo; see Fig. 1(c). (2) PrismWAM outperforms several WAMs, including models with action pretraining, with particularly strong gains in precision manipulation and generalization: on RoboDojo Precision, it surpasses OpenWAM-α by 21.37 points (39 .82 vs. 18 .45 ), while improving the success rate on RoboTwin-C2R by 14.62 percentage points (83 .67 vs. 69 .05 ), showing a higher ceiling for complex tasks and novel scenes. (3) PrismWAM even matches or surpasses large-scale pretrained models on several benchmarks despite being trained with only ∼2,300 hours of depth data. On RoboDojo, it improves the score by 16.00 points over G0.5 (36 .23 vs. 20 .23 ) and 16.16 points over Xiaomi-Robotics-1 ( 36 .23 vs. 20 .07 ), showing that scaling action pretraining is not the only path to stronger embodied foundation models; principled architec-tural design offers a more data-efficient alternative. Our key contributions are listed as follows: • A decoupling principle for World Action Models. We identify the entanglement of seman-tics and physics in video latents as a fundamental limitation of existing WAMs, forcing action generation to disentangle high-level task planning from fine-grained physical state transitions. 2Preprint. • An effective and efficient WAM architecture. We instantiate the principle with a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT), where an RGB semantic planner guides depth-based physical rollout, and develop a learning pipeline combining dense depth supervision with sparse RGB goal supervi-sion, enabling fast inference without explicit RGB goal prediction. • Strong performance and data efficiency. PrismWAM outperforms existing VLAs and WAMs, with particularly strong gains in precision manipulation and generalization. It also surpasses a number of models with large-scale action pretraining, demonstrating that principled architec-tural design offers a more data-efficient path toward stronger embodied foundation models. 

2 RELATED WORKS 

Vision-Language-Action Policies. The general and embodied knowledge encoded in pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) (Comanici et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026b; Xiaomi Embodied Intelligence Team, 2025) provides a foundation for vision-language-action (VLA) models that map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions (Bro-han et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024; Black et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025b; Shukor et al., 2025). Internet-scale image–text pretraining provides semantic priors for complex instruction understanding and long-horizon manipulation (Brohan et al., 2023; Black et al., 2024; Physical Intelligence et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025b; Shi et al., 2025). However, such pretraining emphasizes recognition and semantic alignment, with limited explicit supervision for continuous state transitions and how actions change the environment. 

World Action Models. Advances in video generative models (Team Wan et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025a; NVIDIA, 2026) have paved the way for world action models (WAMs), a promising paradigm that jointly models future states and robot actions for generalist robotic manipulation. Most jointly model future RGB observations and action sequences (Zhu et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026). Some methods guide actions based on predicted future RGB observations (Shen et al., 2025; Bi et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026b; Ye et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026a). Others use future prediction as training supervision and generate actions directly from ob-served context (Yuan et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026a; Fang et al., 2026; Gu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a). Both approaches primarily learn state evolution in RGB appearance space, which entangles action-relevant geometry and pose with texture, illumination, and background. This requires mod-eling appearance details that may be irrelevant to control and inferring physical changes from visual variations that may not reflect motion (Qiu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026a). 

Physical Representations for Manipulation. Physical or spatial representations support precise manipulation and spatial generalization (Shridhar et al., 2022; Goyal et al., 2023; Ze et al., 2024; Ke et al., 2024; Qu et al., 2025). Such information can directly guide action generation through depth or point-cloud features extracted from current observations (Yuan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Ke et al., 2024). Complementary approaches introduce physical or spatial structure through supervision, including representation alignment (Li et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026) and auxiliary prediction (Guo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026c; Zhang et al., 2026a; 2025). Recent world action models take RGB and geometry as joint inputs and predict their future states together (Zhao et al., 2026; Lopetegui Gonzalez et al., 2026). This couples semantic and physical information, retaining the RGB-based modeling issues discussed above. PrismWAM separates their roles along a Semantic Planning → Physical Rollout → Executable Action chain, distinguish-ing semantic task understanding from the physical state evolution that guides action execution.

Architecture with Sparse–Dense Temporal Supervision. Building on the pretrained video back-bone WAN (Team Wan et al., 2025), PrismWAM uses RGB and depth streams with asymmetric tem-poral supervision, coupled to an action tower through a mixture-of-transformers (MoT) architecture (Fig. 2, left). The RGB stream predicts a single intended-future frame rF , a sparse target for learning task semantics. The depth stream predicts the denser sequence d1: F , providing intermediate spatial states that guide action evolution. This assigns semantic planning to sparse RGB prediction and physical rollout to dense geometric prediction, whose representation is less entangled with texture and illumination. 4Preprint. The model receives RGB history r≤0, current depth d0, instruction ℓ, and proprioception s. Cam-era views are resized into matching RGB and depth mosaics, and RGB values and clipped metric depth are normalized. The frozen RGB and depth VAEs encode observations and future training targets into latent representations, which modality-specific patch projections convert into visual to-kens. Frozen T5 embeddings and projected, normalized proprioception form the context C, which all branches access through cross-attention. Appendix A.1 specifies input layouts and temporal sampling. During joint training, the RGB token sequence combines clean history latents with noisy latents of the single future target, while the depth sequence combines clean current-depth latents with noisy future-depth latents. The action branch linearly projects each action vector in the noisy sequence into an action token. Gaussian corruption is applied only to prediction targets; observed RGB and depth remain fixed. Each branch receives its own noise-level embedding. 

Chain Attention. A structured attention mask controls information flow between these token groups (Fig. 2, right). Within each visual stream, future tokens attend to observed tokens and one another, while observed tokens cannot read future tokens. Depth tokens additionally read only the observed-RGB tokens. Action tokens attend to one another and to both observed and future depth tokens, with no direct access to RGB. These cross-modal connections are directed: RGB does not read depth or actions, and depth does not read actions. Thus, action prediction uses evolving depth features without accessing the future-RGB target. Specifically, let h(j)

Training Details. We initialize the depth VAE from the pretrained Wan VAE and pretrain it on 2,300 hours of depth data (see Appendix A.2 for the data mixture, losses, and optimization set-tings); both VAEs and T5 remain frozen during policy training. Policy training follows two stages. First, we adapt the pretrained Wan backbone to depth space by predicting depth sequences condi-tioned on current depth and language, using depth-prediction loss alone. We then jointly optimize the RGB and depth streams, action tower, and conditioning projections with sparse RGB goal super-vision, dense intermediate depth supervision, and action supervision, connecting semantic planning, physical rollout, and executable action generation. For joint training, let R≤0, R F and D0, D 1: F denote the encoded RGB and depth observations and targets, and A1: H the normalized action chunk; visual subscripts identify the source frames rather than the number of temporally compressed latent frames. Inference. At inference (Figure 2, lower right), the RGB stream encodes r≤0 once and caches its features for semantic guidance, without generating future RGB frames. Future depth latents and the normalized action chunk are initialized from Gaussian noise and jointly denoised, with the observed RGB, current depth, and task context held fixed. The resulting depth latents can be decoded into a depth sequence for visualization, but action generation uses intermediate rollout features and does not require decoding depth maps. We denormalize the predicted action chunk, execute a prefix, and replan from the next observation.

RQ1: Does physical-state prediction offer more than appearance robustness? Depth encodes distance rather than texture or illumination, making it naturally insensitive to changes in color and lighting. This raises a key question: does physical-state prediction offer more than appearance ro-bustness? Results across multiple benchmarks point to broader benefits for generalization and pre-cise control. PrismWAM achieves 81.70% under layout perturbations on LIBERO-Plus, compared with 77.38% for OpenWAM-α and 66.49% for FastWAM; on LIBERO-Pro, it achieves 88.65% when object attributes such as size, shape, and color change, exceeding OpenWAM-α at 84.45% and FastWAM at 73.00% (table 2). On RoboDojo’s challenging Precision tasks, PrismWAM sub-stantially outperforms pretrained OpenWAM-α, achieving 19.50% success versus 9.25% (table 1). These results suggest that physical-state prediction goes beyond appearance robustness: it supports broader generalization, as illustrated by robustness to changes in scene layout and object shape, and exposes physical structure for precise control .

RQ2: Can PrismWAM remain reliable with real-world-like corruption? Real-world depth sensors may produce measurement noise and missing values, motivating evaluation with real-world-like depth corruption. The corruption profiles, which are informed by the manufacturers’ specifica-tions for Orbbec Gemini 335 and RealSense D435, are controlled stress tests rather than calibrated models of either sensor; full settings are provided in section A.4. As shown in fig. 4, we train and evaluate PrismWAM on RoboTwin-C2R across three corruption levels constructed from Metric Jit-ter , Edge Missing , and Patch Hole . On randomized scenes, all three corruption levels reduce success relative to uncorrupted depth. In the clean setting, light corruption yields a small improvement be-fore performance declines at higher severity. Nevertheless, even at the strongest corruption level, the average performance drops by only 2.45 pp. These results demonstrate that PrismWAM remains robust to diverse forms of depth corruption. 

RQ3: Do PrismWAM’s design choices work together effectively? We examine the contribu-tions of representation composition , attention routing , and supervision and training through ab-lations on RoboTwin-C2R (table 3). Full settings and results for all six ablations are reported in Appendix A.3. Removing either the RGB or depth stream reduces both clean-scene success and transfer, supporting the complementary roles of semantic context and physical state. Direct RGB-to-action access improves clean-scene success but weakens transfer, while exposing depth to future RGB also reduces transfer, favoring the proposed chain from observed semantic context through physical rollout to action. Together, these results support representation decoupling through com-plementary streams , with the full design achieving the best clean–random average. 8Preprint.

5 CONCLUSION 

We introduce PrismWAM , a World Action Model built on the principle of decoupling semantic context from physical state. Rather than treating video latents as a monolithic world representation, PrismWAM decomposes world-action modeling into Semantic Planning → Physical Rollout →

Executable Action : RGB future-frame prediction captures task-level intent, depth models physical state transitions, and the resulting rollout is mapped directly to continuous actions without RGB goal prediction at inference. Across diverse simulation and real-world manipulation benchmarks, PrismWAM achieves strong performance and generalization with only 2,300 hours of depth data and without embodied action pretraining, while matching or surpassing large-scale action-pretrained models on challenging settings. These results suggest that scaling action data is not the only path to stronger embodied foundation models; principled state representations and architectural design offer a more data-efficient alternative. More broadly, PrismWAM paves the way for a new generation of WAMs that shifts the focus from modeling how the world looks to how the world responds to action .While depth is one practical realization of this principle, it can extend to occupancy, flow, tactile sensing, proprioception, and other representations capturing action-induced state transitions. 

Limitations and future work. At present, large-scale real-world manipulation data with synchro-nized depth observations remains relatively limited, which constrains the scale of depth pretraining compared with existing action-pretrained models. We view this primarily as a reflection of the current data landscape: as depth sensing becomes more accessible and embodied datasets incor-porate richer physical modalities, such data is likely to become increasingly available. Physical-state pretraining may therefore emerge as a natural scaling direction for World Action Models, with PrismWAM taking an early step toward establishing this new paradigm. AI USE STATEMENT

Perturbation Setting. We corrupt metric depth at three severity levels (fig. 4 and table 8). Metric Jitter adds Gaussian noise whose standard deviation increases quadratically with depth, ranging from σ0 = 12 –36 mm at 0 m to σ1 = 20 –60 mm at 1 m, with uniform clip-/episode-wise offsets in 

[−25 , 25] and [−50 , 50] mm at Levels 2 and 3, respectively. Edge Missing removes pixels around depth discontinuities exceeding 30 mm, using dilation radii of 1, 5, or 7 pixels and nominal removal fractions of 24–25%, modulated by depth at Levels 2–3. Patch Hole is enabled at Levels 2–3: each camera receives at most one connected hole targeting 1.5% of valid pixels per event, with a duration of 4–8 frames and onset offsets of 16–32 frames within each 80-frame cycle.
