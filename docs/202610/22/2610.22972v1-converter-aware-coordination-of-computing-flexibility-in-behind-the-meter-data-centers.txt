# Converter-Aware Coordination of Computing Flexibility in Behind-the-Meter Data Centers 

Ruolan Cui, Student Member, IEEE, Ruizhang Yang, Member, IEEE, Chenxi Hu, Member, IEEE, 

Francis Yunhe Hou, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Data centers are emerging as large electricity con-sumers whose demand can be reshaped through workload scheduling. However, computing flexibility in behind-the-meter facilities does not automatically translate into electrically realiz-able power flexibility. Ignoring converter and network operating limits may cause converter overloads, protection trips, and interruptions to power supply and computing services. This paper proposes a converter-aware intraday scheduling methodology. First, a task-level mixed-integer linear formulation preserves syn-chronous training configurations and completion requirements, while Minkowski-sum aggregation and Shapley–Folkman convex-ification yield a linear inference-pool representation without unit-level binary variables. The latter admits a conditional relative relaxation-gap bound that decreases inversely with pool size. Second, a four-node electrical model links computing demand, on-site injections, and grid exchange to converter and network limits through lifted voltage variables and second-order cone relaxation. Finally, a mixed-integer model coordinates workloads, photovoltaic generation, energy storage, and grid purchases. Case studies show that electrical limits reshape scheduling and renewable utilization, while on-site resources reduce grid dependence. Under the studied conditions, fixed training cannot support full inference service within voltage limits, whereas coordinated scheduling achieves negligible inference shortage and passes verification against the original alternating-current model. These findings demonstrate the role of computing–electrical coordination in realizing the operational value of workload flexibility. 

Index Terms —Behind-the-meter data center, computing flex-ibility, converter-aware scheduling, electrical feasibility, SOCP relaxation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# DATA centers (DCs) are emerging as large, computation-driven loads whose power demand can be reshaped through workload scheduling [1]–[3]. Unlike traditional loads, their power profiles depend on service-request arrivals, computing-resource allocation, and power-control strate-gies [2], [3]. This dependence creates an opportunity to coordinate computing services with power-system operation, but workload schedulability alone does not establish whether the corresponding power trajectory is electrically feasible. The form of computing flexibility differs across workload types [1], [3]. Training tasks exhibit strong intertemporal schedulability. Their power profiles can be adjusted through their start times, execution progress, and parallel scale [5]. Inference tasks have strict real-time requirements, but their power–throughput relationship can still be adjusted through resource-pool allocation and the proportions of different op-erating configurations [2], [3]. show that software workload orchestration can achieve sus-tained power reductions in graphics processing unit (GPU) clusters [3]. These features allow DCs to be treated as dis-patchable computing loads [6], [7], while requiring distinct representations of synchronous training decisions and weakly coupled inference allocation. This scheduling flexibility becomes particularly valuable in behind-the-meter (BTM) DCs, where site-level resources can be coordinated with computing loads. The BTM configuration highlighted by the North American Electric Reliability Corpo-ration (NERC) enables computing loads, on-site photovoltaic (PV) generation, energy storage (ES), and power-electronic interfaces to operate behind the point of common coupling (PCC) [4], [8]. These components jointly determine the site’s net power exchange, power balance, and electrical operat-ing state [8], [9]. Consequently, the electrical realization of computing flexibility depends on coordinated decisions across workloads, on-site resources, and power-electronic interfaces. Schedules that satisfy computing-service requirements and site energy balance may still violate internal voltage, branch-capacity, PCC exchange, or converter physical limits [9], [10]. Energy-only optimization therefore cannot by itself establish the electrically realizable value of computing flexibility. In the considered site, training and inference occupy separate computing pools but share electrical infrastructure. Moving training toward PV-rich periods must therefore be coordinated with concurrent inference demand and interface loading. Ad-dressing this gap requires both a workload representation that preserves the relevant scheduling choices and an electrical representation that connects these choices to site operation. The literature is reviewed from three related perspectives: (1) computing-load models, (2) converter-aware security con-straints, and (3) intraday scheduling. First, existing DC computing-load models mainly include fine-grained, facility-level, and data-driven models [2], [11]. Fine-grained models provide detailed task and device repre-sentations, but they introduce many discrete variables when embedded into large-scale scheduling [7], [12]. Facility-level models are low-dimensional and easy to optimize, but their coarse representations have difficulty describing computing-service constraints and task-scheduling decisions [13]. Data-driven models have strong prediction capability, but their physical interpretability and compatibility with optimization models are often limited [11], [14]. The remaining modeling need is to preserve the distinct scheduling structures of training and inference without carrying all unit-level discrete decisions into site-level optimization [2]. Second, research on the secure operation of microgrids and power-electronic interfaces mainly includes static security regions, optimal power flow, and converter physical con-arXiv:2610.22972v1 [eess.SY] 16 Oct 2026 2

straints [9], [10]. Static security regions enable efficient secu-rity screening but are typically not integrated with computing-load scheduling [10], [15]. Optimal power-flow models pro-vide detailed physical descriptions but become computation-ally challenging when alternating-current (AC) power flow and power-electronic interfaces are considered [9], [16]. Converter physical constraints describe interface capabilities [17], but these capabilities must be linked to computing-task decisions when DC flexibility is scheduled. The issue for BTM DC operation is therefore not the availability of converter limits in isolation, but their integration with workload decisions and on-site energy coordination. Third, existing optimal scheduling studies for DCs mainly include task-time scheduling and grid-coordinated schedul-ing [3], [7]. The former adjusts power timing through task deferral, continuous execution, or interruption and restart [12], [18]. The latter enables DCs to participate in demand response, active distribution-network optimization, or ancillary-service markets as controllable loads [7], [12]. These studies demon-strate the operational flexibility of DCs. However, translating this flexibility into BTM operation requires joint consider-ation of heterogeneous workload structures, on-site energy resources, and converter and network limits. The remaining need is a tractable scheduling model that preserves training completion and period-wise inference-service requirements while accounting for the electrical conditions under which workload adjustments are used. The central question is therefore how computing-side schedulability can be converted into electrically feasible op-eration at a BTM DC. Two coupled modeling difficulties must be addressed: preserving task-level training choices with-out retaining all discrete inference-unit decisions, and em-bedding nonlinear electrical relationships into a multi-period mixed-integer problem. This paper addresses these difficulties through task-aware workload models and a converter-aware electrical representation. The main contributions are as fol-lows:  

> •

Task-aware linear representations preserve the different scheduling structures of training and inference. Syn-chronous training retains discrete configurations and con-tinuous within-segment throughput, whereas weakly cou-pled inference is represented by a resource-pool linear model through feasible-set aggregation and resource-pool convexification. The inference representation scales with the number of operating segments rather than computing units. Under the stated assumptions, its relative relaxation gap admits an O(1 /N ) upper bound, where N is the number of inference computing units.

This representation couples computing power, on-site injec-tions, and PCC exchange with voltage, PCC and branch-capacity, and converter current and modulation limits. The resulting represen-tation enables tractable electrical coordination within the intraday scheduling model.

II. H IGH -F IDELITY LINEAR COMPUTING MODEL 

This section develops task-aware linear power–throughput models. Here, fidelity refers to retaining discrete operating modes and workload service requirements under the stated assumptions. Strongly coupled training is represented at the task level, whereas weakly coupled inference is convexified at the resource-pool level. 

A. Computing-Unit Power Model 

Computing units in a DC include GPUs and application-specific integrated circuits. The power of a single comput-ing unit is affected by computing throughput and operating mode [19], [20]. Under a fixed operating mode, the computing-unit power can usually be approximated by a linear function of throughput. When the unit switches among idle, low-load, high-load, and power-capping states, its power–throughput relationship becomes piecewise linear and nonconvex [3], [7], [14]. B. Training Workloads 

Training uses large datasets to iteratively update model parameters and weights. A single training job is usually allo-cated to multiple computing units for parallel execution. This process has strong synchronization coupling. Computing units assigned to the same training job must maintain consistent progress and operating states, and the task advances through gradient synchronization, parameter exchange, and communi-cation coordination. Therefore, the smallest schedulable unit for training workloads is a training-task cluster [22], [23]. This section examines coordinated operation, electrical and resource ablations, capacity-mix sensitivity, and the two mod-eling approximations. 

A. Case Parameters and Input Construction 

The case uses a 24-h intraday scheduling horizon divided into 96 15-min periods. The electricity-price and PV-output profiles are based on data from the Electric Reliability Council of Texas for June 1, 2026 [38], [39]. The DC site capacity is 80 MW, the equivalent PUE is 1.21, and the power factor is 0.98. The computing hardware is the NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPU. The theoretical peak throughput of one GPU is 312 tera floating-point operations per second (TFLOP/s), and the achievable throughput is set to 52% of the theoretical 8

peak [23], [40]. In the resource organization, one GPU node contains 8 GPUs, and one cluster contains 32 GPU nodes. Training tasks use clusters as the basic scheduling unit. The candidate parallel scales are {0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256 }. Inference tasks use GPU nodes as the aggregation unit. Training-task samples come from the Alibaba GPU Cluster Dataset 2023, and the inference request curve comes from the BurstGPT workload trace for large language model serving [25], [26]. The training share χ allocates a fraction χ

of the site IT power budget P dc /PUE to training, with the remaining fraction 1 − χ assigned to inference. The energy system includes 80 MW of installed PV capacity and 240 MWh of ES. The maximum charging and discharging powers of the ES are both 60 MW. The SOC range is 10%-90%, the initial and terminal SOC values are both 50%, and the round-trip efficiency is 85% [37]. The ES cycling cost is set to 5 U.S. dollars per megawatt-hour (USD/MWh). The inference-shortage penalty is 50 USD/( TFLOP /s · h). Operating costs are reported in thousands of U.S. dollars (kUSD), where 1 kUSD equals 10 3 USD. For electrical security, the node-voltage range is 0.95–1.05 per unit (p.u.), the interface current and modulation-voltage limits are 1.05 p.u. and 1.20 p.u., and the PCC apparent-power limit is 80 MVA [41], [42]. The electrical model uses an 80-MVA base, with X0 = 0.15 ,

X1 = X2 = Xc1 = Xc2 = 0 .27 p.u., and δ = 35 ◦.The computations are performed on a 64-bit Windows workstation equipped with an Intel Core i5-10505 processor operating at 3.20 GHz and 16 GB of memory. All scenarios are solved in the same environment to ensure comparability. 

B. Baseline Scheduling Results 

The baseline uses χ = 0 .5, PV, ES, and the SOCP-relaxed electrical constraints. Six multi-period training tasks account for 99.23% of the training workload and provide most of its temporal flexibility. Fig. 3(a) shows that PV supplies the computing load and charges ES during PV-rich periods, reducing grid imports; grid purchases and ES discharge increase when PV output is insufficient. Panels (b) and (c) of Fig. 3 show ES shifting PV energy to deficit periods, with some curtailment under the site limits; no period has both charging and discharging above 10 −6 MW. Fig. 3(d) shows training shifting toward PV-rich periods, while inference follows service requests. Around 14:00–15:00, an inference spike coincides with reduced training power. This pattern illustrates coordination through shared electrical infrastructure despite separate computing-resource pools.

D. Capacity-Mix Sensitivity 

Table III compares training-share scenarios in which both resource pools and workload demands are rescaled. At 30% and 40%, inference-shortage penalties dominate cost. At 50%, 60%, and 70%, shortage is negligible at the reported precision, but higher training shares increase grid purchases and cost. The cost driver thus shifts from service shortfall to energy supply across these scenarios. Among these jointly rescaled scenarios, the 50% training share gives the lowest operating cost. 

E. Aggregation Accuracy and AC-Feasibility Verification 

The inference aggregation is evaluated against an integer node-count model. The maximum relative power gap decreases from approximately 4.9% at N = 10 to 0.451%, 0.0448%, and 0.00541% at N = 10 2, 10 3, and 10 4, respectively, approaching zero at 10 5. The diminishing relative gap supports aggregation for large inference pools and is consistent with the conditional bound in Theorem 1. The maximum absolute and normalized voltage-product residuals are 6.056 × 10 −2 and 6.08%, respectively, both on branch (1 , b) . The normalized residual measures relaxation slack relative to the product of the corresponding squared voltage magnitudes. An ex-post check fixes the scheduling commands and re-stores the original AC voltage-product equalities and operating limits. All 96 baseline periods pass, with a maximum equality residual of 9.36 × 10 −8 p.u. and no reported inequality violation. The check confirms that the baseline scheduling commands admit feasible operating points under the original AC model despite the nonzero lifted-voltage residuals. Under the lower-bound completion constraint (7), the base-line meets the training requirements with an aggregate over-completion of 0.0674%. A separately constructed schedule also meets the exact work requirement of every task while retaining baseline inference service and grid purchases; all 96 periods pass the original AC check. These results show that exact task completion is compatible with the electrical operating limits for the tested inputs. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper develops an intraday scheduling methodology for BTM DCs around the distinction between computing-side schedulability and electrically realizable power flexibility. The central difficulty is to retain heterogeneous service require-ments and discrete workload choices while coordinating them with nonlinear converter and network operating conditions. The proposed model addresses this difficulty through differ-ent representations at the task, resource-pool, and electrical-network levels, rather than treating the DC as an unconstrained adjustable load. For synchronous training, task-level configuration decisions preserve parallel-scale choices, continuous within-segment throughput, and completion deadlines. For weakly coupled inference, feasible-set aggregation and resource-pool convexi-fication replace unit-level binary decisions with segment pro-portions. The resulting linear model scales with the number of operating segments and has a conditional O(1 /N ) relative relaxation-gap bound. On the electrical side, a four-node model connects computing demand and on-site injections to PCC exchange and equipment limits. Exact conic representations of converter current and modulation limits, combined with SOCP relaxation of voltage products, allow these relationships to be embedded in mixed-integer multi-period scheduling. The case studies show that electrical constraints reshape the cost-minimizing schedule and renewable utilization relative to energy-only optimization. PV and ES complement workload adjustment by supplying and shifting on-site energy. Under the case parameters, fixed training conflicts with full inference ser-vice within the AC voltage limits; any feasible fixed-training solution must therefore incur service loss. The coordinated baseline instead achieves negligible inference shortage and 10 

passes the ex-post AC check despite nonzero SOCP residu-als. The observed voltage-limit activity explains why energy balance alone is insufficient to assess the use of computing flexibility. Overall, the methodology shows how workload timing, resource allocation, and on-site energy decisions must be coordinated with electrical limits to realize the operational value of computing flexibility.
