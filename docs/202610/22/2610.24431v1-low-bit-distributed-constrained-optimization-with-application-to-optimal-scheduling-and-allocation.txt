Abstract 

This paper proposes a distributed resource allocation algorithm in which each agent communicates only the sign of its local cost gradient—a single bit per directed link per iteration. The algorithm is based on Laplacian-gradient dynamics and preserves the equality (demand) constraint at every iterate, guaranteeing anytime feasibility irrespective of termination time. We make three technical contributions. First, we provide a complete Lyapunov-based convergence analysis for the fixed-step-size regime: the iterates are shown to enter, in at most O(1 /ε 2)iterations, a neighborhood of the optimizer whose radius scales as O(τ ) in the decision variable and O(τ 2) in the cost residual, where τ is the step-size. Crucially, the step-size bound is a time-invariant network constant (depending only on the graph Laplacian spectrum and the cost curvature bounds), so no adaptive tuning is required. Second, we characterize the invariant set under B-connected, weight-balanced digraphs—strictly weaker than the all-time connectivity and doubly-stochastic weights assumed in most prior work—and show that any odd, sign-preserving nonlinearity (not only the signum) yields the same invariant-set and descent structure, with the single-bit protocol being the extreme communication-minimizing instance of a broader nonlinear class. Third, we address local box constraints via smooth penalty relaxation and quantify explicitly how the penalty weight interacts with the step-size bound and the optimality gap. Simulations on economic dispatch (1 000 generators) and CPU schedul-ing (500 servers) demonstrate that the single-bit protocol achieves 5–60 ×

A diminishing step-size schedule is shown empiri-cally to eliminate this gap; its formal convergence analysis is identified as an open problem.

1. Introduction 

Distributed sum-preserving optimization underpins resource allocation in CPU scheduling [1, 2], autonomous vehicle battery charge scheduling [3], cyber-physical energy systems [4, 5], economic dispatch [6, 7], and auto-matic generation control [8]. A common thread in the recent literature is the assumption of infinite-precision data exchange. For instance, projection-based methods [9], drift-plus-penalty algorithms [10], privacy-preserving gra-dient schemes [11], event-triggered accelerated algorithms [12], second-order disturbance-rejection methods [13, 14], and random coordinate descent for open systems [15]. Nonetheless, this demands high-bandwidth, low-latency links that may be unavailable in large-scale or unreliable networks. Low-bit communication has been studied for diffusion strategies [16] and consensus [17, 18], and sign-of-relative-state methods have been applied to unconstrained optimization [19, 20]. Quantized coordination for quadratic objectives appears in [1]. None of these works addresses constrained resource allocation under a single-bit regime. On the application side, distributed economic dispatch has received considerable attention [21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]; all existing methods require multi-bit exchange and, with the exception of Laplacian-gradient (LG) schemes, typically rely on all-time network connectivity and doubly-stochastic weights. A related line of work addresses communication efficiency in federated learning through zeroth-order (ZO-FL) optimization. In [30], the authors propose FedZO, a derivative-free FL method that constructs stochastic gra-dient estimators from function evaluations, thereby enabling optimization when explicit gradient information is unavailable; an over-the-air computa-tion variant is further developed to reduce the communication burden in wireless FL. More recently, [31] proposes DeComFL, which exploits the de-composition of a ZO gradient estimator into a scalar gradient magnitude and 2a shared perturbation direction to reduce the per-round communication from 

O(d) to O(1), independent of the dimension d of the optimization variable. This direction is pushed further in [32], where only the signs of the ZO gradi-ent scalars, together with the information needed to reconstruct the common perturbation directions, are communicated, yielding an extremely low com-munication footprint while retaining applicability to high-dimensional model parameters. Reducing the per-iteration communication payload is therefore a press-ing practical concern: lower bandwidth consumption enables deployment over resource-constrained or unreliable links, simplifies protocol design by replacing floating-point encoding/decoding with a single binary flag, and ac-celerates coordination in latency-sensitive applications such as real-time CPU scheduling and grid-integrated energy dispatch. Henceforth, this paper ad-dresses the following question: can distributed resource allocation be solved when each agent communicates only a single bit per iteration? We answer affirmatively by proposing a single-bit LG dynamics in which every agent broadcasts solely the sign of its local gradient (with a one-time multi-bit fea-sibility initialization). Specifically, we make the following main contributions relative to the state-of-the-art: 1. Single-bit communication. The proposed algorithm requires each agent to transmit only {+1 , −1} per link per iteration. This is specif-ically for scalar-valued resource variables as in many distributed re-source allocation formulations, e.g., economic dispatch, load balancing, bandwidth allocation, charging-rate allocation, and proportional shar-ing problems, where each agent contributes one scalar amount to satisfy a global demand constraint. This is, to the best of our knowledge, the first distributed resource allocation scheme with provable convergence under one-bit data exchange (with a one-time multi-bit feasibility ini-tialization), and it yields lower per-iteration computational cost than existing methods (verified empirically in Section 5). 2. Anytime feasibility. The equality constraint is preserved at every 

iteration, not merely in the limit. This is a strict improvement over ADMM-based approaches [33, 34, 3], which achieve feasibility only asymptotically and therefore cannot guarantee constraint satisfaction upon early termination. 3. Weak connectivity and weight-balance. Convergence is estab-3lished under uniform connectivity of the time-varying directed graph, together with a weight-balance condition. Both requirements are strictly weaker than the all-time connectivity and doubly-stochastic weights as-sumed in [33, 34, 35, 36, 22, 21]. 4. General nonlinear analysis. The Lyapunov-based convergence proof applies to any odd, sign-preserving nonlinearity (i.e., not only the signum function), and accommodates non-quadratic objectives with smooth penalty terms for box-constraint handling. 

Outline. Section 2 formulates the problem and collects auxiliary lemmas. The single-bit algorithm is presented in Section 3 with convergence analysis in Section 4. Simulations on economic dispatch and CPU scheduling appear in Section 5. Section 6 concludes.

3. The Single-Bit Allocation Algorithm 

The key idea is to replace the full-precision gradient exchange in standard LG dynamics [6, 36, 44] with a single-bit transmission: each agent broadcasts only sgn( ∂xi fi) to its out-neighbours. Concretely, under the standard pro-tocol every agent sends a 64-bit floating-point value per link per iteration, yielding a network-wide payload of 64 |E (k)| bits per round. The proposed scheme reduces this to |E (k)| bits. Thus, a 64 × reduction that grows linearly with network size and link density; for instance, a network of n=1000 agents with average out-degree 10 saves approximately 640 kbits per iteration. Relative to the existing literature, Algorithm 1 simultaneously provides three relaxations: (i) all-time connectivity is weakened to B-connecti-vity (Assumption 2(ii)), accommodating intermittent link failures; (ii) dou-bly-stochastic weights are replaced by the weaker weight-balance condition, eliminating the need for compensation algorithms upon topology changes [55]; and (iii) per-link communication is reduced from multi-bit to a single bit. • Part (i): As long as the agents’ gradient signs still disagree with each other by a non-negligible amount, the Laplacian sign-update is guaran-teed to decrease the optimality residual F (k) by at least a fixed amount per iteration. Remark 15. Note that the optimization variables in our framework should not necessarily be interpreted as instantaneous actuator commands. In a practical implementation, the optimization layer would normally provide set-points or references to a lower-level controller that accounts for actuator dy-namics and other physical constraints. Consequently, the bounded oscillations observed in the optimization iterates do not automatically imply that physi-cal actuators must switch at the same frequency or with the same amplitude. However, in applications where the optimization output is closely applied to physical devices, for example in economic dispatch, rapid variations of gener-ator setpoints could lead to undesirable interactions with generator governor dynamics if the optimization update rate is comparable to the actuator band-width. Similarly, in CPU scheduling, rapid oscillations in the requested CPU frequency could result in excessive dynamic voltage/frequency transitions or undesirable power fluctuations. These effects are implementation-dependent 

29 and are therefore not captured by the present optimization model. To mitigate such effects in practical cases, the optimization layer can operate at a slower rate than the low-level actuator controller, or the computed setpoints can be passed through an actuator-aware tracking/rate-limiting setup. 

6. Conclusions and Future Work 

We have presented a single-bit distributed resource allocation algorithm based on Laplacian-gradient dynamics, in which each agent broadcasts only the sign of its local cost gradient. The Laplacian structure on weight-balanced digraphs preserves the demand constraint at every iterate, enabling anytime termination – unlike ADMM or primal-dual methods that achieve feasibility only asymptotically. Under a fixed step-size and uniform connectivity of the union graph, the iterates enter a neighborhood of the optimizer in finite time, with the opti-mality gap scaling linearly in the step-size (decision variable) and quadrati-cally (cost residual). A formal ε-convergence guarantee follows directly: any prescribed accuracy is attained by choosing a proportionally small step-size. The analysis relies only on sign-preservation and uniform boundedness, so the single-bit protocol is the communication-minimizing instance of a broader class of odd nonlinearities sharing the same Lyapunov structure. Several limitations remain open. The feasible initialization may require a one-time multi-bit spanning-tree routine, partially offsetting the single-bit savings in short-horizon settings. The penalty-function treatment of box con-straints provides only approximate satisfaction, with the constraint tightness, step-size, and penalty weight coupled in a trade-off that deserves sharper characterization. Finally, while diminishing step-sizes eliminate the resid-ual gap empirically, a formal convergence rate analysis is complicated by the discontinuity of the signum at zero and remains an important direction for fu-ture work, alongside noise resilience, connections to the compressed-gradient literature, and extensions to non-balanced digraphs and multi-dimensional decisions. Moreover, communication delays and asynchronous packet arrivals are not analyzed in the present work and constitute an interesting direction for future research. The underlying approach can be extended to the vector case. Specifically, suppose that ( xi ∈ Rd). The sign operation can be applied entry-wise, i.e., the transmitted message consists of the signs of the d coordinates. With this modification, the key properties used in our analysis continue to hold. In 30 particular, feasibility is preserved, the KKT characterization remains valid, and the convergence argument extends to the vector setting. The only change in the convergence bound is in the graph-dependent constant, which acquires the corresponding dimension-dependent factor (of order d) due to the norm conversion between the coordinate-wise and vector representations. This extension is an important direction for future research.
