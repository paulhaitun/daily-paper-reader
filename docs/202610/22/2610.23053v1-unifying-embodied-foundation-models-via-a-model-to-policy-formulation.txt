## Abstract 

Vision-language-action (VLA) models and world-action models (WAMs) have emerged as promising approaches to robot learning and control. These models integrate multimodal perception and action generation, but differ in their architec-tures, output representations, and learning objectives. In this paper, we present a unified, architecture-agnostic probabilistic formulation for embodied foundation models based on stochastic kernels. Our formulation has two key components: (i) we characterize embodied foundation models through conditional output distribu-tions and action-realization kernels, which together induce task-conditioned robot policies; and (ii) we connect pretraining and post-training to policy deployment, accommodating model-specific pretraining objectives and offline or online post-training strategies. We instantiate the formulation for OpenVLA and DreamZero, covering autoregressive action-token prediction and joint video-action generation. 

## 1 Introduction 

Robot learning is moving from task-specific controllers toward embodied AI systems that learn reusable perception-to-action policies across tasks and embodiments [O’Neill et al., 2024, Team et al., 2024b]. At its current stage, embodied AI largely adapts the broader foundation-model development pipeline to physical interaction [Driess et al., 2023]. This pipeline combines a shared architectural core with three stages: pre-training, post-training, and deployment. The shared architectural core is typically based on the Transformer family, with modality-specific encoders, tokenizers, or output heads added (if needed) [Vaswani et al., 2017, Brown et al., 2020, Team et al., 2024a, Xu et al., 2025, Team, 2026]. First, pre-training learns general representations from large-scale corpora and multimodal collections through model-specific objectives [Brown et al., 2020, Team et al., 2024a, Team, 2026]. Specifically for LLMs, the resulting next-token loss follows predictable power-law relations in model size, data, and compute (similarly rigorous scaling laws are less established for other foundation models) [Kaplan et al., 2020, Hoffmann et al., 2022, Yang et al., 2025]. Second, post-training adapts the pretrained model to follow instructions, align with human or verifiable preferences, and improve downstream task performance [Wei et al., 2021, Ouyang et al., 2022]. Common approaches include supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and preference optimization (e.g., DPO and KTO) [Ouyang et al., 2022, Rafailov et al., 2023, Ethayarajh et al., 2024]. Other routes use reinforcement learning (e.g., RLHF and RLVR, often implemented with PPO or GRPO) or on-policy distillation (OPD) [Ouyang et al., 2022, Schulman et al., 2017, Shao et al., 2024, Guo et al., 2025, Agarwal et al., 2024]. Finally, deployment turns the trained model into a reusable inference and reasoning core. The above foundation-model pipeline has shown broad effectiveness across scales and modalities, underpinning recent language and reasoning models (e.g., GPT-5.6 and DeepSeek-V4), multimodal or omnimodal models such as MiniMax M3 and Qwen3.5-Omni, and generative

models including FLUX.2 [Achiam et al., 2023, Xu et al., 2026, Team, 2026]. The resulting models can also serve as reasoning and perception cores for agents [Hong et al., 2026a]. Such agents support tool use, coding, digital-environment interaction, and multi-agent coordination [Yao et al., 2022, Schick et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2024, Wu et al., 2023]. Can the success of large foundation models be carried into the physical world? The above non-embodied foundation models generally learn a mapping fθ : X → Y from input data to predictions or generated content [Hong et al., 2026b]. However, for robot learning, embodied foundation models aim to induce a task-conditioned policy πθ : T × C → P (A) that maps the available interaction history and task condition to a probability distribution over robot actions. Fitting a fixed data distribution is insufficient, since a policy must also perform well on the trajectories induced by its own actions [Kaelbling et al., 1998, Ross et al., 2011, Brohan et al., 2023]. Vision-language-action (VLA) models pursue this policy-learning objective by combining visual observations and language instructions to generate robot actions. The Robotics Transformer (RT) series develops this approach from RT-1’s discretized action prediction to RT-2’s transfer of internet-scale visual-semantic knowledge through autoregressive action-token prediction [Brohan et al., 2022, 2023]. OpenVLA likewise adapts a pretrained vision-language backbone with an action-token cross-entropy objective, while π0 couples such a backbone to a flow-matching action expert that generates continuous action chunks [Kim et al., 2024, Black et al., 2024]. Scaling their robot pre-training is constrained by the difficulty of collecting real robot trajectories across diverse tasks, environments, and embodiments [O’Neill et al., 2024, Black et al., 2024]. Video pre-training offers a complementary route to learning motion and scene dynamics from internet-scale data without robot action labels, reducing reliance on embodiment-specific trajectories for acquiring these priors [Assran et al., 2025, Ye et al., 2026]. For control, the value of this pre-training lies in acquiring transferable regularities of physical interaction that support action, rather than visual realism alone [Assran et al., 2025]. Such priors support control through predictive world models, as in UniPi, which uses inverse dynamics to extract robot actions from generated video plans [Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018, Du et al., 2023]. World-action models (WAMs) couple these visual predictions with action generation under language conditioning, as in DreamZero, which adapts a pretrained video diffusion backbone to jointly generate future video and robot actions [Ye et al., 2026]. Conditioned on visual history and proprioception, DreamZero uses flow matching within chunks and autoregressive generation across chunks [Ye et al., 2026]. Real robot trajectories ground these pretrained representations in embodiment-specific commands and physical transitions, supporting transfer across tasks, environments, and embodiments [O’Neill et al., 2024, Intelligence et al., 2025]. Adapting these representations to downstream control can also change the model’s outputs, as π0.5

adds a continuous action expert during post-training after discrete-token pre-training [Intelligence et al., 2025]. The resulting outputs must then be converted into executable actions through decoding and action selection or scheduling [Kim et al., 2024, Black et al., 2024, Ye et al., 2026]. These actions change the observations conditioning subsequent predictions, so the deployed policy depends on both the learned output distribution and its realization in closed-loop interaction. A common description must therefore connect these two components with the objectives and data used in pre-training and post-training. Motivated by this connection, we develop an architecture-agnostic probabilistic formulation based on stochastic kernels. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first formulation that explicitly connects model output distributions and action realization with pre-training, post-training, and closed-loop robot interaction for both VLAs and WAMs. We instantiate it for OpenVLA and DreamZero. 

## 2 Task-Conditioned Robot Interaction Setting 

We consider a robot interaction setting in which a robot observes its environment and takes actions to complete a given task. Let T denote the number of action steps, indexed by t ∈ { 0, . . . , T − 1}, with 

ot ∈ O denoting the observation available at step t and at ∈ A the action selected at that step, where 

O is the observation space and A is the action space. Specifically, given a task condition c ∈ C and an initial observation o0, the robot selects at based on the available trajectory τ≤t = ( o0, a 0, . . . , o t) ∈T , where C is the space of task conditions and T is the space of partial trajectories. The robot then receives ot+1 and repeats this process with the new observation for t = 0 , . . . , T − 1. After T action steps, the resulting complete trajectory is τ = ( o0, a 0, . . . , a T −1, o T ). Within this setting, we aim to model task-conditioned action selection through a policy πθ : T × C → P (A), where P(A) denotes the space of probability distributions over actions and θ denotes the learnable parameters. At each step t, given τ≤t and c, the robot samples its next action through at ∼ πθ (· | τ≤t, c ).23 Unified Formulation for Embodied Foundation Models

## 5 World Action Models (WAMs) 

While conventional vision-language-action (VLA) models primarily learn to predict robot actions from visual and language inputs, world-action models (WAMs) additionally model future environment evolution by jointly predicting future observations and robot actions. We discuss DreamZero [Ye et al., 2026] in detail below.

## 6 Discussion 

We present the first architecture-agnostic probabilistic formulation based on stochastic kernels that explicitly connects pre-training, post-training, and closed-loop robot interaction for embodied foundation models. By relating heterogeneous model outputs to task-conditioned robot policies, this formulation provides a common probabilistic language for understanding embodied models with different architectures, output representations, and learning objectives. More importantly, it clarifies how pre-training and post-training shape the policies deployed in closed-loop interaction.
