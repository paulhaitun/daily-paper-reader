## Abstract 

Intelligent warehouse systems have transformed logistics by inte-grating robots with minimal infrastructure changes, boosting effi-ciency and optimizing workflows compared to traditional human-only operations. However, human-robot collaboration introduces new challenges, particularly due to human uncertainty, which leads to dynamic interactions and unpredictable behavior. To address these complexities, this paper presents the Multi-Agent Pickup-and-Delivery with Human Collaboration (MAPDwHC) problem, an extension of the Multi-Agent Pickup-and-Delivery (MAPD) problem that incorporates human unpredictability. This problem adds key constraints, such as dynamic obstacles caused by robots temporarily blocking paths when interacting with humans, and the need for real-time responsiveness . We formalize the MAPDwHC problem with additional constraints and properties. Based on this problem setting, we propose the CARVS algorithm, which improves task assignment and path planning by integrating task swapping, validation checks, and random initialization. CARVS reduces congestion, enhances adaptability to human-robot interactions, and ensures real-time responsiveness. Simulation results show that CARVS significantly reduces timeouts of path planning in large-scale scenarios where traditional MAPD algorithms fail, improving both efficiency and scalability.

## 2 Related Works 2.1 Multi-Agent Path Finding 

In Multi-Agent Pathfinding (MAPF), algorithms are broadly clas-sified into optimal and suboptimal categories. Optimal algorithms like CBS [24] and MA-CBS [25] aim to minimize the total cost but are computationally expensive, making them impractical for real-time applications with many agents. As a result, suboptimal algorithms have become the focus of MAPF research, prioritizing runtime efficiency over strict optimality. Suboptimal algorithms are typically divided into complete and in-complete methods. Incomplete approaches, such as Rolling-Horizon Collision Resolution (RHCR) [14], simplify planning by resolving conflicts within limited timeframes but can suffer from deadlocks in constrained environments due to their non-exhaustive search. On the other hand, complete suboptimal algorithms like LaCAM [21] and LaCAM* [22] use dual-level search techniques that guarantee finding a feasible solution or proving its non-existence, though they can result in significant computational delays, particularly in complex scenarios like those encountered in MAPDwHC. Recent studies [2, 9] have applied multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) [28] to MAPF problems, but these methods of-ten struggle to meet the runtime requirements necessary for real-time human-robot collaboration in the MAPDwHC problem. While MARL can improve adaptability and decision-making, it still faces challenges in balancing computational demands with the need for real-time solutions. 

## 2.2 Task Assignment 

Task assignment is often overlooked in traditional Multi-Agent Pathfinding (MAPF), resulting in inefficiencies as agents may be assigned suboptimal tasks that are difficult to coordinate. To address this, several studies have integrated task assignment with path planning. Nguyen et al. [20] propose a generalized Combined Target Assignment and Path Finding (TAPF) model using Answer Set Programming, which handles scenarios with unequal numbers of agents and tasks, and incorporates complex requirements such as deadlines. Honig et al. [6] introduce Conflict-Based Search with Optimal Task Assignment (CBS-TA), integrating task assignment directly into pathfinding to optimize both simultaneously. However, these integrated approaches often struggle with scalability in large or complex environments. Okumura et al. [23] address the unlabeled variant of MAPF, where any agent may be sent to any target. Their algorithm TSWAP swaps targets between agents and uses a cached distance table to keep the planning cost of each time step low even at large agent counts. All targets are given at the start rather than arriving over time. While MAPD addresses dynamic environments with real-time task arrivals, TAPF is typically designed for static settings. Ma et al. [18] extend the Lifelong MAPF framework to Multi-Agent Pickup and Delivery (MAPD), proposing algorithms like Token Passing (TP) and Token Passing with Task Swaps (TPTS) to improve flexibility and efficiency. Building on this, Liu et al. [15] develop MAPD strategies by framing task allocation as a Travel-ing Salesman Problem for each agent, while integrating deadlock avoidance mechanisms. In a recent work, Xu et al. [29] introduce algorithms for the Multi-Goal Multi-Agent Pickup and Delivery (MG-MAPD) prob-lem, extending MAPD to handle tasks with multiple goal locations. While their algorithms, LNS-PBS and LNS-wPBS, show significant CARVS: A Congestion-Aware Algorithm for Online MAPD in Human-Collaborative Warehouses DAI ’26, November 29–December 2, 2026, Hong Kong, China 

scalability and efficiency improvements, particularly in large-scale settings, they focus primarily on offline environments and assume that tasks are known in advance. In contrast, our work deals with real-time task arrivals and dynamic human-robot collaboration, which introduces additional complexities and requires more adap-tive solutions. Despite these advancements, challenges remain in managing dynamically increasing tasks and ensuring scalability in complex environments. For instance, Chen et al. [1] present a unified ap-proach combining task assignment and MAPF but may encounter efficiency issues in larger-scale settings. Additionally, Krnjaic et al. ## 4 Method 4.1 Greedy Assignment 

Since both MAPD and MAPF are NP-hard [19], finding optimal so-lutions within reasonable time is challenging. We simplify the prob-lem by separating it into two levels: task assignment and pathfind-ing. At the high level, tasks are assigned to agents, and at the low level, existing MAPF algorithms solve the pathfinding problem. A straightforward yet suboptimal approach to solve the task assignment in MAPDwHC is the First-Come, First-Served (FCFS) strategy, where tasks are processed strictly in arrival order. While simple to implement, FCFS often causes unnecessary back-and-forth travel. A more effective alternative is the Greedy Assignment (GA) algorithm. Let 𝑎 𝑖 .𝑔 denote the current goal vertex of agent 𝑎 𝑖 ,and let ℎ(· , ·) be a heuristic function that estimates the travel cost be-tween two vertices (e.g., Manhattan distance). For each unassigned agent 𝑎 𝑖 , if its current load 𝑐 𝑖 < K, GA selects the nearest available task as its goal, i.e., 𝑎 𝑖 .𝑔 ← min 𝑠 𝑖 ∈ T 𝑡 ℎ(𝑎 𝑖 .𝑣, 𝑠 𝑖 ), where 𝑎 𝑖 .𝑣 is the agent’s current vertex; if 𝑐 𝑖 = K (capacity full), the agent is directed to the nearest delivery point, i.e., 𝑎 𝑖 .𝑔 ← min 𝑑 𝑖 ∈𝑉 𝐷 ℎ(𝑎 𝑖 .𝑣, 𝑑 𝑖 ). To respect Property 1, any agent currently interacting with a human has its position removed from the graph. The resulting set of start-goal pairs is then passed to a complete MAPF algorithm to compute collision-free paths. Note that FCFS is a special case of GA where the heuristic ℎ assigns tasks in arrival order. Theorem 3. A well-formed MAPDwHC instance can be solved by the greedy assignment algorithm in combination with any complete MAPF algorithm, together with a heuristic function ℎ that ensures no agents have overlapping goals. 

Proof. We can design a heuristic function ℎ that selects a goal based primarily on the Manhattan distance, while ensuring that no agents have overlapping goals. Given Property 3, the selected goals form a MAPF instance that can be solved by any complete MAPF algorithm. Although Property 3 may not always be satisfied when some agents are interacting with humans, Theorem 1 proves that the MAPF instance will become solvable within a finite number of time steps. By applying Theorem 1, we can conclude that Theorem 3 also holds. □

Complete MAPF algorithms always return either a valid plan or a proof that no solution exists. On unsolvable instances, however, demonstrating unsolvability still forces the search to (eventually) explore the entire search space, so the runtime can grow prohibi-tively large—even when advanced pruning or ordering heuristics are used. 

## 4.2 Solution Refinement 

We propose CARVS (Congestion-Aware Random-initialization, 

Validation, and Swapping), an approach that enhances a baseline greedy assignment with three key improvements, as outlined in Algorithm 1. CARVS is designed to optimize task allocation while keeping the underlying logic lightweight, thereby ensuring efficient performance in real-world deployments. The key refinement steps are highlighted in blue. The refinement algorithm is composed of two main parts, with the three improvements illustrated in Fig-ure 2. Together, these enhancements make CARVS more robust than purely greedy methods in large-scale warehouses. CARVS adopts a complete MAPF solver as its back-end. Lever-aging this completeness, CARVS is guaranteed to solve any well-formed MAPDwHC instance (a formal proof is given later). The method’s three enhancements are specifically crafted to prevent the excessive runtimes that complete MAPF solvers can incur when temporary blockages arise, enabling CARVS to pair seamlessly with any future, more advanced complete MAPF algorithm. Although the solvers used in this paper are centralized, any centralized or distributed MAPF solver that retains the completeness property could serve equally well as CARVS’s underlying planner. 

4.2.1 Validate Assignment. The first part of the improvements fo-cuses on refining the current assignment. A key idea is task swap-ping, which is only allowed during pickup tasks before items are collected (i.e. 𝜙 (𝜏 𝑖 , 𝑎 𝑖 , 𝑡 ) = 𝜙 (𝜏 𝑗 , 𝑎 𝑗 , 𝑡 ) = 1). When two agents’ short-est paths intersect at each other’s current positions and both are heading to pickup points, the algorithm can swap their tasks. Since agents can not swap physical items with one another, delivery tasks will not be swapped between them. Another important idea is to validate whether an agent can reach its goal using a path planning algorithm 𝜔 (e.g. A*). If the agent cannot reach its goal, a waiting point 𝑤 𝑖 ∈ 𝑉 𝑊 will replace its real goal. The set of waiting points

The heuristic function ℎ in the algorithm can select a waiting point near the agent’s pickup or delivery point based on the Man-hattan distance, while ensuring that no agents have overlapping goals. When an agent’s goal is temporarily unreachable, redirect-ing it to a nearby waiting point alleviates congestion in key areas. 4.2.2 Random Initialization. Random initialization modifies the initial assignment for a free agent by randomly assigning its first goal from the available tasks T𝑡 . This adjustment helps alleviate con-gestion, particularly in areas with uneven distributions of pickup points or high task density. Without this improvement, if a ro-bot consistently selects the nearest goal after unloading, it may end up handling only tasks near the drop-off zone, especially in regions where new tasks frequently appear. While the greedy as-signment performs well on open maps, it can lead to problems in environments with narrow passages. In such cases, corridors may be blocked for extended periods, increasing robot density in certain areas and causing congestion. By selecting the first goal randomly and then choosing the nearest goal for subsequent tasks, CARVS effectively disperses the robots across the map, reducing bottlenecks and ensuring smoother overall operation. Proof. We can design a heuristic function ℎ that selects a goal based primarily on the Manhattan distance, ensuring that no agents have overlapping goals. Given Property 3, the selected goals form a MAPF instance that can be solved by any complete MAPF al-gorithm. Although Property 3 may not always be satisfied when some agents interact with humans, Theorem 1 guarantees that the MAPF instance will become solvable within a finite number of time steps. Therefore, a valid MAPF instance can be constructed from the current task assignment. Swapping goals does not invalidate the MAPF instance, and if no suitable waiting point is found, retaining the previous assignment is acceptable. By following Theorem 1, we can conclude that Theorem 4 holds. □

## 5 Experiment 

Following Theorem 2, we can compare CARVS against traditional MAPD algorithms. The experiments aim to demonstrate the effec-tiveness of CARVS combined with MAPF algorithms in solving the MAPDwHC problem, with a focus on potential real-world ap-plications. We use a simulation that aligns human activities with real-world scenarios for comparisons. The 𝜂 is set to 1 second. Fail-ing to produce a solution within this time limit results in a skipped time step, where all agents remain stationary until the next time step. Each time step, a robot moves 0.75 meters (one edge long), while humans, moving at 1.5 m/s, are set to twice the robot’s speed. In a real-world deployment the signal 𝜎 (𝑎 𝑖 , 𝑡 ) is triggered directly by the human worker. In the simulation, human workers are following an A* path and 𝜎 (𝑎 𝑖 , 𝑡 ) is generated from the predicted trajectory. In the simulation, the interaction duration follows a degenerate dis-tribution with Pr (𝛿 = 10 ) = 1, and hence E[𝛿 ] = 10 time steps (10 seconds). Performance is evaluated based on total tasks completed. We test several recent MAPF algorithms with CARVS, including EECBS [13], RHCR [14], CBSH2-RTC [12], LaCAM [21], LaCAM2 [22], and MAPF-LNS2 [11]. Not all MAPF algorithms perform well on complex maps with many agents, so we select LaCAM2 for its best performance. A detailed comparison of the MAPF algorithms is provided in Appendix C. Most of the MAPF algorithms are implemented in C++, and we have added a wrapper to make them compatible with our simulator and the MAPDwHC problem. The wrapper generates a map file and a MAPF instance for the algorithm. After the program completes, the wrapper reads and parses the output solution for the simulation. For the MAPD algorithms, we include results from TP/TPTS [15], MCA/RMCA [29], TSWAP [23], and MAPD-d [16]. We run MAPD-d as 𝑘 -TP with 𝑘 = 0, because our setting provides no reliable way to fix 𝑘 in advance and a larger 𝑘 degrades performance sharply. Appendix D explains this choice. These algorithms were adapted for our simulation environment, with detailed modifications provided in Appendix D. The simulator and task assignment algorithms were developed in Python and run on a server with an AMD EPYC 7H12 64-Core Processor and 128GB RAM. Each configuration was tested with 10 random seeds affecting agent positions, task allocation, and MAPF algorithms, with results averaged across all seeds.

Algorithm scalability is a critical factor in performance assess-ment. Figure 4 illustrates how task completion varies across differ-ent map layouts and agent counts. As map congestion increases, most algorithms exhibit a decline in completed tasks. However, CARVS demonstrates remarkable robustness, with its performance advantage becoming more pronounced as map difficulty and agent count increase. By contrast, TP/TPTS and MCA/RMCA degrade sharply even in the warehouse_tiny benchmark: once the agent count surpasses 60, their task-completion curves plunge, and at 100 agents they process no more than 10% of their own peak workload. In the larger warehouse settings their performance collapses further, completing virtually no tasks at all. While FCFS completes fewer tasks than GA and CARVS, it consistently outperforms TP/TPTS and MCA/RMCA on larger maps. TP and TPTS evaluate every agent-task pair; when an agent is free, they assign the task and generate a plan using prioritized planning. Unfortunately, prioritized planning can still fail on some MAPF instances. MCA and RMCA, in turn, build a separate task heap for each task, calling the path-planning routine in the process. Because the task set changes continuously, these heaps must be rebuilt at every timestep, which is time-consuming. That’s why both TP/TPTS and MCA/RMCA scale poorly. The two remaining MAPD baselines also perform poorly on com-plex maps, although only one is limited by planning cost. TSWAP rarely exceeds the time limit because each step uses a greedy move guided by cached distances and local conflict rules rather than a joint search. Its task completion increases on the three smaller layouts but remains nearly constant on the largest one. TSWAP resolves conflicts by swapping targets rather than rerouting agents. However, TSWAP is designed for unlabeled targets. MAPD distin-guishes pickup and drop-off targets. The adapter can only restore the intended assignment in a later planning attempt after a target swap overrides it. A delivery may therefore require multiple plan-ning attempts even though each attempt is fast. With 100 robots on warehouse_large , less than 2% of successful TSWAP paths for dropping robots ended at the assigned drop-off target. This explains the low efficiency of drop-off tasks. MAPD-d fails in the opposite way. Its replanning cost grows with the agent count until it exceeds the time budget at almost every step, and task completion drops to 2 at 100 agents. Although GA outperforms FCFS, it struggles on complex maps such as warehouse_small and warehouse_large . The non-uniform task distribution leads to agent crowding in certain areas, complicat-ing path planning. Additionally, long tunnels and dynamic obstacles further reduce GA’s efficiency. Due to the lack of full integration between high-level task assignment and low-level path planning, agents often remain idle when path planning fails, reducing effi-ciency. In contrast, CARVS-V and CARVS validate goal reachability beforehand, improving the chances of solving the MAPF subprob-lem in time. The validation improvements increase task completion by at least 2x on warehouse_large with 100 agents. CARVS further refines CARVS-V by mitigating agent conges-tion near drop-off zones. GA and CARVS-V tend to select pickup locations close to drop-off points, leading to bottlenecks. CARVS circumvents this issue by randomly selecting the first pickup loca-tions, ensuring a more even agent distribution. This enhancement increases task completion by at least 1.1x on warehouse_large

## 5.2 Additional Experiment Settings 

Apart from the choice of MAPF algorithms, the performance can vary based on three factors: map layout, task distribution, and the human-robot ratio. Below, we outline the settings for each factor. 

5.2.1 Map Layout. The layout of the map significantly impacts algorithm performance. 5.2.2 Task Distribution. Temporal and spatial task distributions significantly impact algorithm performance. We collect real-world task sets from a warehouse management system, which records tasks of SKUs over time. The original task sets contain a limited number of tasks and can be easily solved by CARVS and its variants, so they cannot distinguish the performance of different algorithms. To enable a more comprehensive evaluation, we repeat each task from the original task sets 10 times to create larger task sets. Tasks are then continuously added to the task pool over time following the original distribution. The resulting spatial distribution is non-uniform, with certain areas having higher task densities than others. 

5.2.3 Human-Robot Ratio. Human interaction increases the diffi-culty of the MAPDwHC problem, and algorithm performance can be affected by the ratio of humans to robots. In our simulation, humans avoid collisions with robots and follow A* paths to reach their targets. It is important to note that this behavior is confined to the simulation; in real-world applications, humans manually update their state changes in the system via a tablet. Unlike traditional MAPD scenarios, boosting processing capacity requires scaling both the robots and the human workforce. In our experiments, we fixed the number of human operators at 50, which nearly suffices for 100 robots. Too few human operators reduce picking efficiency and lengthen the robots’ idle periods, whereas too many operators increase their own waiting times. In practice, we aim to keep human operators fully engaged, which usually means employing fewer humans than robots. ## 6 Conclusion 

This paper formalizes the Multi-Agent Pickup and Delivery with Hu-man Collaboration (MAPDwHC) problem, extending the traditional MAPD framework by integrating human agents and addressing the additional challenges introduced by human-robot interactions. Although a MAPDwHC instance reduces to a sequence of MAPD problems when no interaction occurs, our empirical results con-firm that MAPDwHC poses significantly greater computational challenges than MAPD in realistic warehouse settings. To tackle these challenges, we propose CARVS, an approach that optimizes task assignment and path planning while accounting for congestion, human-robot interactions, and real-time adaptability. We formally prove that CARVS can solve a well-formed MAPDwHC instance. Extensive simulations demonstrate that our methods out-perform existing approaches in terms of completed tasks, highlight-ing their potential for real-world applications. This work advances multi-agent systems research and lays the foundation for future op-timization of human-robot collaboration. Beyond reducing planning timeouts, our results show that congestion-aware task assignment is also important. Fast planning alone does not guarantee high task completion. CARVS deliberately avoids optimizing task assignment and path planning as one model. The joint search space of goal selection and collision-free paths is too large for the time budget of one step, and methods that couple the two, such as TP/TPTS and MCA/RMCA, break down on the complex maps. Keeping the two levels separate is what lets the system meet its real-time require-ment. Future work could focus on incorporating order distribution as a parameter into the task assignment algorithm to improve robot efficiency. Developing efficient partial-movement strategies when the planner times out is a promising direction for future work.

## A Real-world Demonstration 

Our MAPDwHC formulation is inspired by modern e-commerce ful-fillment centers that stock thousands of stock-keeping units (SKUs). Because the shelving is non-standardized and items vary widely in size, shape, and packaging, current robotic grippers cannot manipu-late every product with high reliability. As a result, human workers perform the pick and pack operations, while fleets of mobile robots ferry plastic totes between storage aisles and packing stations. Each robot is equipped with on-board LiDAR, localizes itself using SLAM, and continuously transmits its position to a central planner. The robots reliably detect and avoid nearby humans, and workers can easily step through the gap between a robot and the shelving; hence collisions between humans and robots are negligible. Accordingly, our MAPDwHC model assumes that human-robot collisions do not occur. The photographs in Figures 5 and 6 highlight the practical con-straints shaping our design: narrow aisles, close human-robot inter-action, and limited payload capacity. Collectively, these challenges motivate the fast, reactive algorithms detailed in the main paper.

## D Details of Compared Algorithms 

We modified TP/TPTS and RMCA/MCA so that they operate under our MAPDwHC assumptions while preserving their original deci-sion logic. First, TP/TPTS now runs in an online manner: at every time step it returns a partial plan that ends when the first task of the current task sets finishes, rather than waiting until all tasks are completed. The simulator verifies this plan, manages the pick-up and drop-off states, and injects the drop-off vertices. Each drop-off task carries an agent-specific ID to prevent reassignment. RMCA/MCA constructs a heap of all tasks and agents at the start of the simulation before producing the paths. We trigger a global replan as soon as any agent reaches its goal. Drop-off locations are also pre-assigned to the corresponding agents and treated as exclusive goals. Whenever an agent engages in human interaction its current vertex is marked temporarily as an obstacle. Our simulation re-moves that agent from the active set and alters the graph supplied to the solver. Any change in task status or human-agent interaction immediately triggers a new round of task allocation and path plan-ning, ensuring that the planners face the same dynamic conditions as CARVS and the other algorithms. These adaptations allow TP, TPTS, RMCA, and MCA to be compared fairly with the proposed methods in realistic warehouse scenarios that involve human-robot collaboration. TSWAP was originally designed for simultaneous assignment to unlabeled targets. In our MAPD setting, any robot can be as-signed to a pickup target, but a robot carrying an item must reach its assigned drop-off target. To apply TSWAP in this setting, the wrapper supplies current pickup targets to picking robots and as-signed drop-off targets to dropping robots when constructing each planning instance. Each drop-off target carries the corresponding agent index, so the initial target allocation preserves the assignment. However, TSWAP handles conflicts by swapping targets and breaks deadlocks by rotating them. These operations change existing task assignments, so TSWAP cannot maintain a fixed assignment be-tween a robot and its target during execution. A dropping robot may therefore be redirected toward another drop-off target or a pickup target. The wrapper can only reapply the original assignment in a later planning attempt. Diagnostic results on warehouse_large with 100 robots show how these target exchanges affect task completion. Only 0.73% of successful paths for dropping robots ended at the assigned drop-off target. Instead, 44.94% ended at another robot’s drop-off target and 54.33% at a pickup target. Neither pathfinding failures nor solver execution errors were observed. Robots spent an average of 45.92% of their time in the drop-off phase. The median duration of a completed drop-off averaged 728 steps across runs. When the simulation ended, an average of 48.6 robots had not completed their drop-offs. These results show that fast planning and a low skip count do not guarantee efficient task completion. MAPD-d exposes a robustness parameter 𝑘 , which stands for the number of delay steps an agent must absorb without replanning. However, our setting provides no reliable value for it. Although the interaction time is set to 10 time steps in the simulations, the picker still needs time to walk to the picking point, and that walking time varies with the distance. So the delay an agent experiences is unknown in advance. We therefore report 𝑘 = 0 in the main results. We also ran 𝑘 = 10 for comparison. On warehouse_small

## F Data and Ethics Statement 

The warehouse layouts, task records, and facility photographs used in this work were obtained through an industrial collaboration. The study involves no human subjects and no personally identifiable information. The warehouse-management-system records consist solely of stock-keeping-unit (SKU) task events over time rather than data about individuals. Each SKU appears only as an index number that distinguishes one item from another, not as its real identifier. The task sets used in our experiments are aggregate temporal dis-tributions together with synthetically generated tasks derived from those distributions, not raw operational records. The photographs contain no people and no product information, because the product imagery in them has been pixelated. No personally identifiable information was collected, processed, or released as part of this work.
