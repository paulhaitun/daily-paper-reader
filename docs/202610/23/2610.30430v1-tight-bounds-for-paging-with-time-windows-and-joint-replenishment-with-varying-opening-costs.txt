Abstract 

We study the paging with time windows problem, a natural generalization of classical paging, in which each request must be served within a specified time interval. This model allows the algorithm to delay service and batch service requests over time, but also introduces significant challenges due to overlapping requests. While the problem is well understood in the unweighted setting, where optimal O(log k)-competitive algorithms are known, the weighted case remains more elusive. The best previously known algorithm, due to Gupta, Kumar, and Panigrahi [20], achieves an O(log k log n)-competitive ratio, leaving a gap between weighted and unweighted settings. We obtain an O(log k)-competitive algorithm for weighted paging with time windows, matching the optimal competitive ratio. The core of our approach is a structural decomposition that separates the evolution of the cache from the scheduling of page loads. We show that, given a sequence of cache configurations, computing a near-optimal sequence of page loads reduces to a variant of the Joint Replenishment Problem with deadlines (JRP-D) with time-dependent opening costs. We design a constant-competitive online algorithm for this variant and prove that the reduction is tight. Combining this with an existing online construction of cache configurations yields the desired bound.

In the classical weighted paging problem there is a universe of n pages, where each page p has a weight wp, and there is a cache that can hold up to k pages. At each time step, a page is requested, and if the requested page is already in the cache then no cost is incurred, otherwise, the page must be loaded into the cache, incurring a cost equal to its weight. The goal is to minimize the total cost incurred. In the unweighted version of the problem, all weights are equal (unit weights). Paging is one of the earliest and most extensively studied problems in online computation and competitive analysis [26, 18, 27, 29, 25, 6, 5, 1, 2, 10, 9, 21, 22, 23], including works on non-standard caching models, e.g., elastic caches [19], caching with time windows [20], caching with dynamic weights [16], and caching with machine learning predictions [24]. In fact, online paging has become a focal point for many of the recent developments in competitive analysis, e.g., the online primal-dual method, projections, and mirror descent [14, 12, 11]. In their seminal paper, Sleator and Tarjan [26] showed that any deterministic online algorithm is at least k-competitive and that the LRU policy (Least Recently Used) is exactly k-competitive for unweighted paging. The k-competitive bound was later generalized to weighted paging as well [15, 28]. When randomization is allowed, Fiat et al. [18] gave the elegant randomized marking algorithm for unweighted paging, which is Θ(log k)-competitive against an oblivious adversary. Bansal et al. [6] gave a Θ(log k)-competitive randomized algorithm for weighted paging based on the online primal-dual framework [14]. We now elaborate on the two problems on which we focus in the paper. 

Paging with time windows. A natural and well-studied generalization is paging with time windows , where each request must be served within a specified interval. Formally, a request r is represented by a triple r = ( p(r), s (r), e (r)), where p(r) ∈ P is the requested page, s(r) is the release time, and e(r) ≥ s(r) is the deadline for serving it. A request r is satisfied if page p(r) is present in the cache at some time during the interval [ s(r), e (r)]. This model significantly increases the flexibility of any paging algorithm, allowing it to delay service, while batching together the service of several requests with overlapping time windows. At the same time, it introduces new challenges, as requests may overlap and interact in complex ways. In the unweighted setting, Azar et al. [4] showed that paging with time windows admits an 

O(k)-competitive deterministic algorithm and an O(log k)-competitive randomized algorithm. The latter matches the optimal randomized guarantee for classical paging up to constant factors. At a high level, the randomized algorithm delays loading a page until the deadline of one of its pending requests, thereby batching together overlapping requests for the same page. However, in the weighted setting, the authors of [4] showed that this structural approach no longer holds, although the Ω( k) lower bound for deterministic algorithms continues to hold. Gupta et al. [20] subsequently introduced a novel LP formulation based on sophisticated covering constraints. They showed that paging with time windows is NP-hard and APX-hard even when 

k = 1 and all pages have unit weight. Nevertheless, their LP has a constant integrality gap, yielding an O(1)-approximation algorithm for the problem. In the online setting, they obtained an O(log n log k)-competitive algorithm for weighted paging with time windows, which remains the best known bound to date. Gupta et al. [20] also considered the more general problem of paging with delays . Here, each request r is represented by a triple r = ( p(r), s (r), F r), where p(r) is the requested page, s(r) is the release time, and Fr : {s(r), s (r) + 1 , . . . } → R≥0 is a non-decreasing delay-loss function associated with r. If request r is served at time t ≥ s(r), the algorithm incurs delay cost Fr(t). The objective is to minimize the total weight of the loaded pages plus the total delay costs incurred by all requests. 1r 12 1 2 3 47 6 5 1

Joint Replenishment Problem. A well-studied problem that turns out to be closely related to paging with time windows is the Joint Replenishment Problem with deadlines (JRP-D). The problem can be viewed as a depth-2 tree (see Figure 1), consisting of a warehouse at the root and a single leaf for each item, with a bijection between items and leaves. Requests for items arrive over time and an item can be requested multiple times. At any time t, an order can be placed by opening the warehouse and selecting a subset of the leaves with pending requests for items. A request for an item is served at time t if its corresponding leaf is included in the order. Opening the warehouse incurs a fixed cost, while serving requests for item i incurs an additional item-specific cost wi.Requests for items must be served within prescribed time intervals. Equivalently, serving a request after its deadline incurs infinite cost. An algorithm may place an order at any time and use it to serve multiple pending requests simultaneously. Thus, the main trade-off is between serving requests immediately vs. delaying service to batch multiple requests together. While the opening cost may be smaller than the item costs in general, the most interesting regime is when the opening cost is large, making it advantageous to postpone service and aggregate requests belonging to different items into a single order. For this problem, Bienkowski et al. [8] gave a 2-competitive online algorithm, matching the optimal competitive ratio. JRP-D is a special case of the more general Joint Replenishment Problem (JRP), in which requests incur arbitrary delay costs while awaiting service, rather than hard deadlines. JRP and its variants have been extensively studied in both offline [3, 7] and online settings. In particular, Buchbinder et al. [13] showed that JRP admits a 3-competitive online algorithm. In this work, we study a variant of JRP-D with time-dependent warehouse opening costs. Unlike the classical setting, the algorithm must account not only for the possibility of batching requests, but also for changes in the cost of placing an order. Although time-dependent opening costs have previously been investigated experimentally [17], we examine this setting from the perspective of online competitive analysis. As we show, paging with time windows admits a natural reduction to this JRP-D variant. Throughout the paper, when discussing randomized algorithms, all costs and competitive guarantees are in expectation, unless stated otherwise. 21.1 Our contribution 

Our first contribution is a new formalism for representing solutions to paging with time windows. We represent a solution as a pair ( C, D ), where C = ( Ct) denotes the sequence of cache configurations at the end of each time step t, and D = ( Dt) denotes the sequence of page loads performed during each time step t. This decomposition cleanly separates the evolution of the cache over time from the scheduling of page loads used to satisfy requests that are not served by the cache configurations in C. In particular, an algorithm may load a page during a time step solely to satisfy pending requests and then immediately evict it. Consequently, a page may belong to Dt without belonging to Ct. Such page loads contribute to the cost of the solution, but are not reflected in the cache configurations. This representation is purely conceptual: it does not alter the objective function, but rather decomposes a paging solution into two components, corresponding to cache evolution and page-load scheduling. Given a request sequence R, let cost R(C, D ) denote the cost of the solution represented by a feasible pair ( C, D ). We define cost R(C) = min D cost R(C, D ), where the minimum is taken over all completions D of C that are feasible for R. When the request sequence is clear from context, we omit the subscript R and write cost (C, D ) and cost (C). Furthermore, we show that the online cache-configuration construction implicit in [20] yields a cache trajectory C, i.e., a sequence of cache configurations, satisfying 

cost (C) = O(log k) · OPT , (11) where OPT denotes the cost of an optimal offline solution to paging with time windows. Our second contribution is to isolate the completion problem as a problem in its own right. In the completion problem, the sequence of cache configurations C is fixed, but revealed online, and the goal is to compute a feasible minimum-cost completion of C. We show that the completion problem can be formulated as an instance of JRP with deadlines (JRP-D) with time-dependent opening costs, i.e., opening costs can vary over time. The varying opening costs make this problem fundamentally harder than classical JRP-D, as without additional information about the future no bounded competitive ratio is possible. To get around this difficulty, we introduce a prediction-augmented model in which the algorithm receives limited information regarding future opening costs. Specifically, for each request that has been released but not yet served, which we call an active request , the prediction identifies the cheapest opening opportunity before its deadline. In fact, we show that an even weaker prediction suffices to obtain a constant-competitive online algorithm. 

Theorem 1.1. There exists a constant-competitive online algorithm for JRP-D with time-dependent opening costs under our prediction model. 

Our next contribution is to show that the prediction required by the JRP-D algorithm is naturally available in the reduction we provide from paging with time windows. In fact, the prediction used by the JRP-D algorithm in this case is simply the weight of the page whose pending request has the earliest deadline. Consequently, given any sequence of cache configurations C revealed online, our algorithm computes online a completion D satisfying 

cost (C, D ) = O(cost (C)) .

1.2 Technical overview 

Separating cache configurations from page loads. A key contribution of Gupta et al. [20] is a novel LP formulation for paging with time windows based on sophisticated covering constraints (see Section 2.2 for details). Their framework proceeds in two stages. First, they compute an online fractional solution to their LP and round it to an integral solution. This rounding incurs only an 

O(1) loss in the offline setting, but an O(log k) loss online. The resulting integral solution, however, is not necessarily a feasible solution. In the second stage, they convert it into a feasible paging solution. This conversion incurs only an additional O(1) loss offline, yet an additional O(log n) loss online, resulting in an O(log k log n)-competitive algorithm. Although this distinction is not made explicit in [20], a closer examination of [20] reveals that both online and offline conversion algorithms produce the exact same cache configuration at the end of every time step. The only difference lies in how they schedule the “extra” page loads used to satisfy pending requests. This raises a natural question: if both algorithms maintain the same cache configuration at the end of every time step, how does the additional O(log n) factor arise? The answer is that in contrast to classical paging, the cache configurations alone do not fully determine the paging solution. In classical paging, one may assume without loss of generality that the only page loaded at each time step is the requested page, which then remains in cache. Consequently, if Ct denotes the cache configuration at time t, then the paging cost incurred at time 

t is exactly W (Ct \ Ct−1), where W (S) denotes the total weight of the pages in a set S. Thus, in the classical setting, the sequence of cache configurations completely determines the solution cost. In paging with time windows, however, this is no longer the case. Since requests may be delayed and then batched together, an algorithm may load several pages simultaneously at a single time step. Moreover, a page may be loaded only to satisfy a pending request and then evicted immediately, without ever appearing in the cache configurations. As a consequence, the quantity 

W (Ct \ Ct−1) may be strictly smaller than the actual cost incurred at time t, and the sequence of cache configurations by itself no longer captures all load operations performed by an algorithm. This distinction motivates our ( C, D ) formalism. The cache trajectory records only the pages present at the end of each time step, whereas the completion records all page loads used to serve the request sequence, including (i) pages that are loaded and immediately evicted; and (ii) page loads that are implied by the changes in the cache configurations. Recall that cost (C, D ) denotes the cost of the solution represented by ( C, D ), and that cost (C) is the minimum cost over all completions of 

C. Thus, two solutions may induce the same cache trajectory C while using different completions and incurring different costs. Although Gupta et al. [20] do not explicitly introduce this decomposition, it is implicit in their analysis. Through the lens of our formalism, their online conversion algorithm takes an integral solution and constructs a pair ( C, D ) whose cost is within an O(log n) factor of that integral solution. 4At the same time, the same cache trajectory C does admit (offline) a completion D′ such that 

cost (C, D ′) is within an O(1) factor of the same integral solution. Thus, the additional O(log n)factor arises entirely from the online construction of D, rather than the cache trajectory C itself. We formalize this reinterpretation in Section 2.4. This perspective suggests focusing exclusively on the completion problem. Our goal is therefore the following. Given a sequence of cache configurations C revealed online, compute online a completion D such that cost (C, D ) = O(cost (C)).

Thus, every such request naturally induces a covering constraint over its time window. The second key observation concerns the cost of loading a set of pages while following a fixed cache trajectory C. ot := min {wp | p ∈ Ct} .

The JRP formulation may therefore charge slightly more than the corresponding paging load: at times when the cache changes, the opening cost is charged separately even though the cache transition itself already provides the required loading opportunity. Each page corresponds to an item, represented by a leaf in the JRP tree (see Figure 1). The cost of placing an order consists of the opening cost ot together with the costs of the items served by the order. We show that this reduction faithfully captures the completion problem. # 2 Our Model and Approach 

2.1 Paging with Time Windows 

In the paging with time windows problem, there is a universe of n pages, denoted by P =

{p1, p 2, . . . , p n}. Each page p ∈ P is associated with a positive weight wp, and there is a cache of size k. The system evolves over discrete time steps t = 1 , 2, . . . , T . At each time t, the cache stores a subset of pages Ct ⊆ P , referred to as the cache configuration at time t. Without loss of generality, we assume that |Ct| = k at every time t, since there is no benefit to evicting a page unless another page is loaded in its place. 

Requests. Requests arrive over time. Each request r = ( p(r), s (r), e (r)) specifies a page p(r), a release time s(r), and a deadline e(r), defining a time window [ s(r), e (r)]. In the online setting, a request r becomes known only at time s(r). A request r is served if the page p(r) is present in the cache at some time t ∈ [s(r), e (r)]. Note that pages can be loaded and evicted at the same time step, thus allowing for multiple requests to be served simultaneously. 

Cost. The cost of an algorithm is the total cost of all page loads over the time horizon. Loading a page p into the cache incurs cost wp, while evictions are free. Some prior work instead charges for page evictions; the two cost measures are equivalent up to an additive term determined by the initial and final cache configurations. The objective is to minimize the total cost while serving all requests. The classical paging problem corresponds to the special case in which every request satisfies 

s(r) = e(r). 

2.2 Prior Work: Gupta et al. [20] 

The main difficulty in extending classical paging to the time-window setting arises from limitations of standard linear programming (LP) relaxations. In classical weighted paging, a request for page p

at time t requires the page to reside in cache at time t. In contrast, in paging with time windows, a request r only requires that p(r) appear in the cache at some time during the interval [ s(r), e (r)]. A natural LP relaxation would therefore require that the total fractional residency of page p(r)over the interval [ s(r), e (r)] be at least 1. However, Gupta et al. [20] showed that this formulation suffers from an integrality gap of Ω( k). Intuitively, such a fractional solution can satisfy a request by keeping a small fraction of the page in cache throughout the entire window, while an integral solution must eventually pay the full cost wp.7To overcome this issue, Gupta et al. [20] introduced a fundamentally different LP formulation based on sophisticated covering constraints, which they call the hitting-set LP. For each page p and time t, the variable xp,t ∈ [0 , 1] represents the extent to which page p is evicted at time t.At a high level, the LP guarantees cache capacity through covering constraints over time intervals. Informally, if an interval I contains requests to k + 1 distinct pages, then the cache cannot simultaneously retain all of them throughout I, and therefore at least one of these pages must be evicted during the interval. The LP utilizes this observation by requiring that every such pair ( I, S ), where S is a set of k + 1 pages requested during I, be “hit” by an eviction within the time interval of one of the pages in S.In the standard LP formulations for paging, the LP directly specifies which pages reside in cache at each time step. In contrast, the hitting-set LP only determines when pages are evicted, and does not explicitly specify how requests are served. Consequently, an integral solution to the hitting-set LP does not immediately define a feasible paging schedule. Solving paging with time windows therefore requires addressing two separate challenges: first, rounding the fractional LP solution online, and second, transforming the resulting eviction schedule into a feasible paging solution. Gupta et al. [20] showed that the hitting-set LP admits an online rounding procedure with an 

O(log k) loss and an offline rounding procedure with only an O(1) loss, both relative to the optimal fractional solution. Their approach exploits the interval structure of the constraints and refines the primal-dual techniques developed by Bansal et al. [14] for the classical weighted paging LP. To bridge the gap between the rounded eviction schedule and a feasible paging solution, Gupta et al. [20] introduced an additional scheduling layer. At each time step t, the algorithm determines whether a page must be loaded into the cache. Before updating the cache configuration, the algorithm may also perform additional “load-and-evict” operations: pages are loaded in order to satisfy requests and are evicted within the same time step, thus not appearing in Ct.Implementing this scheduling layer is precisely what separates the online and offline guarantees. The offline algorithm uses future knowledge of the cache trajectory to coordinate these “load-and-evict” operations more efficiently, achieving only an O(1) loss relative to the rounded hitting-set LP solution. In contrast, the online algorithm incurs an additional O(log n) factor, resulting in an overall O(log k log n)-competitive ratio. This gap is the starting point of our work. It suggests that the remaining difficulty in obtaining an optimal online algorithm lies not in constructing the cache trajectory itself, but in scheduling the additional page loads needed to satisfy requests. 

2.3 Our Approach 

The discussion above suggests that paging with time windows can be naturally decomposed into two tasks: determining how the cache evolves over time, and then deciding which additional pages to load in order to serve unsatisfied requests. While these two tasks are intertwined in the online algorithm of Gupta et al. [20], their offline algorithm suggests that they can be partially decoupled: after the cache configurations are determined, a final scheduling step is used to complete the solution by specifying the additional page loads. Our approach is to formalize this separation. Intuitively, the cache trajectory captures the structural decisions of the algorithm, while the loading schedule determines how requests are served. The cache trajectory is not known in advance, but is revealed online one configuration at a time. Given this trajectory, the remaining task is to construct a loading schedule online that satisfies all requests at minimum cost. At first glance, this is an interval-covering problem: each request induces a time interval during which its page must be loaded, and loading a page can simultaneously cover multiple overlapping 8intervals. However, the cache trajectory makes loading pages at certain times inherently more expensive than at other times, as a load may require an additional page to be evicted and then reloaded in order to preserve the given cache configuration. This creates a batching trade-off analogous to that in JRP-D, where serving a set of items requires paying an opening cost (of the warehouse) in addition to the item costs. 

Solutions. We represent a solution using two components. Let C = ( Ct)Tt=0 denote a sequence of cache configurations, where Ct ⊆ P is the set of pages in the cache at the end of time t. Let 

D = ( Dt)Tt=1 denote a sequence of page loads, where Dt ⊆ P is the set of pages loaded at time t. A page belongs to Dt whenever it is loaded at time t, i.e., if it is reloaded at the same time step that it is evicted, or if it is immediately evicted after it is loaded. 

Feasibility. A pair ( C, D ) is a feasible solution if it satisfies the following conditions: 

• Capacity. For every time t, |Ct| = k.

• Consistency. For every time t, Ct \ Ct−1 ⊆ Dt. This implies that, for any page p, if there exist times t1 < t 2 such that p / ∈ Ct1 but p ∈ Ct2 , then there exists a time t′ ∈ (t1, t 2] such that 

p ∈ Dt′ .

• Non-degeneracy. For every time t, if Dt̸ = ∅, then Dt ∩ Ct̸ = ∅. That is, whenever pages are loaded at time t, at least one of the loaded pages remains in cache at the end of the time step. For example, if Ct = Ct−1 and a page p ∈ Ct is temporarily evicted to create space and subsequently reloaded, then p ∈ Dt.

• Service. For every request r, the requested page p(r) must appear in the cache at some time during the interval [ s(r), e (r)]. 2.4 A Reinterpretation of Gupta et al. [20] 

The separation between cache configurations and page loads allows us to reinterpret the guarantees of Gupta et al. [20] in terms of the quantity cost R(C). Consider any integral solution to their 9hitting-set LP whose objective value is M , and let C be the cache trajectory obtained online from this solution. They provide an online conversion procedure that builds on the cache trajectory and yields a feasible solution. (To this end, recall that it is allowed to load a page p and then immediately evict it, so that p does not appear in the cache configuration at the end of the time step.) They also provide an offline conversion procedure that optimizes the online solution by considering for each page p the times at which such load-and-evict operations occur and removing some of these loads, while preserving feasibility. Since only loads of pages that do not remain in cache are removed, the cache trajectory C remains unchanged. The resulting offline completion D computed by [20] is feasible and satisfies cost R(C, D ) = 

O(M ). Since cost R(C) is defined as the minimum cost of any completion of C, it follows that 

cost R(C) ≤ cost R(C, D ) = O(M ). Thus, every integral LP solution naturally induces a cache trajectory whose completion cost is within a constant factor of the LP objective value. Gupta et al. [20] further showed how to construct online an integral solution whose objective value satisfies M = O(log k) · OPT , where OPT denotes the cost of an optimal offline solution to paging with time windows. We combine the two observations and get the following. 

Lemma 2.1. There exists an online algorithm that constructs a cache trajectory C satisfying 

cost R(C) = O(log k) · OPT. 

Lemma 2.1 shows that an O(log k)-competitive algorithm for paging with time windows can be obtained by completing the cache trajectory C produced online by Gupta et al. [20]. Thus, it remains to solve the completion problem online: at each time t, the algorithm observes Ct and computes a completion D, satisfying cost R(C, D ) = O(cost R(C)). 

2.5 Joint Replenishment Problem (JRP) 

To formulate the completion problem as a replenishment problem, we introduce a variant of JRP that will be central to our reduction. Joint replenishment is defined over a discrete time horizon 

t = 1 , 2, . . . , T and a set of items Y , where each item i ∈ Y has cost wi. In our setting, items correspond to pages. Each request r is specified by a triple ( i(r), s (r), h r), where i(r) ∈ Y is the requested item, s(r)is the release time, and hr(t) is the delay cost incurred if r is served at time t ≥ s(r). A solution consists of choosing, for each time t, a set of items Bt ⊆ Y to be served at time t. If 

Bt̸ = ∅, we say that an order is placed at time t. A request r is served at the earliest time t ≥ s(r)such that i(r) ∈ Bt. The cost of a solution has three components: 

• Opening cost. For each time t with Bt̸ = ∅, we pay a cost o for opening the warehouse. 

• Item cost. For each time t and each item i ∈ Bt, we pay wi.

• Delay cost. For each request r served at time t, we pay hr(t). The objective is to minimize the total cost.

JRP-D with time-dependent opening costs. We consider a variant of JRP-D in which the fixed opening cost o is replaced by a time-dependent opening cost ot. This additional flexibility makes the problem substantially harder in the online setting. Consider an instance with a single item i of cost wi = 0 and a single request r = ( i, 1, 2). Let the opening cost at time 1 be o1 = M .At time 2, the opening cost is either o2 = 1 or o2 = M 2, and its value is not known to the online algorithm at time 1. If the algorithm waits until time 2, then when o2 = M 2 it pays M 2, while the optimum pays M

We next specify which prediction values are available to the algorithm. A request is active at time t if it has arrived, but has not yet been served, and an item is active if it has an active request. During the construction of an order, requests for items already committed to that order are treated as inactive for subsequent prediction queries. All committed items must be served by the order placed at the same time step. The algorithm has access to eP (r, t ) only when r has the earliest deadline among the requests active at that point. Thus, committing items to an order may make predictions for other active requests available. 

# 3 A Reduction for Completing Cache Configurations 

We study here the completion problem: given a sequence of cache configurations C, what is the minimum cost of completing it into a feasible paging solution? We show that this problem is captured, up to a constant factor, by a suitable instance J(C) of the JRP-D variant introduced in Section 2.5. We prove that the reduction preserves the completion problem up to a constant factor. First, any completion of C induces a feasible solution to J(C) with comparable cost. Second, any solution to J(C) can be transformed online into a feasible completion of C with no asymptotic loss. Consequently, J(C) characterizes cost (C) up to a constant factor.

4.1 Algorithm 

For an active item i, we refer to the deadline of its earliest-deadline active request as the active deadline of i. At each time t, the algorithm constructs a set Xt of items and places an order for Xt

if it is nonempty. The algorithm consists of two stages. Stage I initiates an order either to meet a deadline or based on a prediction of future opening costs. It then greedily adds active items as long as the total cost of Xt does not exceed the opening cost ot. Stage II uses predictions to determine which additional active items to include. Throughout both stages, the algorithm maintains and processes the active items in nondecreasing order of their active deadlines, breaking ties by nonincreasing item cost and then by item index. The algorithm performs the following at each time t:

Stage I: Deadline items and greedy batching. The algorithm initializes 

Xt = {i : the active deadline of i is t}.

If Xt = ∅, let r be the first active request in the maintained order. If no active request exists, or if 

eP (r, t ) ≤ ot,

Stage II: Prediction-based orders. The algorithm continues with the set Xt constructed in Stage I and considers the remaining active items in the maintained order. For each such item i, let 

r be its earliest-deadline active request. # A Extension to Paging with Delays 

We explain how our framework extends to the paging-with-delays model of Gupta et al. [20]. Gupta et al. show that it suffices to consider a penalty version of paging with time windows. In this variant, each request has a time window and may either be served within its window or left unserved by paying a request-specific penalty. They further show that their analysis for paging with time windows extends to this penalty model. We follow the same approach. In paging with time windows and penalties, each request r is specified by a page p(r), a release time s(r), a deadline e(r), and a penalty πr. A solution may either serve r during [ s(r), e (r)] or pay the penalty πr. The objective is to minimize the sum of the costs and the penalties of all unserved requests. Fix an integral solution to the hitting-set LP of Gupta et al. [20] with objective value X. Recall that their conversion procedure (see Section 2.4) transforms an integral LP solution into a paging with time windows solution. The solution consists of two components: the cache trajectory C = ( Ct)and the sequence of load-and-evict sets D = ( Dt). We now use both components to partition online the requests into two sets, R1 and R2. If a request r is not served (a penalty is paid), we add r

to R1. Otherwise, let t be the first time at which the online conversion procedure serves r, either through cache residency or by loading p(r), and add the page request ( p(r), t, e (r)) to R2. Since the partition is determined by the online conversion procedure, both R1 and R2 can be constructed online. We first bound the completion cost of C with respect to R2. Gupta et al. showed that the offline conversion procedure preserves the cache trajectory C and only removes some of the “load-and-evict” steps (determined online). Nevertheless, it remains feasible for every request served by the online procedure and therefore serves every request in R2. Since its total cost is O(X), it follows that 

cost R2 (C) = O(X).

Moreover, every request in R1 is left unserved by the online conversion procedure. Since the offline procedure only removes loads, these requests remain unserved, and the offline procedure pays their penalties. Therefore, X

Lemma B.1. Let S be a feasible paging solution, and let Q be a nonempty set of requests active at time t1, all with deadline t2 > t 1. Suppose that no active or unrevealed request outside Q has deadline at most t2. Then S can be modified, without increasing its cost, so that p(r) ∈ Ct2 for some 

r ∈ Q. The modification can be implemented online whenever S is generated online. Proof. Follow S until all requests in Q have been served. Let r be the last request in Q to be served, and let K be the cache configuration immediately after this service. In particular, this configuration contains p(r). At this point, all requests with deadline at most t2 have been served. Indeed, every request in Q

has been served, while every request outside Q with such a deadline was already served at time t1,by assumption. Postpone all subsequent loadings and evictions through the end of time t2 to the beginning of time t2 + 1. Execute these operations in their original order, before the operations originally scheduled at that time. This preserves feasibility: any request whose service is postponed and that has not already been served has deadline at least t2 + 1. Capacity is preserved because the postponed operations start from the same configuration K and are executed in the same order. No additional loads are introduced, so the cost does not increase. If t2 is the final time step, the postponed operations can instead be omitted. The configuration K persists through time t2, so p(r) ∈ Ct2 .To implement the modification online, buffer the postponed operations. If a new request with deadline at most t2 arrives, execute the buffered operations before processing it and resume S. This preserves feasibility even when the hypothesis of the lemma does not hold. Under the hypothesis, no such request arrives, so the configuration K persists until the end of time t2, as required. □
