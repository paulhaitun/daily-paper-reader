# CQTwin: A Digital-Twin Toolkit for Operational Analytics of HPC Schedulers 

Kanglin Xu ∗, Yash Kurkure ∗, Yihe Zhang ∗, Michael E. Papka †∗ , Zhiling Lan ∗† ∗Department of Computer Science, University of Illinois Chicago, Chicago, IL, USA 

†Argonne National Laboratory, Lemont, IL, USA 

Abstract —HPC facilities collect years of job scheduler records, yet much of this operational data remains underused because accessible tools for turning it into actionable insight are scarce. We present CQTwin, an open-source digital-twin toolkit for operational analytics of HPC scheduling data. CQTwin addresses this gap through two complementary modes: offline trace replay and online event-driven simulation. Built on the Python-based CQSim discrete-event simulator, CQTwin combines a fidelity-validated, high-performance C++ engine with an interactive browser-based dashboard and a Snapshot Lab, enabling facility staff, operators, and researchers to characterize workloads, compare scheduling policies, and explore what-if scenarios. As an offline twin, CQTwin replays archived workload traces under alternative policies to show how the same workload would have unfolded under different scheduling decisions. As an online twin, it consumes job events as they arrive and projects how alternative policies would process the currently queued jobs, providing guid-ance for real-time decision-making. The dashboard supports the complete analysis workflow, including workload characterization, experiment configuration and monitoring, and multidimensional comparison of simulation results, allowing users to determine visually which policies best suit a workload and what trade-offs they entail. We validate CQTwin using production traces from the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility spanning 2016–2023. 

Index Terms —operational data analytics, batch scheduling, digital twin, discrete-event simulation, workload analysis 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

High-performance computing (HPC) facilities continuously generate rich streams of operational data, including system logs, hardware telemetry, and job logs. Among these sources, scheduler records provide a particularly comprehensive view of facility operation: the lifecycle of every submitted job, how users request resources, how workloads evolve over time, and how scheduling decisions affect wait time, utilization, and fairness [1]. Public archives of production workload traces have therefore become a foundation for reproducible workload characterization and scheduling research [2], [3]. A persistent gap, however, separates collecting scheduler data from acting on it. Operations teams may accumulate years of scheduler telemetry, yet lack accessible tools for translating those records into answers to practical policy questions. These questions generally require two distinct modes of analysis. Some are retrospective: What would the average wait time have been under a different priority policy? Would another backfilling strategy improve fairness at the cost of utilization? 

These are offline questions: the full trace is known, a candidate policy can be replayed from beginning to end, and their aggre-gate outcomes can be compared. Other questions arise during operation: Given the jobs queued and running now, which policy is likely to clear the backlog fastest? Should the policy change as the workload shifts? These are online questions: jobs become known only when they arrive, and decisions must be evaluated without access to future submissions. A digital twin of the batch scheduler can support both forms of analysis. Offline, the twin can replay historical workloads under counterfactual policies without disrupting production. Online, it can mirror the scheduler-visible state and project the near-term consequences of alternative decisions before they are committed. Together, these modes can turn scheduler records from passive archives into an operational decision-support resource. Turning scheduler data into operational insight, however, remains harder than it should be. First, existing open-source trace simulators [4]–[8] are primarily programming frame-works that demand simulation expertise to set up experiments and compare outcomes (§II). Support for online decision-making without future-workload knowledge is very limited. Moreover, the surrounding workflow, from ingesting scheduler records to configuring experiments and comparing outcomes, is rarely exposed through an interface designed for facility staff. The resulting gap is not simply a lack of simulation methods, but a lack of operationally usable analytics that con-nects those methods to day-to-day facility decisions. Closing this gap is a central objective of this work. We present CQTwin , an open-source toolkit that provides offline and online digital twins for HPC schedulers, together with an interactive dashboard, that turns operational schedul-ing data into actionable insights for facility staff, operators, and researchers. Rather than building a simulator from scratch, CQTwin builds on CQSim [8]–[11], a Python-based, open-source discrete-event simulator refined through more than a decade of trace-driven scheduling research. CQTwin pre-serves CQSim’s scheduling semantics, while adding a high-performance C++ discrete-event engine, an online event-streaming interface, and a browser-based environment for operational analysis. 

Offline twin: trace replay. In offline mode, CQTwin re-plays a complete production trace by submitting each job at its recorded arrival time and allowing a selected scheduling policy and backfilling strategy to determine when that job starts. This replay reveals how the same workload would have unfolded under a policy different from the one used in production. Users can compare per-job wait times, slowdown, system utilization,

Online twin: real-time decision support. In online mode, jobs enter the twin only as their arrival events are received. At each scheduling cycle, the twin therefore contains exactly the information that would be visible to a live scheduler. From this state, CQTwin can generate projections showing how each policy in a user-selected pool would process the current workload. It can also expose scheduling control to an external agent, enabling learning-based or adaptive policy selection [12]. 

Visual dashboard: analysis without scripting. CQTwin in-tegrates these capabilities into a browser-based dashboard that supports the complete analysis workflow. Users can load a job trace in the Standard Workload Format (SWF) [13], examine job sizes, runtimes, arrival patterns, and queue pressure, and then configure and launch simulations through a web form. Completed runs are summarized through interactive plots and tables, including wait-time distributions, utilization timelines, and side-by-side policy comparisons, without requiring post-processing scripts. Moreover, the dashboard includes a Snap-shot Lab for state-level what-if analysis: users can freeze a simulated system state, inspect its queued and running jobs, and replay counterfactual interventions such as policy switches or planned system downtime. Together, these components support an end-to-end opera-tional workflow. For example, an operator observing rising wait times can load a recent trace, compare candidate schedul-ing policies, and inspect the resulting tradeoffs directly in the dashboard. The offline twin shows how each policy would have performed over the observed workload, while the online twin reveals, cycle by cycle and without hindsight, when an alternative policy would have processed the scheduler-visible workload more effectively. Analyses that previously required simulator expertise, custom scripts, and separate visualization pipelines can therefore be completed within a single interactive environment. We validate CQTwin using production workload traces from the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility (ALCF) span-ning 2016–2023. The C++ engine exactly reproduces Python CQSim’s per-job wait times, with zero divergence across 50,000 jobs under five scheduling policies. This equivalence extends through the online path: a stream-fed online run exactly reproduces trace-driven replay across the complete 147,513-job Polaris-2023 trace, establishing an end-to-end fidelity chain from the event stream to the validated reference implementation. The engine replays a year-long production trace in 2.3 seconds, achieving up to a 24 × speedup over the original Python-based CQSim. Per-cycle projections add approximately 0.66 ms of overhead while leaving the commit-ted schedule unchanged, and a greedy controller demonstrates end-to-end external policy control by switching the committed policy 3,084 times during a year-long run through the Gym-style interface. II. R ELATED WORK 

Trace-driven scheduling simulation. Production workload traces allow researchers to hold the workload fixed while varying the scheduling policy. By replaying these traces through simulators, prior studies have evaluated FCFS and backfilling behavior [14], fault-aware utility scheduling [8], energy-aware scheduling [9], multi-resource allocation [10], and reinforcement-learning-based dispatch [15], [16]. Several open-source trace simulators have served as ex-perimental platforms for such studies. Alea [4], built on the GridSim toolkit [17], replays SWF workloads using built-in scheduling algorithms that users can extend at the source-code level. The Slurm Simulator [5] takes a different approach from purpose-built simulators by adapting the production Slurm codebase itself, allowing offline studies of scheduler config-urations to reproduce real Slurm behavior. AccaSim [6] pro-vides a customizable event-driven framework for dispatching research, while ElastiSim [7] extends batch-system simulation to malleable jobs. These systems are capable experimental instruments, and some provide visualization of simulation outputs; Alea, for example, can display utilization and queue metrics during a run. Nevertheless, they are primarily operated as programming or configuration frameworks: setting up an experiment requires writing scheduler code or editing config-uration files, while comparing policies across runs typically relies on user-developed analysis scripts. None packages the end-to-end workflow within a single interactive interface or supports evaluating alternative policies from an intermediate system state as a first-class capability. 

Digital twins of HPC systems. A complementary line of work develops digital twins for facility-level operational decision support. ExaDigiT [18] is an open framework for supercomputer digital twins that couples job-trace replay with resource-allocation, power, and cooling models. Its schedul-ing extension evaluates how policies and incentive structures affect facility-level outcomes [19]. A digital twin of the AOBA system similarly reproduces job scheduling and power-management behavior to help administrators tune parameters before deployment [20]. These efforts demonstrate the oper-ational value of twin-based what-if analysis [15], [16], [19], but their fidelity depends on coupling scheduler behavior with telemetry and installation-specific power and cooling models, making them facility-scale infrastructure projects. CQTwin addresses a lightweight, scheduler-focused portion of the same design space. It is portable across systems as long as scheduler records are represented in the SWF format, and runs locally without facility-level infrastructure integration. More importantly, CQTwin introduces an online digital twin capability absent from existing simulators, enabling real-time simulation from a live system state.

CQT WIN DESIGN 

CQTwin is organized around a simple idea: a single, high-performance simulation core serves as a digital twin of the batch scheduler and can be accessed through the visual in-terface. Figure 1 shows how the components fit together. At the center is a C++ discrete-event engine, accompanied by the original Python-based CQSim engine [8]–[10], which remains available to users who prefer to develop and extend scheduling policies in Python. The C++ engine is validated against Python CQSim and reproduces its scheduling decisions exactly on a per-job basis (§IV-A); the two engines therefore preserve the same scheduling semantics. The twin operates in two modes: offline, by replaying archived scheduler traces, and online, by consuming scheduler job events as they occur. Users can access both modes through a browser-based dashboard connected to a local server that configures runs, launches the engine, and transforms simula-tion outputs into interactive plots and tables. CQTwin also provides a programmatic interface through which external programs—such as reinforcement-learning agents or scripted experiments—can drive the online twin directly and select the dispatch policy at each scheduling cycle (§III-C). The entire toolkit runs locally: it requires no cloud service or user ac-count, and no operational data leaves the host machine. This is a deliberate design choice for facilities whose data-governance policies restrict movement of production job records. 

A. Discrete-Event Simulation Core 

The engine simulates a batch-scheduled cluster as the real system experiences it: jobs arrive over time and wait in a queue, a scheduling policy orders the queued jobs, and each job starts when sufficient nodes become available and releases those nodes upon completion. Workloads are derived from scheduler records, SWF workload traces in offline mode and live scheduler event streams in online mode. The simula-tion distinguishes between the information available to the scheduler and the job’s actual execution behavior: scheduling and backfilling decisions use each job’s requested walltime, whereas resource occupancy is determined by its recorded runtime. An optional perfect mode instead schedules jobs using their actual runtimes, providing an oracle upper bound on the potential benefit of perfectly accurate runtime estimates. CQTwin provides twenty scheduling policies behind acommon interface (Table I), from simple orderings to the weighted-priority functions used in production at ALCF, such as WFP and UNICEP [21], and the machine-learned composite functions F1–F4 of Carastan-Santos and de Camargo [22]. All policies combine with three backfilling modes [14], [23]: none, EASY, and Conservative. Scheduled and unscheduled maintenance windows can be injected from a file, so policies can be evaluated against realistic operational disruptions. The engine preserves CQSim’s scheduling semantics: Sec-tion IV verifies exact per-job agreement with the Python-based CQSim implementation. At the same time, it is fast enough to replay a year-long production trace in seconds. This performance makes the twin operationally useful: policy comparisons complete within an interactive time frame, and repeated simulations are inexpensive enough to run at every scheduling cycle, as required by the online twin. 

B. Offline Digital Twin: Trace Replay and What-If Analysis 

Trace replay. The offline twin answers retrospective ques-tions using a facility’s accumulated scheduler records. The user selects a historical trace, a scheduling policy, and a backfilling mode; the engine then replays the complete work-load under that configuration and reports how the system would have behaved, including each job’s wait time and slowdown, system utilization over time, and fairness metrics. Because the same workload is used across runs, differences in outcomes can be attributed to the scheduling configurations under comparison. Multiple configurations can be launched together and compared side by side (Figure 3). From an ODA perspective [24], the offline twin transforms archived scheduler logs into an analysis instrument. Scheduling policies can be evaluated against real production workloads without modifying the production system. 

Snapshot Lab: what-if analysis on frozen states. Beyond complete trace replay, the offline twin supports exploratory what-if analysis through the Snapshot Lab, which operates on completed simulation runs. A user can freeze a run at any selected time and inspect the system as it appeared at that moment, including queued jobs, running jobs, available nodes, and the next expected completion event. Selecting a queued job produces a plain-language explanation of why it is waiting. The explanation includes its queue position and dispatch rank, the running jobs occupying the resources it requires and their expected completion times, its earliest capacity-constrained start time, and an estimated wait time with a confidence indicator derived from similar completed jobs. For example, freezing a run during a burst of job arrivals captures the system when its queue is deepest. The explanation view identifies which running jobs hold the nodes required by the head-of-queue request and when those nodes will become available, while a forked scenario shows how the same backlog would drain under an alternative scheduling policy. From the frozen state, the user can create what-if scenarios and compare them with the baseline: switching the scheduling policy to examine changes in projected wait times, pinning a job as the next dispatch candidate, or injecting a planned maintenance window to measure queue-level delays. A downtime advisor can rank candidate maintenance windows by projected impact. Scenarios can be saved, reloaded, and viewed side by side. 

C. Online Digital Twin: Real-Time Decision Support 

The online twin addresses a different question: not what would have happened over the complete trace , but what is the appropriate decision now . The twin is event-driven: jobs enter only when their submission events are received, so its state at each scheduling cycle contains the current queue and running jobs but no information about future submissions. Events reach the twin in one of two ways. First, a recorded trace can drive the twin directly, with jobs admitted strictly at their recorded submission times. Second, the twin can consume a job-event stream through its socket protocol. An external feeder submits jobs as they arrive and indicates the time through which the stream is complete. The twin refuses to simulate beyond this asserted frontier: if the next simulation event lies beyond it, the twin waits for additional input rather than assuming that no jobs will arrive. The no-future-knowledge property is therefore enforced by construction: the twin process never contains a job event that it has not yet received. CQTwin includes a feeder that replays recorded traces as timed streams; for live schedulers, it documents an adapter interface specifying how a site-specific bridge would map scheduler events into the same protocol. CQTwin supports real-time decision-making. At each scheduling cycle, the live state can be forked across a user-selected pool of policies, with each fork simulated to estimate how the corresponding policy would process the currently visible workload without altering the committed schedule. Alternatively, an external program can interface with the pro-duction scheduler, observe the current state and optional pro-jections, and select the dispatch policy at each cycle. Together, these capabilities make CQTwin a training and evaluation environment for learning-based and adaptive scheduling [15], [19], [25]. 

D. Visual Dashboard: Scheduler-Data Analytics 

The dashboard is a browser-based visual analytics interface backed by a local server; no command-line interaction is required at any step. It serves both twins, and analysis proceeds in three stages: before, during, and after simulation. 

Before simulation , the workload analysis view charac-terizes a loaded trace from the SWF alone—no simulation required—across six panels: runtime and requested-processor distributions, wait time by runtime class, requested versus actual runtime, job arrival rate against average wait, and requested load over the makespan (Figure 2). This triage layer is operational data analytics in its own right, and often answers questions by itself. On Polaris-2023, for example, it immediately reveals a workload dominated by short, small jobs—91% finish within an hour, and 69% of submissions request eight or fewer nodes yet account for under 6% of requested processor-hours, while the top 5% of jobs by size account for 65%—alongside heavily overestimated walltimes (jobs run to only 17% of their request on average, the slack Fig. 2. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

We evaluate CQTwin along three dimensions: the fidelity of its offline and online twins, simulation performance, and online-twin overhead. CQTwin is compiled with -O3 , while Python CQSim runs under CPython 3.9. All reported timing results are the medians of three runs. The 2023 Polaris trace covers the system’s first full year of production operation. All three traces are publicly available through the ALCF data reports [26].

B. Simulation Speed 

As Table II shows, CQTwin achieves up to 24 × speedup over Python CQSim under FCFS with EASY backfill, com-pleting every trace in under 2.5 seconds; the year-long Polaris trace simulates in 2.3 seconds. The speedup comes from moving the event loop, job state, and policy dispatch into com-piled code with incremental event submission and lean per-run state. In practical terms, a six-policy comparison completes in about a minute through the dashboard, and workflows built on repeated simulation—parameter sweeps, per-cycle projections, and training loops that invoke the simulator thousands of times—drop from days of compute to hours. C. Online Overhead 

Per-cycle projections are cheap. On the full Polaris-2023 trace, forking and replaying all three pool policies costs about 0.66 ms per cycle, and even projecting at every cycle completes the year-long trace in under three minutes (Ta-ble III). The overhead buys information without side effects: the committed average wait is identical whether projections run at every cycle or not at all. The same projections drive external policy control end to end—a greedy controller ran the full trace through the Gym-style interface, switching the committed policy 3,084 times. This demonstrates that pro-jection outputs can drive an external control loop through a standard interface. We deliberately do not evaluate whether greedy switching improves scheduling outcomes, as CQTwin’s goal is operational analytics and decision support rather than developing a new scheduling method. V. D ISCUSSION 

CQTwin replays scheduler records, making it portable across workload traces but limiting the behaviors it can rep-resent. SWF-style records do not capture job dependencies, advance reservations, queue-specific access rules, topology constraints, or multi-resource requirements such as GPUs and memory. CQTwin is therefore a scheduler-focused digital twin rather than a full production-scheduler emulator. Exact per-job agreement with Python CQSim is validated for five policies under EASY backfilling, and the online twin inherits this fidelity through fixed-policy and stream-equivalence tests. The online twin currently operates on recorded scheduler events rather than a validated live production feed. Its on-line property is therefore about information availability : jobs become visible only when they arrive, and each projection is computed from the state available at that decision point, without access to future submissions. The validated event-stream interface reproduces trace-driven results exactly, so production deployment requires only a site-specific bridge that maps scheduler events into an event stream, without modifying the CQTwin engine. Future arrivals are unknowable at decision time, so a projection is a reference outcome rather than a prediction: it answers how a scheduling policy would clear the queue from the current state. CQTwin is an analytics tool, not a scheduling method: the committed policy always drives the actual run, while projections provide evidence for analysis or external policy control. Overall, CQTwin provides a fast, high-fidelity, and visualization-friendly foundation for HPC batch-scheduling research in both offline and online settings. Its modular interfaces also provide a flexible basis for developing and evaluating more advanced scheduling methods, allowing future intelligent and adaptive scheduling techniques to be built on top of CQTwin. Currently CQTwin models whole-node allocation only and does not represent GPUs, memory, heterogeneous node types, or other multi-resource constraints. It also excludes energy, power, cooling, temperature, and other facility telemetry mod-eled by broader digital twins such as ExaDigiT [18]. VI. C ONCLUSION 

HPC facilities collect scheduler data, but converting these records into practical insight remains difficult. CQTwin ad-dresses this gap by providing an open-source, scheduler-focused digital twin that supports both offline trace replay and online decision guidance without future-workload knowledge. Built on the CQSim simulator family, CQTwin combines a fidelity-validated, high-performance C++ engine with an in-teractive browser-based dashboard and Snapshot Lab, enabling facility staff, operators, and researchers to analyze workloads, compare scheduling policies, and explore what-if scenarios without modifying the production system or transferring oper-ational data off site. Our evaluation shows that the C++ engine reproduces Python CQSim’s scheduling decisions exactly on a per-job basis while running up to 24 × faster; stream-fed online execution exactly reproduces trace-driven replay; per-cycle online simulation adds approximately 0.66 ms of overhead per decision cycle; and end-to-end external policy control is demonstrated through the Gym-style interface.
