Abstract 

We study the problem of characterizing the optimal consistency–robustness tradeoff for single-machine non-clairvoyant scheduling with predicted processing times. For a fixed number of jobs 𝑛 , Wei and Zhang (2020) established a lower bound on the achievable consistency–robustness tradeoff which is known to be tight only in a few restrictive cases, leaving the optimal tradeoff for general 𝑛 unresolved. A different worst-case-𝑛 regime was introduced by Benomar et al. (2025a), who applied the former work’s construction to show that for 𝜆 ∈ ( 0, 1), any 

(1 + 𝜆 )-consistent algorithm must be 1 + Ω(√︁ 1/𝜆 )-robust. On the other hand, the best known upper bound gives 

(1 + 𝜆 )-consistency and 1 + 𝑂 (1/𝜆 )-robustness, leaving a polynomial gap. In this work, we develop a new lower bound using a multi-stage adversarial construction and a new algorithm that together give a near-optimal characterization of the consistency–robustness tradeoff: for every 𝑛 , our upper bound is within a multiplicative factor 3 /2 of our lower bound, and thus our algorithm is within a multiplicative factor 3 /2 of the online optimal. In particular, our lower bound shows that any (1 + 𝜆 )-consistent algorithm must have robustness at least 1 /√𝜆 as 𝑛 → ∞ , while our algorithm achieves robustness at most 12 +√︁ 54 + 1/𝜆 for any 𝑛 , matching the lower bound in the leading term as 𝜆 ↓ 0; our upper bound also closes the polynomial gap in the worst-case-𝑛 regime. We additionally show how our algorithm can be made error-tolerant, preserving the near-optimal consistency-robustness tradeoff while incurring only slightly degraded performance under bounded prediction errors. 

# 1 Introduction 

The framework of learning-augmented algorithms (also known as algorithms with predictions ) (Mitzenmacher and Vassilvitskii, 2021) enables the integration of machine-learned predictions into the design and analysis of algorithms, extending the classic competitive analysis framework (Borodin and El-Yaniv, 2005). Central to this framework is the study of the Pareto frontier between consistency , which measures algorithm performance when predictions are perfect, and robustness , which measures performance under potentially adversarial prediction error. While the optimal tradeoff between these metrics has been characterized (either exactly or asymptotically) for a number of problems including ski rental (Wei and Zhang, 2020), online search (Sun et al., 2021), and metrical task systems (Christianson et al., 2023), it remains an open question for the classical problem of single-machine non-clairvoyant scheduling with total completion time objective (Motwani et al., 1994), despite the significant attention this problem has received in the learning-augmented algorithms literature (e.g., (Kumar et al., 2018; Wei and Zhang, 2020; Benomar et al., 2025a)) and the problem’s deep connections with applications in computer systems and beyond. In this work, we seek to resolve this gap and develop near-optimal learning-augmented algorithms for this problem. In particular, we will consider both lower and upper bounds that depend on the number of jobs 𝑛 . We parameterize the algorithm’s consistency-robustness tradeoff (see Definition 2.2 for formal definitions) by a hyperparameter 𝜆 > 0; the open question is the characterization of the optimal robustness that can be achieved by any (1 + 𝜆 )-consistent algorithm.

## 1.1 Contributions 

This paper makes contributions along two fronts: improved lower and upper bounds on consistency and robustness for the non-clairvoyant scheduling problem, and new technical ideas that enable these improved bounds. 

Consistency–robustness tradeoff. This work provides a near-optimal characterization of the consistency–robustness tradeoff for non-clairvoyant scheduling. On the lower bound side, we prove that the bound of Wei and Zhang (2020) is not tight for 𝑛 ≥ 5, even when considering stronger padded versions of their bound (Proposition 3.1 and Corollary 3.3). We then derive a stronger, implicitly-defined lower bound Φ(𝑛 ) (𝜆 ) on the robustness of any (1 + 𝜆 )-consistent algorithm (Theorem 3.9). On the upper bound side, we propose a new algorithm, Modified Two-Stage Schedule (mTSS ), which achieves (1 + 𝜆 )-consistency and Ψ𝑛 (𝜆 )-robustness, where Ψ𝑛 (𝜆 ) is the optimal value of a certain finite optimization problem (Theorem 4.4). We prove that, for every 𝑛 ≥ 2 and admissible 𝜆 , our upper bound is at most 3 /2 times our lower bound (Theorem 4.6), and thus our algorithm achieves within a factor 3 /2 of the optimal robustness-consistency tradeoff. Moreover, our bounds can be computed for any value of 𝑛 , and appear to match empirically up to a much smaller multiplicative gap of ≲ 1.0937. We also characterize the asymptotic behavior of the optimal tradeoff as 𝑛 → ∞ . Specifically, we show that any 

(1 + 𝜆 )-consistent algorithm must have robustness at least ˆ 𝛾 (𝜆 ) ≥ 1/√𝜆 in the large-𝑛 limit, while mTSS achieves robustness at most 12 +√︁ 54 + 1/𝜆 for every 𝑛 and admissible 𝜆 (Theorems 3.10 and 4.7). These bounds match in their leading term 1 /√𝜆 as 𝜆 ↓ 0. In particular, our lower bound improves the leading constant of the Wei–Zhang lower bound by a factor of √2 in this asymptotic regime (cf. Proposition 3.2). Our universal upper bound also closes the polynomial gap in the worst-case-𝑛 regime. We summarize our lower and upper bounds in Table 1 and show plots comparing our bounds against the prior state-of-the-art bounds in Figure 1. As consistency only measures algorithm performance under perfect predictions, we complement these guarantees on consistency and robustness by additionally studying how to retain good performance when predictions have bounded error. To this end, we develop a generic error-tolerant simulation method that preserves consistency while providing local smoothness, i.e., smooth degradation in performance as prediction error increases, at the cost of only a small increase in the worst-case robustness bound (Theorem 4.8). 

Technical contributions. Our analysis departs from traditional combinatorial arguments by adopting an optimization-based perspective. Rather than analyzing specific hard instances or fixed algorithms, we formulate the search for consistency–robustness lower and upper bounds as variational problems over the space of adversarial attacks, using the following insights. 

Lower bounds: from single-stage to multi-stage attacks. Existing lower bounds—in particular, the lower bound of Wei and Zhang (2020)—rely on adversaries that “attack” the algorithm at a single critical moment by revealing a prediction error. We introduce the 𝛼 -Instance Attack Set (Definition 3.4), which allows the adversary to choose among attacks at different stages—i.e., after varying numbers of jobs have been processed. This forces the algorithm to balance its processing decisions against multiple possible attack times and leads to a structured nonlinear optimization problem characterizing the relationship between consistency and robustness on this instance class (Problem 5 or 36). Our key structural insight is that, within a certain range of robustness values, optimality of a solution to this problem forces equal processing across unfinished jobs and tightness of all constraints (Lemma 3.6). This yields a transformation 3of the optimization problem into a finite system of bilinear equations implicitly characterizing the finite-𝑛 lower bound; in the asymptotic 𝑛 → ∞ limit, this system of equations yields the scaling √︁ 1/𝜆 . We anticipate that our general technique of developing and analyzing a multi-stage adversarial construction as an optimization problem may be useful for studying consistency–robustness tradeoffs in other online problems. 

Upper bounds: reduction to the time of error detection. In developing our refined upper bounds, our algorithm  

> mTSS

refines the two-stage schedule of Wei and Zhang (2020) by enforcing exact calibration of consistency at the level (1 + 𝜆 ). To analyze this strategy, our main technical insight is that the high-dimensional, continuous space of prediction errors admits a simple worst-case structure for mTSS which reduces to algorithm performance at the time of error detection. Leveraging the affine structure of the online cost and the concavity of the offline optimum, we develop a unified reduction accounting for both over-predictions and under-predictions. Reparametrizing the remaining configurations with a collection of nonnegative “gap” variables, we show that the exact consistency calibration in mTSS 

imposes a single linear equality constraint on these variables, so the resulting feasible region is a cone. Elements of this cone can be decomposed in order to obtain a simplified optimization-based expression for the robustness. Together, these techniques make robustness analysis tractable for general 𝑛 , yielding a computable finite-𝑛 guarantee and a closed-form universal bound that closes the polynomial gap in the worst-case-𝑛 and 𝑛 → ∞ regimes.

## 1.2 Related Work 

Our work contributes to a long history of research on algorithms with predictions and the non-clairvoyant scheduling problem. We briefly review these areas below. 

Learning-Augmented Algorithms. Learning-augmented algorithms (also known as algorithms with predictions )is an emerging research area that incorporates predictions about future inputs into algorithm design, with the goal of combining the strong empirical performance of machine learning predictors with the worst-case guarantees of traditional online algorithms. Since the introduction of this framework by Kumar et al. (2018) and Lykouris and Vassilvitskii (2021), it has been applied to a wide range of online problems such as caching (Lykouris and Vassilvitskii, 2021; Rohatgi, 2020; Wei, 2020; Im et al., 2022), ski rental (Kumar et al., 2018; Wei and Zhang, 2020; Antoniadis et al., 2021), online knapsack (Im et al., 2021; Lechowicz et al., 2024c; Daneshvaramoli et al., 2025), online conversion (Sun et al., 2021; Lechowicz et al., 2024b), convex function chasing (Christianson et al., 2022; Lechowicz et al., 2024a), and metrical task systems (Antoniadis et al., 2020; Christianson et al., 2023; Coester et al., 2026). Beyond classic online algorithms problems, the learning-augmented algorithms framework has also been adopted in several neighboring areas, including mechanism design (Agrawal et al., 2022; Balkanski et al., 2024), control (Li et al., 2022, 2024), and reinforcement learning (Golowich and Moitra, 2022; Li et al., 2023). While most works focus on developing algorithms with consistency and robustness guarantees, several recent works have proposed additional performance criteria that go beyond these standard notions, such as smooth degradation of performance in the face of prediction error (Elenter et al., 2024; Benomar et al., 2025b), learnability of predictions (Dinitz et al., 2021; Khodak et al., 2022), and prediction-specific performance guarantees (Li et al., 2026). Since improving consistency typically comes at the expense of robustness, an overarching goal of this area is to characterize the Pareto-optimal tradeoff between consistency and robustness (Wei and Zhang, 2020). A number of works have characterized exactly- or asymptotically-optimal consistency–robustness tradeoffs for online problems such 4as ski rental (Wei and Zhang, 2020), online conversion (Sun et al., 2021), metrical task systems (Christianson et al., 2023), and non-clairvoyant scheduling with 2 jobs (Wei and Zhang, 2020). However, despite this progress, exact and asymptotically Pareto-optimal consistency–robustness tradeoffs remain open for many online problems, including non-clairvoyant scheduling with a general number of jobs 𝑛 .

Non-Clairvoyant Scheduling. Scheduling problems are a foundational class of optimization problems with a wide variety of applications spanning computer systems and operations research (Panwalkar and Iskander, 1977; Lenstra and Rinnooy Kan, 1978; Pinedo, 2022). In its most basic form, scheduling considers a set of jobs to be processed on a single machine, with the objective of minimizing the total completion time of all jobs. In the non-clairvoyant setting, where job processing times are unknown a priori and are revealed only upon completion, the Round-Robin (RR )algorithm, which divides the machine equally among all unfinished jobs, achieves the optimal deterministic competitive ratio 2 𝑛 /( 𝑛 + 1) for 𝑛 jobs; its tight uniform bound over all 𝑛 is 2 (Motwani et al., 1994). In contrast, in the clairvoyant setting where job processing times are known a priori, the Shortest Job First (SJF ) algorithm, which schedules jobs in non-decreasing order of their sizes, is optimal. Beyond these classic settings, a growing body of work has studied scheduling models augmented with machine-learned predictions, with the goal of leveraging predicted job properties to improve performance while preserving worst-case guarantees. The most standard prediction model assumes that predictions provide estimates of all job processing times. Using this model, Kumar et al. (2018) proposed the Preferential Round-Robin algorithm, which achieves (1 + 𝜆 )-consistency and (2 + 1/𝜆 )-robustness. Subsequently, Wei and Zhang (2020) established a lower bound on the achievable consistency–robustness tradeoff in this setting, and showed that their bound is tight for 𝑛 = 2 jobs, as well as at the two endpoints of the consistency parameter 𝜆 .A number of works have also explored alternative prediction models and performance metrics, such as partial predictions (Benomar and Perchet, 2024), permutation predictions (Lindermayr and Megow, 2022), progress bar predictions (Benomar et al., 2025a), classification-based predictions (Dugois et al., 2026), different metrics of prediction error (Im et al., 2023), and smoothness (Zhao and Zomaya, 2026). The results of Benomar et al. (2025a) in particular specialize to the setting we consider, improving upon the consistency–robustness tradeoff of Kumar et al. (2018) by giving (1 + 𝜆 )-consistency and (1 + 1/𝜆 )-robustness. Their work also established a robustness lower bound of 1 + Ω(√︁ 1/𝜆 ) for any (1 + 𝜆 )-consistent algorithm in the aforementioned “worst-case 𝑛 ” regime.

## 2.1 The Classic Non-Clairvoyant Scheduling Problem 

In the classic single-machine non-clairvoyant scheduling problem, a scheduler is given 𝑛 jobs. Job 𝑖 ∈ [ 𝑛 ] requires 

𝑥 𝑖 units of processing on the machine, but 𝑥 𝑖 is unknown to the scheduler until the job completes. At each time, the machine has one unit of processing capacity. A schedule may allocate this capacity among unfinished jobs, and jobs may be preempted and resumed at any time at no cost; equivalently, a fractional allocation can be implemented by time-slicing on a single machine with arbitrarily small time quanta. The objective is to minimize the total completion time Í𝑖 ∈ [ 𝑛 ] 𝐶 𝑖 , where 𝐶 𝑖 is the clock time at which job 𝑖 completes, i.e., the first time by which job 𝑖 has received its required 𝑥 𝑖 units of processing. An instance of the problem is specified by the vector of processing requirements 

𝐼 = (𝑥 1, . . . , 𝑥 𝑛 ); we use I to denote the set of all such instances. As a benchmark, the clairvoyant version of the problem is the case in which the processing requirements {𝑥 𝑖 }𝑖 ∈ [ 𝑛 ]

are known to the scheduler in advance. In this setting, it is well known that Shortest Remaining Processing Time 

(SRPT ) minimizes the total completion time (Schrage, 1968). Since all jobs are available at time 0, this is equivalent to Shortest Job First (SJF ), a special case of Smith’s rule (Smith, 1956). However, in the non-clairvoyant setting, 5the processing requirements are unknown a priori , so algorithms are evaluated by comparing their total completion time to the clairvoyant optimum (Motwani et al., 1994). This performance metric is formalized as the competitive ratio (Borodin and El-Yaniv, 2005), defined as follows. 

Definition 2.1 (Competitive Ratio) . Let I denote the set of instances of the non-clairvoyant scheduling problem. For an algorithm A and instance 𝐼 ∈ I , let ALG (A, 𝐼 ) be the total completion time achieved by A, and let OPT (𝐼 ) be the total completion time of the optimal clairvoyant schedule. An algorithm A is 𝑐 -competitive, or has competitive ratio 𝑐 , if 

ALG (A, 𝐼 ) ≤ 𝑐 · OPT (𝐼 ) for all 𝐼 ∈ I .

It was established by Motwani et al. (1994) that the Round-Robin ( RR ) algorithm achieves the optimal deterministic competitive ratio of 2 𝑛 /( 𝑛 + 1) for 𝑛 jobs, with a tight uniform bound of 2 over all 𝑛 .

## 2.2 Non-Clairvoyant Scheduling with Predictions 

The competitive ratio measures the worst-case performance of an algorithm on any problem instance; however, in practice, one may be able to do better when given predictions about the underlying instance. To move beyond the pessimism of worst-case algorithms for non-clairvoyant scheduling, we consider the “prediction-augmented” setting, where the algorithm has access to a prediction for each job’s size; let 𝑦 𝑖 denote the predicted size of job 𝑖 . Importantly, we place no assumptions on the accuracy of these predictions: they can be arbitrarily inaccurate or even adversarial. Thus, the algorithm seeks to take advantage of the predictions when they are good, while remaining robust in case of poor prediction quality. Consistent with the standard non-clairvoyant scheduling model, we continue to assume that the true processing time 𝑥 𝑖 of each job 𝑖 is not known to the algorithm in advance and is revealed only once the job has received 𝑥 𝑖 units of processing. To evaluate the performance of algorithms for non-clairvoyant scheduling with predictions, we use the standard notions of consistency and robustness (Kumar et al., 2018; Wei and Zhang, 2020). To formalize these criteria, we define an instance of the prediction-augmented problem as comprising both the true processing requirements {𝑥 𝑖 }𝑖 ∈ [ 𝑛 ] and the predicted processing requirements {𝑦 𝑖 }𝑖 ∈ [ 𝑛 ] of the jobs in an instance. Let I denote the set of all such instances, and let I∗ ⊆ I denote the set of consistent instances , i.e., those for which the predictions are correct, so 𝑥 𝑖 = 𝑦 𝑖 for all 𝑖 ∈ [ 𝑛 ]. Consistency then measures the worst-case performance of an algorithm over all consistent instances I∗,while robustness considers the worst case over all prediction-augmented instances I, regardless of prediction quality. Formally, we have the following definitions: 

Definition 2.2 (Consistency and Robustness) . The consistency of an algorithm A is its worst-case competitive ratio over consistent instances. In particular, an algorithm A is 𝛽 -consistent if 

ALG (A, 𝐼 ) ≤ 𝛽 · OPT (𝐼 ) for all 𝐼 ∈ I ∗.

The robustness of A is its worst-case competitive ratio over all instances. In particular, an algorithm A is 𝛾 -robust if 

ALG (A, 𝐼 ) ≤ 𝛾 · OPT (𝐼 ) for all 𝐼 ∈ I .

In learning-augmented algorithms, there is typically a tradeoff between consistency and robustness: a smaller consistency requires the algorithm to more closely follow the predictions, which can in turn lead to a larger robustness. From a game-theoretic perspective, for a given parameter 𝜆 > 0, the optimal consistency–robustness tradeoff can be viewed as the equilibrium of a constrained minimax game in which the algorithm aims to minimize its worst-case robustness subject to maintaining (1 + 𝜆 )-consistency, while an adversary chooses instances to maximize the algorithm’s robustness. This is the view that will guide our lower and upper bounds in this work as we seek to characterize this equilibrium.

# 4 Algorithms and Upper Bounds 

With our refined lower bounds established, we now proceed to develop an improved upper bound for the optimal consistency–robustness tradeoff. We first introduce our Modified Two-Stage Schedule (mTSS ) algorithm, which retains the two-stage structure of Wei and Zhang (2020)’s algorithm, starting with a round-robin ( RR ) stage that allocates equal processing time to all jobs before switching to processing the jobs in predicted order. The key difference in our approach is to calibrate the duration of the RR stage to exhaust the consistency budget. We show that the robustness of this algorithm can be reduced to its performance at the time that any error is detected, which yields a tractable, optimization-based robustness bound for general 𝑛 . Relaxing this bound gives a closed-form analytic bound that holds uniformly over all 𝑛 .

# 5 Conclusion and Future Directions 

In this work, we provide a nearly-optimal characterization of the consistency–robustness tradeoff for non-clairvoyant scheduling with predictions. We show that the lower bound of Wei and Zhang (2020) is not tight for general 𝑛 and introduce a multi-stage adversarial strategy that yields a strictly stronger piecewise lower bound for 𝑛 ≥ 5 over a nontrivial range of 𝜆 . Complementing this lower bound, we propose the Modified Two-Stage Schedule algorithm (mTSS ), which achieves a nearly-optimal consistency–robustness tradeoff, with an upper bound within a factor of 3 /2of our lower bound for every 𝑛 ≥ 2 and any admissible 𝜆 .

Furthermore, our results resolve (up to constant factors) the polynomial gap between the upper and lower bounds on robustness in the worst-case-𝑛 regime (Benomar et al., 2025a). Together, our results suggest that the key to optimal scheduling with predictions lies in the effective management of the algorithm’s 

flexibility budget , i.e., balancing following the predictions directly with preserving robustness with a more conservative strategy. We also show that these guarantees can be made error-tolerant, developing a generic approach to enable learning-augmented algorithms to provide locally smooth degradation to small prediction errors. Our work suggests several potentially promising directions for future work. First, a natural extension is to characterize near-optimal consistency–robustness tradeoffs for non-clairvoyant scheduling with known job weights and predicted processing times, complementing existing results for weighted scheduling with other forms of predictions (Lindermayr and Megow, 2022). Second, our results could potentially serve as a foundation for refining consistency–robustness tradeoffs under alternative prediction models, including partial predictions (Benomar and Perchet, 2024), permutation predictions (Lindermayr and Megow, 2022), and progress bar predictions (Benomar et al., 2025a). Third, it would be interesting to explore whether the proposed optimization-based formulation of multi-stage lower bounds can be extended to other online problems such as metrical task systems in order to obtain tighter, instance-dependent lower bounds. Finally, closing the small gap for finite 𝑛 ≥ 5 may require new technical ideas, particularly on the upper bound front. To this end, we conjecture that optimal robustness may require repeated cycles of RR and committing to the predicted order.
