## ABSTRACT 

The goal of a world model is to capture the dynamics of an environment. This has the great appeal that it allows agents to plan and simulate a course of ac-tions with the help of a world model. For these uses to be reliable, however, the learned dynamics must behave well beyond the specific state–action combinations observed in expert rollouts. Despite this, the relationship between data coverage, dynamics generalization, and downstream planning performance remains under-explored. We study this relationship through the lens of compositional general-ization: can a world model recombine familiar state and action factors into valid dynamics? To answer this, we introduce AnyGoal, a benchmark of goal-shifted task variants built from widely used world-model environments, together with an open-source library. Each task is designed to be within reach of a world model that has learned the underlying dynamics: individual state factors and actions remain within their training support, but their combinations are underrepresented in ex-pert trajectories. Across multiple environments and world-model families, we find that current models consistently exhibit failures under these recombinations. By examining dynamics and representation failures across model families, interaction regimes, and evaluation metrics, we study where current world models generalize and where challenges remain for goal-shifted prediction and planning. Together, this framework, benchmark, and analysis provide a basis for evaluating progress toward world models that generalize to new planning tasks. Dino-WM

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

A key goal of physical intelligence is the ability to plan and perform new tasks. Routine tasks, such as driving to work, can be automatized and executed without much cognitive effort. Yet novel tasks requires us to plan and simulate the consequences of our actions. World models (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Maes et al., 2026b; Ivashkov et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025) aim to learn action-conditioned dynamics of environment by predicting how the world evolves under actions rather than task-specific behavior (Sutton & Barto, 2018). Therefore, a good world model should not merely memorize behavioral biases present in the training distribution. However, in-distribution evaluation offers limited evidence of reusable dynamics. In Push-T, the agent must push a block to a target pose, so expert rollouts strongly couple far-from-block states with actions that move the agent toward the block. A model trained on Push-T may therefore learn this rollout regularity rather than the action-conditioned effect of the supplied action, as reflected by the action-sensitivity plot in Fig. 1.When evaluated on Avoid-T, which requires avoiding the block using familiar 2D navigation, the model fails. If the training data fully covered the relevant joint state–action space, then a model could in principle predict correctly on that support. However, such coverage is unrealistic in many control problems, especially when the relevant space is high-dimensional. First, offline datasets for world-model train-ing are often collected from goal-directed policies that reach task-relevant states (Burda et al., 2019), which can introduce strong state–action correlations. One possible remedy is to mix data from mul-tiple policies (Zhao et al., 2026). However, even with such mixtures, it can be difficult to determine whether the resulting dataset sufficiently covers the relevant state–action space, especially when the true reachable set is unknown. Even when a coverage gap is identified, collecting targeted rollouts to fill it can be difficult in offline settings. This makes it necessary to study generalization under incomplete coverage, rather than assuming that data scale alone will resolve the issue. Generalization of world models has been studied to some extent. Closest to our setting, Hansen & Wang (2026) study hallucination detection and mitigation in world models, while we evaluate gen-eralization in a statistically controlled space with explicit state-action support checks. Other OOD evaluations introduce states or factors outside the training support (Maes et al., 2026a; Wang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). Such settings are valuable for testing robustness to novel factors or extrapola-tion, but they may go beyond the generalization expected from a reusable dynamics model. What remains missing is a systematic evaluation of the generalization that a good world model should support. First, we formalize this criterion as compositional generalization (Wiedemer et al., 2024b) and link it to goal shifts and behavior bias in the training data. We further show that the bias is empirically realized through targeted action interventions on familiar but under-covered state– action combinations, eliciting degenerate model responses. We then propose AnyGoal (Fig. 2), a benchmark of goal-shifted task variants built from widely used world-model environments. To re-main in the compositional-generalization regime, AnyGoal preserves the observed marginal support of states, actions, task-relevant factors, and physical rules, while changing the joint combinations induced by the training behavior distribution and targeting under-covered state–action regions. With AnyGoal, we test whether current world models can generalize to new tasks without requiring ex-trapolation to unseen states, actions, or visual factors. Across this controlled setting, we observe broad failures across modeling paradigms, including generative world models (Zhu et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2026) and JEPA-style architectures (Maes et al., 2026b; Ivashkov et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 22025). Our results then characterize this degradation across several axes, showing that failures can arise from both dynamics prediction and representation learning, and that their severity depends on task complexity, evaluation metric, and model family. Finally, we outline a theoretical direction to-ward compositional generalization for world models, framing what future models may need in order to move beyond behavior-biased rollouts. 

## 2 RELATED WORKS 

Compositional generalization diagnostics and benchmarks. Compositional generalization tests whether a model can recombine familiar components into unseen configurations, a key chal-lenge when possible compositions exceed finite data coverage. Early sequence-to-sequence bench-marks such as SCAN (Lake & Baroni, 2018) and CFQ (Keysers et al., 2020) exposed this fail-ure mode through controlled compositional splits. The same diagnostic was subsequently adopted in vision and multimodal settings, with benchmarks such as CLEVR (Johnson et al., 2017), Winoground (Thrush et al., 2022), and CVR (Zerroug et al., 2022) testing whether familiar ob-jects, attributes, and relations can be composed correctly. These failures remain prominent in mod-ern large-scale models: ConceptMix (Wu et al., 2024) shows that text-to-image models degrade as prompts require more familiar concepts to be composed jointly, while recent vision–language work continues to reveal failures in object–attribute and relational composition (Assouel et al., 2026). At the same time, compositional evaluation is becoming broader and more systematic, with scalable protocols for probing generalization across controlled compositions (Camposampiero et al., 2026), and similar shifts being used as diagnostics in mechanistic interpretability (Pacela et al., 2026). AnyGoal brings this perspective to world models, where goal-directed trajectories naturally induce restricted joint coverage over otherwise familiar physical states and actions. 

World-model generalization and support mismatch. Recent world-model work has studied generalization through object-centric compositional dynamics (Zhao et al., 2022), composable causal components for unseen environments (Wang & Huang, 2025), multi-task world mod-els (Georgiev et al., 2025), environment-level evaluation queries (Warrier et al., 2026), action-conditioned planning (Zhang & Du, 2026), and coverage-related hallucination failures (Hansen & Wang, 2026). However, less is known about whether expert-trained world models generalize to new goal-induced recombinations of familiar states and actions. This question is closely connected to support mismatch in offline reinforcement learning, where fixed behavior datasets can lead methods to fail when evaluating poorly covered state–action regions (Fujimoto et al., 2019; Kumar et al., 2019; Levine et al., 2020). Offline RL addresses support mismatch by constraining or regularizing policies toward covered regions (Gao et al., 2025b;a; Laroche et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2025; Ku-mar et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2025). Model-based offline RL extends this issue to learned dynamics, motivating uncertainty- or pessimism-based objectives for poorly covered regions (Yu et al., 2020; Kidambi et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2021; Rigter et al., 2022). D4RL further shows that dataset compo-sition strongly affects offline policy improvement (Fu et al., 2021). AnyGoal is complementary to these approaches: it evaluates whether the world model learns reusable dynamics under goal shifts.

## 4 ANY GOAL : C ONTROLLED GOAL -S HIFT EVALUATION 

We introduce AnyGoal, a benchmark for testing whether world models generalize beyond the goal-directed trajectories on which they are trained. AnyGoal uses the controlled goal shifts discussed in Sec. 3.2 and builds on four widely used world-model tasks: TwoRoom (Sobal et al., 2025), Push-T (Chi et al., 2025), DMC Manipulator Insert-Peg (Tassa et al., 2018), and OGBench Cube (Park et al., 2025). AnyGoal contains eight variants: one free-space variant per base task and one or two contact-rich variants except TwoRoom. We release 400 episodes per variant, with data-collection tools for generating additional datasets. Figure 2 summarizes the tasks, with details in Appendix B. 4.1 ANY GOAL TARGETS THE COMBINATORIAL GAP 

Validating Cte ⊆ C ×. For each base–target task pair, we apply three factor-wise support checks to verify that the target distribution remains marginally in-distribution relative to its reference dis-tribution. The range check requires all query samples to lie within the coordinate-wise reference range. The Mahalanobis check uses the reference mean and covariance, thresholds distances at the reference 0.99 -quantile, and requires at least 95% of query samples to fall below it. The KDE check thresholds reference densities at the 0.01 -quantile and requires at least 95% of query samples to lie above it. The final support verdict is the conjunction of all three checks. All AnyGoal variants are designed to pass these factor-wise checks, which indicates that all tasks lie within the combinatorial space of training data. Appendix C provides the full details of pass-criterion definitions, the verified factor groups for each base task, and the complete score of range, Mahalanobis, and KDE results. 

Measuring combinatorial-gap coverage of Cte . We then estimate how much each target dis-tribution overlaps with the base-task joint support. Unlike the factor-wise checks above, this joint test is meant to diagnose the combinatorial gap: individual factors may be marginally familiar while their full state–action combination is rare under the training distribution. For each base–target pair, we concatenate the action, agent state, and task-object or target state, fit a full-dimensional Gaus-sian KDE to the reference vectors, and set the density threshold to the empirical 0.01 -quantile of log-densities. We then report the fraction of target vectors whose log-density exceeds this threshold. Lower joint scores indicate less overlap with the base-task joint distribution and greater coverage of the combinatorial gap. Full vector definitions and KDE implementation details are in Appendix D. 4.2 RELATIVE EVALUATION AGAINST BASE TASKS

Goal-shift performance cannot be summarized by a single number. The same degradation can reflect different sources depending on whether it appears in prediction or planning, in latent or pixel space, in free-space or contact-rich tasks, and with or without oracle state access. We therefore organize the results around five recurring dimensions for interpreting the results: 

1. Failure source : Predictor failures can be isolated from encoder failures via oracle-state predictors. 

2. Evaluation metric : Rollout prediction and planning are showing different aspects. 

3. Task complexity : Free-space and contact-rich tasks impose different dynamics challenges. 

4. Representation quality : Learned representations sometimes obscure task-relevant dynamics, although they sometimes can improve planning geometry. 

5. Prediction space : JEPA-style and generative models show distinct trends, but comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because prediction errors are measured in different spaces.

This suggests that the difficulty is not merely access to the relevant state factors (Klindt et al., 2026; Shi et al., 2026), but whether the goal shift requires precise object interaction. This asymmetry is consistent with prior work showing that compositional generalization requires constraints on the learned function. Additive or interaction-free settings are especially favorable, since factor-specific mechanisms can be learned from observed compositions and reused under novel recombinations (Dong & Ma, 2022; Lachapelle et al., 2023; Wiedemer et al., 2024a; Mahajan et al., 2025). This intuition aligns with the independent causal mechanisms principle, under which reusable mechanisms remain autonomous as their joint occur-rence changes (Parascandolo et al., 2018). Contact-rich variants depart from this favorable regime: action effects may no longer decompose into reusable factor-specific mechanisms. Compositional generalization may still be possible, but only with additional structure on these interactions (Brady et al., 2025). The same asymmetry appears in rollout prediction. Contact-rich variants show much larger error increases, often by one to three orders of magnitude, even with oracle states. Free-space variants degrade far less, and in some cases match or improve on the base-task error. Thus, removing representation learning does not make goal-shifted dynamics prediction uniformly reliable. 

Metric alignment depends on task complexity. We additionally observe that rollout error and planning performance sometimes diverge in free-space variants where planning can tolerate imper-fect prediction. The closer agreement in contact-rich settings may reflect the structure of the planning problem. Rollout error measures whether the model predicts the full trajectory accurately, whereas MPC only needs to find some action sequence that reaches the goal, regardless of the exact interme-diate path. Free-space tasks often admit many such trajectories, while contact-rich tasks allow fewer viable sequences because success also depends on the correct object interaction. 5.3 REPRESENTATION EFFECTS IN END -TO -E ND WORLD MODELS

Factor recombination can induce observation-level shifts. AnyGoal controls generalization at the level of physical factors, but end-to-end models observe rendered inputs s = f (z). Recombining marginally familiar factors can therefore create observations outside the joint visual support of ex-pert rollouts, adding an encoder-inference challenge beyond dynamics prediction. (See Appendix I.2 for details.) Crucially, correct factor inference on training observations need not extend to unseen compositions. Even when factors are identifiable in distribution, an encoder may fail to invert novel recombined observations without compositional-consistency constraints (Wiedemer et al., 2024a). Thus, end-to-end world models face a representation-level generalization problem before dynamics prediction is considered. End-to-end models must therefore infer task-relevant factors from obser-vations generated by unseen factor combinations, in addition to learning dynamics. 

Latent geometry shapes degradation under goal shifts. TwoRoom provides a useful case where end-to-end degradation cannot be attributed to an observation-level shift. Because agent position is the only changing factor and TR-Route positions remain marginally within the TwoRoom training support, the corresponding observations stay visually in support, enabling a controlled compari-son between end-to-end models and oracle-state predictors. ## 6 TOWARDS WORLD MODELS THAT GENERALIZE COMPOSITIONALLY 

Our oracle-state experiments show that compositional failures cannot be attributed only to visual representation learning. Even when task-relevant physical state is provided directly, goal shifts re-main difficult, particularly when prediction involves contact-rich interactions. This raises a broader question for future work: what assumptions on the dynamics, data, and learned representation are sufficient for a world model to generalize compositionally? We see two distinct challenges. 

1. Identifying mechanisms. Fitting training trajectories does not ensure that a predictor has learned reusable action-conditioned mechanisms. This becomes harder in interaction-rich dy-namics where action effects can vary with state and regime. Characterizing when such mecha-nisms can be identified from offline trajectories remains an open problem. 

2. Extrapolation beyond the training support. Even if the correct mechanisms are identified, their behavior on novel state–action compositions is generally not determined without additional structural assumptions. Interaction-free dynamics provide a favorable case for compositional generalization because factor effects can often be reused independently, but world models must also handle interaction-rich dynamics where action effects vary with the joint physical config-uration and regime. In this setting, extrapolation is fundamentally harder because the relevant interactions may change across compositions, and is a fruitful direction for future work.

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced AnyGoal, a benchmark for evaluating whether world models generalize to new be-havioral goals by recombining familiar states and actions. Across a diverse set of models, goal-shift generalization remains difficult even when the relevant state and action factors are marginally cov-ered by the training distribution. Together, these analyses show that goal-shift failures are heteroge-neous, reflecting interactions among representation, dynamics prediction, task structure, and evalu-ation metric. More broadly, AnyGoal connects world-model evaluation with compositional general-ization. It asks for prediction on novel joint state–action combinations within the product of marginal training supports, a reasonable but unresolved requirement for reusable dynamics. Our theoretical perspective suggests that global coverage of all state–action combinations is neither realistic nor necessary. Instead, world models need coverage of local mechanism inputs, together with objectives or architectures that identify action-conditioned mechanisms rather than behavior-policy shortcuts. By isolating goal-induced recombination as a failure mode, AnyGoal provides a controlled basis for evaluating progress toward world models that learn reusable dynamics beyond expert rollouts. We formalize why our compositional evaluation is an essential test for world models. The key dis-tinction is between extrapolating to unseen factors and recombining observed ones. Good action-conditioned world model should predict the effect of an observed action on familiar physical factors, even when that particular combination was not selected by the behavior policy. For clarity, consider a deterministic oracle-state setting with true controlled dynamics zt+1 =

T ⋆(zt, a t). Suppose the offline dataset is collected by a behavior policy a = πE (z). Then the ob-served state–action support lies on the policy graph, 

ΓπE = {(z, a ) : a = πE (z)}. (6) Thus, expert rollouts cover only the state–action pairings induced by the behavior policy, not arbi-trary interventions with observed actions. 

Zero Training Error Does Not Imply Action-Conditioned Dynamics. A predictive world model trained on (z, a, z ′) ∼ ptr is constrained only on the state–action pairs generated by the behavior policy. Since observed actions satisfy a = πE (z), the training loss constrains ˆT (z, π E (z)) , but not 

ˆT (z, a ) for alternative observed actions at the same state. Consequently, the action-ignorant predictor 

ˆTdeg (z, a ) = T ⋆(z, π E (z)) (7) achieves zero prediction error on the behavior dataset while ignoring the queried action a. Thus, expert-rollout prediction cannot distinguish a controlled dynamics model from a predictor that only models behavior-policy transitions. 

Compositional Evaluation Requires Action-Conditioned Dynamics Compositional evaluation rules out this degenerate solution. Assume actions have nonzero effect within the observed marginal supports: for a set of oracle states with positive measure, there exist observed actions a, a ′ ∈ A tr 

such that 

T ⋆(z, a )̸ = T ⋆(z, a ′).

Now consider any action-ignorant predictor ˆT (z, a ) = h(z). If it were correct on the relevant com-positional state–action space, then for every observed action a ∈ A tr , h(z) = T ⋆(z, a ). Therefore, for any two observed actions a, a ′,

T ⋆(z, a ) = h(z) = T ⋆(z, a ′), (8) which contradicts the action-relevance assumption. Hence, no action-ignorant model can be correct on recombinations of observed state and action factors when actions causally affect the next state. 

Interpretation. Expert rollouts test whether a model predicts what happens under the behavior policy. Compositional state–action evaluation instead tests whether the model predicts what hap-pens when observed actions are applied to familiar physical factors, including combinations not generated by the behavior policy. This is an essential requirement for model-based control, since planning must evaluate candidate actions other than the behavior action. 

## B ANY GOAL TASK DETAILS 

B.1 TWO ROOM AND ITS VARIANTS 

TwoRoom is a simple navigation environment with two rooms connected by a narrow slot. The agent must move through the slot and reach a target location, with little to no meaningful interaction with the environment beyond collision with walls. Because the dynamics are essentially planar motion, this environment isolates whether models can preserve action-conditioned movement under goal-biased trajectory data. 

TwoRoom Route is an interaction-light navigation variant. The agent must follow a piecewise-linear triangular route defined by a start point, an intermediate waypoint, and an invisible endpoint, rather than moving directly through the slot toward the target. Episodes mix clean, noisy, and variable-speed actions within the observed action support. This variant tests whether the model preserves planar action-conditioned dynamics when familiar positions and actions are recombined outside the dominant slot-crossing behavior of the base dataset. 18 B.2 PUSH T AND ITS VARIANTS 

PushT is a planar manipulation environment where the agent must push a T-shaped block into a fixed T-shaped goal pose. Unlike TwoRoom, the base task already requires meaningful contact, object translation, and object rotation. The expert trajectories are strongly goal-directed, coupling agent motion with the block pose and the target configuration. This environment tests whether world models learn action-conditioned contact dynamics rather than only the dominant expert pushing manifold. 

AvoidT is a non-interactive variant. The agent must avoid touching the physical T-block and move to one of four corner regions. This variant keeps the agent and action factors familiar, but changes the required behavior from goal-directed pushing to free-space navigation around the object. It primarily tests whether the model preserves the action effect when the expert pushing objective is no longer appropriate. 

StackT is an interaction-rich variant. The physical T-block must be placed so that its head overlaps with the fixed goal T, while its rotation is opposite to the original PushT goal orientation, creating a plus-shaped target configuration. This variant requires contact and rotation dynamics under a goal that differs from the original training objective. 

PushT Anti-goal is also interaction-rich and directly reverses the dominant goal bias in the base dataset. The physical T-block is initialized near the original PushT goal pose, but the agent must push it away from the fixed goal region and move it toward one of four corner regions, with rotation opposite to the original goal orientation. This tests whether the model can predict familiar pushing dynamics when the required behavior contradicts the original expert trajectory distribution. B.3 INSERT -P EG AND ITS VARIANTS 

Insert-Peg is a planar manipulation environment where an arm must move a peg toward a sampled slot and insert it into the target opening. The task involves articulated robot motion, object con-tact, and alignment between the peg and the slot. Expert trajectories are strongly structured around successful insertion behavior. This environment tests whether models can learn action-conditioned manipulation dynamics under goal-biased contact-rich data. 

Avoid-Peg is an interaction-light variant. The arm must move around the target box in a speci-fied hugging direction while avoiding contact with the peg. This changes the goal-conditioned arm behavior without requiring peg manipulation, testing whether the model can predict familiar arm motion under a non-insertion objective. 

Extract-Peg is an interaction-rich variant. The arm must extract the peg from the target box, move it through a waypoint, and place it at a destination, with episodes balanced between left- and right-side box placements. This reverses the dominant insertion behavior while keeping the peg, box, workspace, and action space familiar. B.4 OGB ENCH CUBE AND ITS VARIANTS 

Cube is a robot manipulation environment where the end-effector must interact with a cube and move it toward a target configuration. The base behavior follows a structured pick-and-place pat-tern, including approach, grasp, lift, transport, and release. This creates a goal-biased trajectory distribution in which robot motion, gripper state, cube pose, and target location are tightly coupled. The environment tests whether models learn manipulation dynamics beyond this dominant expert sequence. 

CubeHop is a non-interactive variant. The robot does not intentionally move the cube. Instead, the end-effector moves between waypoint and target positions while gradually changing its yaw. This variant changes the goal-conditioned end-effector behavior while avoiding object interaction. It tests whether the model can predict familiar robot motion and yaw changes outside the dominant cube-manipulation trajectory distribution. 

CubeDiagonal is an interaction-rich variant. The robot manipulates the cube using a diagonal grasp configuration, where the gripper contacts opposite edges of the cube instead of the standard parallel faces. This introduces a novel contact regime with different grasp geometry while keeping the ob-19 ject, task objective, and action space unchanged. It tests whether the model can compose familiar manipulation factors under unseen interaction configurations.

## F MODEL EXPLANATION 

Our model selection is designed to span several substantially different approaches to visual world modeling rather than to concentrate on a single architectural family. In particular, we evaluate both 25 joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) and generative visual world models. Within the JEPA family, we include methods that jointly learn perception and dynamics from the offline dataset as well as a method that keeps a large pretrained visual encoder frozen. Within the generative fam-ily, we include both a direct action-conditioned generator and a latent video diffusion model with an explicit visual encoder–decoder. This diversity is important for our central question: whether goal-induced combinatorial bias is specific to a particular representation or generative objective, or can arise more broadly when action-conditioned prediction is trained on a biased offline distribu-tion. For consistency, all models use frame skip 5, history length 3, and one-step prediction, except Sensorimotor-WM, where we keep the original history length 1 to preserve its inverse-dynamics setup. F.1 END -TO -E ND WORLD MODELS 

LeWorldModel (LeWM). LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026b) is an end-to-end joint-embedding predictive architecture trained directly from pixels and actions. An image encoder maps observations to compact latent representations, and an autoregressive transformer predictor models the next latent representation conditioned on the action. Actions are injected into each transformer layer through adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN-zero). Unlike approaches that rely on a frozen encoder, stop-gradient target network, or exponential-moving-average encoder, LeWM jointly optimizes its en-coder and predictor. A central design choice in LeWM is its anti-collapse regularization. In addition to the next-embedding mean-squared prediction loss, LeWM applies the Sketched-Isotropic-Gaussian Regu-larizer (SIGReg), which encourages the empirical latent distribution to match an isotropic Gaussian. The resulting model is particularly useful for our study because it represents a recent example of a fully end-to-end JEPA in which both the visual representation and dynamics predictor are shaped by the same offline trajectories. 

Sensorimotor World Model (SMWM). The Sensorimotor World Model (Ivashkov et al., 2026) is also an end-to-end JEPA, but uses a different principle to prevent representation collapse. Rather than prescribing the marginal geometry of the latent representation, SMWM introduces an inverse dy-namics model that predicts the executed action from consecutive latent states. The encoder, forward dynamics model, and inverse model are optimized jointly using forward-prediction and inverse-dynamics losses. The inverse objective requires consecutive representations to preserve information that is useful for recovering the action that produced the transition. Consequently, the learned representation is explic-itly biased toward controllable, action-relevant structure. Architecturally, SMWM adopts the LeWM encoder and transformer forward predictor, including AdaLN-zero action conditioning, while re-placing Gaussian latent regularization with inverse dynamics as its anti-collapse mechanism. This makes SMWM particularly relevant to our benchmark: it provides a strong test of whether explic-itly encouraging action-aware representations is sufficient to prevent failures induced by goal-biased state–action coverage. 

DINO World Model (DINO-WM). DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2025) separates representation learning from dynamics learning. Instead of jointly learning an image encoder from the offline con-trol dataset, it uses pretrained DINOv2 patch features as the observation representation and keeps the DINOv2 encoder frozen during both world-model training and evaluation. A causal ViT-based transition model then predicts future patch representations from a history of latent observations and actions. DINO-WM also differs in how actions enter the predictor. The action is first mapped through an MLP and the resulting action representation is concatenated to each spatial patch representation before the transformer processes the latent state. Thus, compared with LeWM and SMWM, DINO-WM provides a useful separation between perception and dynamics: the visual representation is inherited from large-scale pretraining, while only the action-conditioned transition model is learned from the potentially goal-biased offline trajectories. This lets us test whether combinatorial failures persist even when the visual encoder itself is not learned from the biased task dataset. 26 DriftWorld. DriftWorld (Lu et al., 2026) represents a substantially different, reconstruction-based family of world models. It is an action-conditioned generative model based on drifting rather than a JEPA or iterative diffusion model. Its core generator is a spatial–temporal U-Net that receives observation history, Gaussian noise, and a future action sequence and generates the corresponding future visual observations. Actions condition the U-Net frame-by-frame through FiLM, such that each future frame is explicitly conditioned on its corresponding action. For relatively simple simulated domains such as PushT, DriftWorld operates directly in pixel space and does not require a learned visual state encoder between the observations and the action-conditioned generator. Its drifting objective moves generated samples toward ground-truth future observations and away from generated negatives, enabling future video generation in a single for-ward pass at inference time. DriftWorld therefore provides an important contrast to the latent JEPA models: prediction and visual generation are implemented by a single action-conditioned generative backbone rather than an explicit encoder followed by a latent dynamics predictor. 

IRASim. IRASim (Zhu et al., 2024) represents the latent generative end of our model suite. It for-mulates world modeling for robot manipulation as trajectory-conditioned video generation. Frames are first compressed using a pretrained VAE, after which a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) models fu-ture video latents conditioned on historical observations and a robot action trajectory. The generated latents are subsequently decoded back to RGB observations. A defining component of IRASim is its fine-grained action conditioning. Rather than conditioning the complete video only on a global trajectory embedding, IRASim can associate individual actions with their corresponding generated frames through frame-level adaptive normalization. This archi-tecture gives us a generative model that is structurally different from DriftWorld: visual prediction occurs in an encoded latent space, the dynamics generator is a diffusion transformer, and an explicit encoder–decoder surrounds the dynamics model. We evaluated all true-state dynamics models using the same goal-conditioned receding-horizon con-troller. For each episode, the initial state and goal were sampled from the same dataset trajectory with a temporal offset of 25 environment steps. Planning was performed in the normalized action space using the cross-entropy method (CEM). Each candidate consisted of five action blocks, where each block contained five consecutive environment actions, yielding an effective planning horizon of 25 environment steps. At every planning round, CEM evaluated 300 candidate sequences for 30 iterations and refitted a factorized Gaussian distribution to the 30 lowest-cost elites. The objective was the squared Euclidean distance between the terminal predicted state and the goal in the model’s normalized state space. The final mean sequence was executed open-loop for all five action blocks before replanning. Episodes were evaluated for at most 50 environment steps, corresponding to at most two planning rounds.

I.3 ADDITIONAL OBSERVATIONS 

Prediction Space Affects Metric Interpretation We do not use the rollout results to directly rank JEPA-style and generative world models. These models predict in different spaces: JEPA-style mod-els are evaluated in latent space, whereas generative models are evaluated in pixel or reconstruction space. As a result, absolute prediction errors are not directly comparable across model families, and we primarily interpret rollout degradation relative to each model’s own base-task performance. Under this relative comparison, generative models often show smaller rollout degradation under some goal shifts, while several JEPA-style models show larger increases. However, this tendency does not translate consistently to planning: a model can show stable rollout error but poor MPC success, or larger rollout degradation while remaining competitive in control. We therefore treat prediction-space effects as an interpretation caveat rather than a model-family ranking. The results suggest that rollout robustness and downstream control utility are related but distinct properties. 

Oracle state access does not guarantee better planning. Interestingly, access to the oracle state does not always lead to stronger planning performance. In Cube, several end-to-end models achieve higher planning success than their oracle-state counterparts, both on the base task and on CubeDiag-onal. This should not be interpreted as evidence that learned latents are generally superior to physical state representations, since the end-to-end and oracle predictors differ in more than just their input space. Rather, these results suggest that state observability and planning suitability are distinct properties. Oracle states preserve the underlying physical factors, but the resulting geometry may not be the most favorable one for the planner’s cost and optimization procedure. Conversely, a learned repre-sentation may, in some cases, emphasize task-relevant variation or compress nuisance factors in a way that makes goal-directed optimization easier. We therefore treat planning performance as de-pending not only on how much state information is available, but also on how that information is organized in the representation space. 

## J TOWARD COMPOSITIONAL GENERALIZATION IN WORLD MODELS 

Our experiments empirically show that removing visual abstraction is not sufficient for reliable goal-shift generalization: free-space dynamics often transfer across new state–action compositions, whereas contact-rich dynamics remain substantially harder even when the predictor receives task-32 relevant physical state directly. This raises a dynamics-level question: what structure must a world model learn for its dynamics to remain reusable under compositional shifts? Ideally, we want to solve AnyGoal by learning reusable dynamics that extrapolate to the combi-natorial space. That is, we want a learned transition function ˆT that matches the observed training transitions on Ctr , while also predicting correctly on novel compositions in C× \C tr . We separate this question into two parts: identifying reusable mechanisms on the training support, and extrapolating those mechanisms to novel compositions. 

Note. The discussion below should be read as a conditional statement, not a claim that reusable mechanisms are identifiable from expert rollouts alone. Rather, we give one possible sufficient condition: if sparse regime-conditioned local mechanisms are identified on training support and test goals query only locally covered active inputs, then the model can generalize to globally novel state–action compositions. J.1 IDENTIFYING REUSABLE MECHANISMS ON THE TRAINING SUPPORT 

To discuss identifiability, we first need to specify what structure the true transition may have. One observation from our results is that world models often face multiple regimes , such as free motion, contact, grasping, or collision. In a single-regime setting with little interaction, such as free-space navigation of an agent, one may expect a transition of the form zt+1 = f (zt) + g(at). In this favorable case, identifiability and compositional generalization are easier to obtain, for example through an additive function class (Dong & Ma, 2022; Lachapelle et al., 2023; Wiedemer et al., 2024b;a; Mahajan et al., 2025). Discussion. This construction should be read as an illustrative sufficient condition rather than a complete solution. Sparse local mechanisms reduce a high-dimensional global coverage problem to smaller local coverage problems, but they do not by themselves solve mechanism identification, regime discovery, or extrapolation to genuinely new local interactions. The broader challenge is to develop world-model architectures, objectives, and data-collection strategies that identify reusable action-conditioned mechanisms and impose enough structure on their interactions to support gener-alization beyond expert rollouts. 

## K ANY GOAL LIBRARY AND REPRODUCIBILITY 

We release AnyGoal as an open-source Python library for collecting goal-shifted datasets, train-ing world models, and evaluating rollout prediction and MPC planning. The library provides both Python APIs and a unified CLI, allowing researchers to reproduce the benchmark experiments and evaluate additional models under the same protocol. AnyGoal builds on Stable-WorldModel (Maes et al., 2026a) and retains the original model implementations and numerical protocols. Complete installation instructions and API references are available in the online documentation. 35 K.1 INSTALLATION AND DATASET ACCESS 

The library can be installed with evaluation dependencies using uv .         

> pip install uv uv venv --python 3.10 source .venv/bin/activate uv pip install "anygoal [eval ]"

The default installation provides dataset access, collection, validation, and simulation environments, while the eval and all extras add evaluation and training dependencies, respectively. The CLI exposes a registry of supported datasets and models.                      

> #Inspect available datasets and models
> anygoal list datasets
> anygoal list models #Inspect default data and checkpoint paths
> anygoal config path data
> anygoal config path checkpoints

Datasets and pretrained checkpoints can be downloaded from Hugging Face through the artifact interface.                       

> #Download training and test datasets
> anygoal artifacts download --dataset pusht
> anygoal artifacts download --dataset avoidt #Download apretrained model checkpoint
> anygoal artifacts download --model lewm

Model integrations use the corresponding upstream implementations. Their source code can be in-stalled separately.      

> anygoal models list
> anygoal models install lewm

Dataset Collection Beyond the released evaluation episodes, AnyGoal provides tools for generat-ing additional goal-shifted trajectories. Dataset collection is available through both Python and CLI interfaces, which use the same in-package collectors. The following example collects 400 Avoid-T episodes using eight parallel environments.    

> import anygoal
> dataset =anygoal .collect( "avoidt", num_episodes=400, num_parallel=8, seed=0, )

The equivalent collection procedure can be invoked from the terminal.               

> anygoal collect avoidt --num-episodes 400 --num-parallel 8#Collect another goal-shifted variant
> anygoal collect cubediagonal --output /path/to/cubediagonal.h5

36 K.2 WORLD -M ODEL EVALUATION 

AnyGoal provides standardized interfaces for evaluating a base-trained world model on both its original task and goal-shifted variants. The same model checkpoint can be used for rollout prediction and downstream MPC planning, enabling comparisons across evaluation metrics. 

Planning evaluation. The following commands evaluate LeWM on Push-T and Avoid-T.                      

> #Original training goal
> anygoal eval planning --model lewm --dataset pusht --seed 0#Unseen goal
> anygoal eval planning --model lewm --dataset avoidt --seed 0

Rollout evaluation. The corresponding rollout-prediction evaluation uses the same model and dataset identifiers.                 

> anygoal eval rollout --model lewm --dataset pusht --seed 0
> anygoal eval rollout --model lewm --dataset avoidt --seed 0

By default, the evaluation commands resolve the checkpoint associated with the selected model and base-task family through the shared artifact catalog. Users can also specify a checkpoint explicitly with --checkpoint or download missing artifacts with --download .The evaluation interfaces additionally support explicit random seeds, allowing comparisons across repeated runs. K.3 REPRODUCIBILITY AND EXTENSIBILITY 

AnyGoal organizes datasets, checkpoints, and supported model implementations through shared registries. Dataset and model identifiers, artifact locations, and evaluation configurations can be specified explicitly, reducing ambiguity when reproducing experiments. The library exposes its core functionality through Python APIs, including anygoal.collect , anygoal.train ,

anygoal.evaluate rollout , and anygoal.plan . These interfaces complement the CLI and support integration into existing research workflows. The released benchmark contains 400 episodes per goal-shifted variant, while the collection tools allow researchers to construct additional evaluation datasets. Together with the standardized eval-uation interfaces, this enables future world models to be tested on the same controlled goal shifts without reproducing the environment and evaluation infrastructure. The source code, configuration files, and implementation instructions are available at github.com/galilai-group/AnyGoal. 37
