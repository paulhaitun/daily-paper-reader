Abstract 

World models have recently emerged as a promising direction toward developing gener-alist agents capable of understanding, predicting, and interacting with diverse environments. However, despite growing interest, the concept of a world model remains loosely defined, en-compassing approaches ranging from predictive dynamics models and learned simulators to models that jointly generate future observations and actions. This introductory paper aims to clarify the concept of world models and provide a unified perspective on their role in develop-ing generalist agents. Starting from the partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) framework, we connect world modeling to classical control, dynamical systems, signal process-ing, and reinforcement learning. We introduce different approaches to world modeling, empha-sizing the knowledge they capture, their learning objectives, and how they support decision-making. We further explore the relationship between world modeling and policy learning, highlighting how these capabilities can be unified through conditional prediction without re-quiring separate models. Rather than offering an exhaustive survey of existing methods, this paper focuses on fundamental concepts, modeling principles, and connections, with the goal of providing an accessible and unified introduction to world models for generalist agents.

# 1 Introduction 

A long-standing goal of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) is to develop gener-alist agents that can perceive, reason, and act effectively across diverse environments and tasks [1]. Achieving this requires more than recognizing patterns in observations or reproducing actions from demonstrations. An agent must acquire knowledge about how the world works, how it changes, and how its actions influence future outcomes. Recently, world models have emerged as a popular and promising direction toward this goal. Advances in generative modeling, video pre-diction, robotics, and large language models (LLMs) have stimulated growing interest in building agents that can learn how the world works and use this knowledge to guide their actions [2, 3]. This direction is particularly relevant to physical intelligence, where agents must understand and interact with the physical world across diverse tasks and embodiments [4]. The growing avail-ability of images, videos, and robotic interaction data provides new opportunities to acquire such world knowledge at scale [5, 6]. Despite this growing interest, the meaning of world model remains surprisingly ambiguous. The term has been used to describe a wide range of models, including predictive dynamics models, gen-erative video models, learned simulators, and models that jointly predict future observations and actions. Even action policies can be viewed as implicitly containing a model of the world. These approaches differ substantially in their learning objectives, representations, architectures, and in-tended applications. The idea of learning internal models of the environment has a long history in AI and control. Model-based control and reinforcement learning have traditionally used mod-els of system dynamics to predict the consequences of actions and support planning [7, 8]. The term world model gained renewed attention with a series of recent work [9–12]. More recently, ad-vances in video generation [13] and world-action models [14] have further blurred the boundaries between predicting the world, simulating interactions, and generating actions. To clarify these concepts, it is useful to consider the two words in world model . A model , broadly speaking, is an informative representation of an object or system, constructed to describe, under-stand, or predict its behavior [15]. A model need not reproduce every detail of reality, but rather capture selected properties relevant to its intended purpose. A mathematical model of atmospheric dynamics, for example, captures aspects of the physical system relevant to weather forecasting. A related concept is simulation , which involves constructing a model of a system and conducting ex-periments with that model to understand its behavior or evaluate alternative strategies [16]. The word world highlights a broader ambition: learning knowledge that is useful across diverse en-vironments and tasks, rather than modeling a narrowly specified system for a single application. For generalist agents, such knowledge may include the structure of environments, the evolution of their states, the consequences of actions, and the relationships between observations, actions, and outcomes. Importantly, this does not require a world model to capture every aspect of the envi-ronment. Different models may represent different components of the world, at different levels of abstraction, depending on what information is useful for prediction and decision-making. Using the physical world as an illustrative setting, passive observations (e.g., images and videos) and action-labeled trajectories (e.g., robotic interactions) provide complementary learning signals for acquiring world knowledge and learning action policies. World modeling and policy learning are closely connected and may share representations, learning objectives, or model architectures. The acquired world knowledge can support inference-time planning, simulated experience for policy improvement through rein-forcement learning, or direct action through a learned policy. Together, these complementary ap-proaches support the development of generalist agents capable of effective decision-making across diverse environments, tasks, and embodiments. whose objective is to maximize rewards across diverse environments and tasks. We formalize this setting using partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs) [8], which provide a uni-fied framework for describing environment states, observations, actions, and dynamics. We adopt a broad definition of a world model as a learned representation that captures aspects of an envi-ronment’s structure, state, or dynamics to support understanding, prediction, or decision-making. Under this perspective, different approaches to world modeling can be characterized by the en-vironmental knowledge they capture and the relationships they learn. For example, observation-space world models predict future observations, while latent-space models learn representations and their evolution. Passive predictive models learn temporal regularities from observations [17], whereas action-conditioned models predict how the environment responds to actions [10]. A policy, in contrast, selects actions based on the available information and task. Although policy learning does not necessarily involve explicit world modeling, effective decision-making often requires implicitly capturing relevant aspects of the environment and its dynamics. Thus, world modeling and policy learning represent distinct but closely related capabilities, both relying on knowledge of the world to varying degrees to support effective behavior . As illustrated in Figure 1, they can leverage diverse data sources and shared representations, while learned world models 3can further support inference-time planning and policy improvement through simulated expe-rience. More fundamentally, world modeling and policy learning can be connected through a unified probabilistic perspective. A policy predicts which action to take given the current con-text, a forward dynamics model predicts the consequences of an action, and an inverse dynamics model predicts the action associated with an observed transition. These can be viewed as different conditional distributions derived from a joint distribution over actions and future observations. This perspective motivates recent world-action models , which integrate world prediction and action generation through shared representations, joint learning objectives, or interacting generative ar-chitectures [14, 18]. LLMs provide another intriguing perspective. Trained on internet-scale text through next-token prediction, followed by fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, LLMs have demonstrated broad capabilities in reasoning, tool use, and interactive problem-solving [19]. Although next-token prediction is not explicitly designed for learning environment dynamics, the training data contains extensive descriptions of events, actions, interactions, and their consequences. Depending on the conditioning context and prediction target, the same autoregressive objective can implicitly provide supervision for policies, forward dynamics, inverse dynamics, and other forms of predic-tion. This suggests that general-purpose predictive learning may acquire implicit representations of the world [20], which can be further strengthened through interaction-based training [21–23]. Our goal in this paper is to provide a unified introduction to world models from the perspec-tive of learning generalist agents. Rather than focusing on specific architectures or implementa-tion details, we emphasize the underlying modeling principles, aiming to clarify the relationships among different approaches to world modeling, their learning objectives, and their roles in sup-porting decision-making. Inspired by the success of large-scale predictive learning in LLMs, we also highlight how these principles may enable generalist agents to leverage diverse data sources, ranging from passive videos and egocentric human observations to action-labeled demonstrations and interaction trajectories. By extracting complementary learning signals from such data, world modeling and policy learning may jointly support the acquisition of transferable knowledge across environments, tasks, and embodiments. We do not attempt an exhaustive survey of the rapidly growing literature, for which we refer readers to several recent surveys [3, 24]. Instead, we focus on representative approaches that illustrate the key concepts and connections. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We first formulate the generalist-agent problem using the POMDP framework in Section 2.1. Following the perspective of [25], we then draw connections to classical control, dynamical systems, signal processing, and reinforcement learning in Section 2.2. In Section 3, we introduce policy learning and different approaches to world modeling, highlighting their learning objectives, representations, and roles in supporting decision-making. Finally, we conclude in Section 4, we conclude with a discussion of some other key challenges and future directions, including generalization across embodiments, scaling data and learning signals, state and action representations, high-level reasoning, and the evaluation of world models for decision-making.

## 2.2 Sequential Decision-Making: Control, Estimation, and Learning 

The formulation (5) connects to a rich body of work on dynamical systems, control, filtering, sys-tem identification, and reinforcement learning. These fields study different aspects of sequential interaction and can be understood within a common framework by considering two fundamental questions: whether the underlying state is directly observable and whether the environment dy-namics are known. These distinctions help clarify the relationships among classical approaches and their relevance to learning generalist agents. 2.2.3 Unknown Dynamics: System Identification and Reinforcement Learning. 

When the transition dynamics are unknown, the agent must learn about the environment from observed data or interactions. A classical approach is system identification , which aims to estimate a model of the underlying dynamics from observed trajectories [35]. For example, consider the above discussed fully observed linear dynamical system st+1 = As t + Ba t + wt, where A and B

are unknown dynamics matrices and wt represents process noise. Given trajectories of states and actions, these matrices can be estimated through least squares regression by minimizing the one-step state prediction error. When the states are only partially observed, classical methods such as maximum likelihood estimation and subspace identification can recover a state-space model from input-output trajectories. For nonlinear systems, the dynamics can be approximated using nonlinear parametric models, including neural networks, learned from observed transitions. More generally, system identification seeks to learn predictive relationships between the agent’s actions and the evolution of the environment. Depending on the available observations and model assumptions, this may involve predicting future states from state-action pairs or predicting future observations from interaction histories. The latter can be implemented using latent-state repre-sentations that summarize past interactions. Once learned, such models can support prediction, simulation, planning, and model predictive control. This establishes a fundamental connection between learning environment dynamics and model-based decision-making. 

Reinforcement learning (RL) addresses the problem of learning effective decision-making poli-cies through interaction with the environment [8]. Its objective is to maximize the expected cumu-lative reward, as formulated in (5). While this formulation accommodates multiple environments and tasks, classical RL often considers a fixed environment and reward function, and seeks to learn an optimal policy from experience when the environment dynamics are unknown. Unlike system identification, which focuses on learning a predictive model of the environment, RL directly op-timizes decision-making performance. Nevertheless, the two are closely connected: learning the environment dynamics can support policy optimization, although an explicit dynamics model is not always necessary. This distinction leads to two major approaches to RL. Model-based RL explicitly learns or uses a predictive model of the environment and leverages it for planning or policy improvement. When the dynamics are unknown, learning such a model is closely related to system identification, al-though the model may be optimized for decision-making rather than prediction accuracy alone. The learned model can predict future states, observations, or rewards, allowing the agent to evalu-ate candidate actions through simulated rollouts. In contrast, model-free RL learns policies or value functions directly from experience, without explicitly constructing a predictive dynamics model. Thus, both approaches aim to improve decision-making, but differ in whether and how an envi-ronment model is explicitly learned and used. 10 Although RL provides a general mathematical framework for learning through interaction, ap-plying it to generalist agents introduces significant practical challenges. • First, many online RL algorithms require extensive trial-and-error interactions to acquire effec-tive behaviors. In controlled settings, these interactions can be generated efficiently through simulators that support repeated trials, environment resets, and parallel exploration. However, constructing sufficiently accurate simulators can be difficult for complex physical environments, particularly those involving diverse objects, contact dynamics, and human interactions [36]. While simulation is highly effective for certain tasks, transferring learned behaviors to the real world introduces additional challenges due to model mismatch and distribution shifts, known as the sim-to-real gap [37]. • Second, learning directly in real-world environments can be expensive, slow, and potentially unsafe. Physical interactions consume time and resources, while exploratory actions may dam-age objects or violate safety constraints. Moreover, many real-world situations cannot be reset to identical initial conditions, making repeated experimentation difficult. These limitations are particularly important for generalist agents, which must operate across diverse tasks and envi-ronments rather than repeatedly train for a single task. • Third, conventional RL formulations often assume that the agent can obtain reward feedback and collect trajectories through interaction with the target environment. In contrast, generalist agents may need to acquire transferable knowledge from heterogeneous sources, including pas-sive observations, human demonstrations, previously collected interaction data, and experience from other environments. Such data may not contain explicit rewards or may not be generated by the agent’s current policy. Offline RL, imitation learning, multi-task RL, and model-based ap-proaches provide important mechanisms for addressing aspects of these challenges [28, 38], but efficiently integrating diverse data sources and generalizing to unseen tasks and environments remain central research problems. These considerations motivate a broader perspective on generalist-agent learning. Beyond opti-mizing a policy through trial and error, an agent must determine what knowledge to acquire, how to learn it from different forms of data, and how to reuse it for prediction, planning, and adaptation. This perspective connects classical system identification and reinforcement learning to modern ap-proaches for learning world models, action policies, and transferable representations. 

# 3 Learning to Act: Policies and World Models 

The preceding section connects generalist agents to classical frameworks for control, state estima-tion, system identification, and reinforcement learning. These frameworks characterize different aspects of sequential interaction, including action selection, hidden-state estimation, and learning unknown dynamics. Building on these foundations, we now examine how generalist agents can learn policies and world models from data to operate across diverse environments and tasks. We organize the discussion around three complementary approaches, as illustrated in Figure 3, which differ in their prediction targets, conditioning information, and representations. The first directly learns action policies from demonstrations, without explicitly modeling environment dy-namics. The second learns predictive world models in observation or latent spaces to support planning, control, and policy improvement. ## 3.1 Direct Policy Learning from Demonstrations 

We begin with the most direct approach to learning an agent: learning an action policy from demonstrations without explicitly modeling the environment dynamics. Recall from Section 2 that a policy π(at | ht, g ) specifies a conditional distribution over actions given the interaction history 13 and task. Given the action examples in Dact , defined in (13), we seek to learn a parameterized policy πθ(at | ht, g ), where θ denotes the model parameters. A standard approach is behavior cloning , which formulates policy learning as a supervised learn-ing problem by maximizing the likelihood of demonstrated actions [39]:

This connects policy learning to other conditional prediction problems, including the world-model learning approaches discussed later. In practice, such policies can be implemented using different architectures, including Trans-formers and diffusion models, with corresponding learning objectives. When demonstrations are generated by a single behavior policy π, the learned policy πθ aims to approximate π on histories represented in the training data. More generally, modern datasets often combine demonstrations from multiple human operators, robots, or algorithms. In this case, the learned policy approxi-mates the conditional action distribution induced by the combined dataset, which may reflect a mixture of different behavior policies. Many recent agent models can be understood as extensions of this direct supervised policy learning paradigm. They differ primarily in the information used to condition the policy, the pa-rameterization of the action distribution, and the underlying model architecture, while sharing the common goal of learning actions directly from demonstrations. A common approach is to pa-rameterize the policy autoregressively by decomposing the conditional action distribution into a sequence of action tokens or action chunks, analogous to next-token prediction in language mod-els. Decision Transformer [40] formulates offline reinforcement learning as conditional sequence modeling by predicting actions conditioned on past observations, actions, and a desired return-to-go. More recent robotic foundation models, including RT-1 [41], RT-2 [42], and OpenVLA [43], similarly represent robot actions as sequences of discrete tokens and train the policy using au-toregressive maximum likelihood. Despite their architectural differences and conditioning infor-mation, these methods all optimize the policy directly through supervised learning rather than explicitly modeling the environment dynamics, learning a value function, or directly maximiz-ing cumulative reward. For continuous and multimodal action spaces, the policy can instead be parameterized by a conditional diffusion model. Diffusion Policy [44] represents the policy as a conditional denoising diffusion process that generates action sequences conditioned on the inter-action history. Although the training objective is a denoising (or score-matching) loss instead of the action-level log-likelihood in standard behavior cloning, it remains a form of supervised policy learning. The diffusion parameterization is particularly effective for continuous, high-dimensional, and multimodal action distributions. Despite their effectiveness, direct supervised policy learning faces several important limita-tions. First, learning a broadly capable policy requires demonstrations covering a sufficiently di-verse range of environments, tasks, embodiments, and behaviors. Second, the learned policy is trained on histories generated by the behavior policies but is deployed on histories induced by its own actions. Prediction errors can therefore cause the agent to encounter situations poorly rep-resented in the training data, potentially leading to compounding errors over long horizons [45]. Third, because behavior cloning optimizes agreement with demonstrated actions rather than cu-mulative reward, its performance depends strongly on the quality and coverage of the demonstra-tions, with no explicit mechanism for improving beyond the demonstrated behavior. To partially address these limitations, many recent VLA models leverage knowledge acquired through large-scale multimodal pretraining. For example, RT-2 and OpenVLA build on pretrained 14 vision-language models and adapt them to robotic control using action-labeled demonstrations. The pretrained models provide rich semantic and visual representations learned from large-scale image-text data, while robotic demonstrations ground these representations in executable actions. By transferring knowledge from multimodal pretraining, these approaches can improve general-ization to new instructions, objects, and visual configurations, reducing reliance on task-specific robot demonstrations. Nevertheless, pretrained representations alone do not eliminate the limitations of direct pol-icy learning. In particular, these policies do not explicitly model how the environment evolves in response to actions, making it difficult to predict the consequences of alternative behaviors or sys-tematically improve through planning. This motivates approaches that learn predictive world mod-els , which can support reasoning about future states, evaluating candidate actions, and improving policies beyond direct imitation. 

## 3.2 Learning World Models for Planning and Control 

Direct policy learning predicts actions from the interaction history and task, without explicitly modeling how the environment responds to those actions. An alternative is to learn a world model 

that captures knowledge about the environment to support prediction, planning, or policy im-provement. As discussed in the introduction, the notion of a world model is broad and need not be restricted to predicting environment dynamics. Such models may capture different aspects of the world, including its structure, states, relationships, and dynamics, at different levels of abstrac-tion. In this section, we focus on a particularly important class of world models, dynamics models ,which predict how observations or learned representations evolve over time, either with or with-out conditioning on actions. We introduce observation-space and latent-space formulations and discuss how they can support decision-making. 

3.2.1 Observation-Space World Models 

As discussed in the preceding section, under partial observability, the underlying environment state is unavailable, and system identification can instead be formulated as predicting future obser-vations from the interaction history and actions. This motivates action-conditioned observational world models, which learn the observable consequences of actions without explicitly modeling the underlying state transitions [46]. 

Definition 2 (Action-conditioned observational dynamics model) . An action-conditioned obser-vational dynamics model is a probabilistic model pθ(ot+1 | ht, a t) that predicts the next observation conditioned on the interaction history ht and current action at.

For learning a generalist agent that maximizes the cumulative reward in (5), accurately predicting future observations may be unnecessary. Instead, an alternative approach is to learn a latent repre-sentation that preserves information relevant to future prediction and decision-making, together with a predictive model that describes how this representation evolves over time [10, 50, 51]. 

Definition 4 (Latent encoder) . A latent encoder ϕ is a mapping that maps the interaction history ht =(o0, a 0, . . . , a t−1, o t) to a latent representation zt = ϕ(ht). The encoder summarizes information relevant to future prediction and decision-making. 

In practice, the encoder may operate on the current observation alone, zt = ϕ(ot), as in JEPA-style representation-learning methods [12, 52, 53], or incorporate the interaction history to account for partial observability. For example, a recurrent encoder can update the latent representation as 

zt = ϕ(zt−1, a t−1, o t), as in recurrent state-space world models [10, 11]. The latter can be viewed as a recursive implementation of a history-dependent encoder. 

Remark 2 (Relation to the underlying state s). The latent representation zt should not be confused with the underlying state st. As illustrated in Figure 3(a), the state st is part of the environment and evolves according to the transition dynamics p(st+1 | st, a t, e ), whereas zt is a learned repre-sentation constructed from the agent’s observations and interaction history. In fully observable environments, zt may approximate the underlying state or a lower-dimensional abstraction of it. Under partial observability, however, the current observation generally does not contain sufficient information to determine the underlying state. A history-dependent latent representation may therefore approximate the belief state p(st | ht, g ) or encode other predictive information relevant to future outcomes and decisions. The pair (zt, g ) must retain the relevant information if it is to replace (ht, g ). Consequently, there need not be a one-to-one correspondence between zt and st.A latent representation alone does not specify how the environment evolves. To model its evo-lution, we introduce latent dynamics models that predict future latent representations, either with or without conditioning on the agent’s actions. 

Definition 5 (Latent dynamics models) . An action-conditioned latent dynamics model is a proba-bilistic model pθ(zt+1 | zt, a t) that predicts the next latent representation conditioned on the current latent representation and action. An action-unconditioned latent dynamics model instead predicts future la-tent representations without conditioning on actions, represented by pθ(zt+1 | z≤t).

As with observational dynamics models, we use the same notation pθ for both formulations, without implying that they share parameters. For passive dynamics learning, latent representa-tions are constructed from observations alone, such as zt = ϕ(ot) or zt = ϕ(o≤t), since action labels are unavailable. For simplicity, we omit task conditioning, although it can be included when useful. Given a fixed encoder ϕ, learning latent dynamics is similar to learning observational dynam-ics, except that prediction takes place in a latent representation space. Specifically, interaction ex-amples in Ddyn can be mapped to latent transitions (zt, a t, z t+1 ), providing supervision for action-conditioned latent dynamics. Similarly, passive observations in Dpass can support latent prediction without action labels. As for learning observational dynamics, probabilistic models may be trained through maximum likelihood or other generative objectives, while deterministic models typically use regression or representation-prediction losses [10, 51, 52]. Representative passive approaches include V-JEPA [53, 54], which learns predictive representations from videos without action anno-tations or pixel-level reconstruction. In practice, the latent encoder and dynamics model are often learned jointly rather than sequen-tially. This introduces an important challenge: accurate latent prediction alone does not guarantee 17 that the learned representation captures meaningful or decision-relevant information about the en-vironment. For example, a constant encoder zt = ϕ(ht) = c admits perfect prediction of zt+1 = c,but provides no useful information for decision-making. More generally, the learned representa-tion may preserve easily predictable features while discarding information important for planning and control. This motivates the notion of control sufficiency , discussed next. 

Definition 6 (Control-sufficient latent representation) . A latent representation zt = ϕ(ht) is control sufficient if it preserves the information necessary for optimal decision-making. Specifically, an agent op-erating on the latent representation can achieve the same optimal performance as an agent operating on the full interaction history in the original decision-making problem (5) .

This definition is closely related to several classical theories of state representation. In fully observable MDPs, state abstraction seeks lower-dimensional representations that preserve value functions or optimal policies [55]. MDP homomorphisms characterize abstractions that preserve transition and reward structures, thereby inducing equivalent planning problems in abstract state spaces [56]. Closely related, bisimulation characterizes states that are behaviorally indistinguish-able by requiring equivalent rewards and transition behavior under each action [57, 58]. For par-tially observable environments, predictive state representations represent the state through pre-dictions of future observable events rather than unobserved physical states [59]. Modern latent-space world models can be understood as learning approximate predictive rep-resentations using neural networks. Reconstruction-based models, such as PlaNet and Dreamer, learn latent dynamics together with observation reconstruction objectives [10, 11]. JEPA-style approaches predict future latent representations without requiring pixel-level reconstruction [12, 52, 53]. A key challenge in such approaches is representation collapse, where the encoder maps different observations to nearly identical representations. Recent work, such as LeWorldModel, addresses this issue by regularizing the latent representations toward an isotropic Gaussian distri-bution, enabling stable end-to-end learning of action-conditioned latent dynamics [60]. Control-oriented approaches instead learn latent dynamics together with decision-relevant quantities such as rewards or values, without requiring full observation reconstruction [51]. The resulting la-tent dynamics models can support inference-time planning, including model predictive control, or provide predictive rollouts for policy optimization through reinforcement learning. 

3.2.3 Learning Policies from Predictive Models 

Once a predictive world model has been learned, either in observation space or in latent space, it can be used to construct a policy for the original decision-making objective in (5). A common approach follows the principle of certainty equivalence, treating the learned model as an approx-imation to the true environment and optimizing decisions accordingly. More generally, model-based methods may also account for prediction uncertainty and model errors. Depending on how the predictive model is used, we distinguish three approaches: inference-time planning, policy learning through simulated experience, and control from passive predictive models. For simplic-ity, we illustrate the main ideas using latent dynamics, although analogous principles apply to observation-space models. 

Inference-time Planning A first approach is to select actions through online planning with the learned dynamics model. At each time step, the agent predicts the outcomes of candidate action sequences and selects actions that optimize a desired objective. For example, consider a goal-reaching task, where zg denotes the latent representation of a desired goal, obtained using a compatible encoder or goal representation. Other planning approaches, such as MuZero, combine learned latent dynamics with tree search to select actions [50]. 

Policy Learning through Simulated Experience. Instead of planning from scratch at every deci-sion step, another approach is to use the learned predictive model as a simulator for policy learn-ing. By generating synthetic trajectories through the learned dynamics, the agent can optimize a parameterized policy using reinforcement learning algorithms [8, 63] without requiring an equiv-alent number of interactions with the real environment. Importantly, policy optimization need not depend on the internal structure of the learned world model. Standard reinforcement learning methods, such as policy gradient, actor-critic, or value-based learning, can be applied to simulated trajectories [7, 64]. For example, given a learned latent dynamics model pθ(zt+1 | zt, a t) and a policy πθ(at | zt, g ), the agent can generate simulated trajectories according to 

at ∼ πθ(· | zt, g ), zt+1 ∼ pθ(· | zt, a t). (20) To optimize the policy, simulated trajectories must also provide a reward signal, which can be obtained from a learned reward predictor or an appropriate task-specific evaluator such as using VLM. Reinforcement learning can then improve the policy using simulated experience, rather than merely imitating demonstrated actions. Recent work extends this paradigm to reinforcement fine-tuning of pretrained VLA poli-cies [65–67]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of world models to reduce costly real-world interactions during policy improvement, while highlighting the importance of accurate simulated dynamics for reliable policy optimization. Alternatively, when the learned dynamics are differentiable, policy optimization can exploit gradients propagated through predicted state transitions, allowing the model to participate directly in the optimization process [11]. In both cases, optimization is amortized into the policy parameters, allowing actions to be selected di-rectly at inference time without repeatedly solving a planning problem. These approaches can potentially improve beyond the behavior represented in demonstration data by evaluating alter-native actions in the learned model. However, their effectiveness depends on the accuracy of the predictive model, particularly for trajectories outside the training distribution. 

Control from Action-unconditioned Predictive Models. The preceding approaches rely on action-conditioned predictive models that estimate the consequences of candidate actions. In contrast, action-unconditioned predictive models learn how observations or latent representa-tions evolve without access to the actions responsible for the transitions. Consequently, predicted future trajectories do not directly specify how an agent should act to realize them. 19 One approach to bridging this gap is to learn an inverse dynamics model that infers the action associated with a transition between latent representations [68, 69]. This allows agents to lever-age predictive world models learned from abundant passive observations, while using relatively limited action-labeled data to learn how to translate predicted transitions into executable actions. 

Definition 7 (Inverse dynamics model) . An inverse dynamics model predicts the action associated with a transition: in observation space, it can be represented as pθ(at | o≤t, o t+1 ), while in latent space, it can be represented by a probabilistic model pθ(at | zt, z t+1 ).

Both formulations can be learned from action-labeled transitions in Ddyn , using at as the pre-diction target. Once learned, an inverse dynamics model can serve several purposes. First, given a trajectory predicted by a passive world model, it can infer candidate actions corresponding to successive transitions, potentially enabling the agent to execute the predicted trajectory. Second, it can infer missing action labels from passive observation sequences in Dpass , thereby expand-ing the available action-labeled training data for learning action-conditioned dynamics models or policies [68, 69]. However, passive predictions are not necessarily controllable: some predicted transitions may be infeasible, and multiple actions may produce similar outcomes. Consequently, inverse dynamics requires sufficient action-grounded supervision, and effective control may need additional feedback or feasibility constraints. An alternative is to infer latent action representa-tions from passive observations. For example, Genie learns latent actions from unlabeled videos and uses them to condition a generative dynamics model, enabling controllable video prediction without explicit action labels [2]. This illustrates how passive observations can support learning interactive world models, although grounding learned latent actions in executable commands re-mains an additional challenge. 

## 3.3 World-Action Models: Joint Learning of World Models and Action Policies 

The previous subsections introduce two complementary approaches: learning action policies di-rectly from demonstrations, and learning world models, particularly dynamics models, to support planning and policy improvement. Although these approaches emphasize different learning objec-tives, world modeling and policy learning are closely related. A world model captures knowledge about the environment, while a policy selects actions to achieve a task. Even when a policy is learned without an explicit world model, effective action selection may require implicitly captur-ing aspects of the environment and its dynamics. This motivates a third approach: jointly learning world models and action policies so that predictive modeling and action learning can reinforce one another. Such approaches are often referred to as world-action models [14, 70]. 

Joint Learning through Shared or Interacting Representations. A general perspective on jointly learning world models and policies is to consider the joint conditional distribution of future obser-vations and actions, p(ot+1 , a t | ht, g ). This distribution captures the relationship between actions and their observed consequences, without prescribing how they should be predicted. This prob-abilistic perspective connects policy learning with the different dynamics models introduced ear-lier, while allowing flexibility in learning objectives, architectural designs, and mechanisms for integrating world prediction and action generation. • Joint learning objectives. One approach explicitly combines policy learning with a world-prediction objective. For example, a shared representation zt = ϕ(ht) can support both an action predic-tor πθ(at | zt, g ) and an observational dynamics model pθ(ot+1 | zt, a t), where the latter may alternatively predict future latent representations. The two components can be trained jointly using action-labeled interaction trajectories, which provide supervision for both action predic-tion and future observation prediction. The datasets Dact and Ddyn provide the corresponding training examples, with task labels used for policy learning. Such joint learning encourages representations that capture environmental dynamics while preserving information relevant to decision-making [18, 70]. Alternatively, a unified generative model can learn the joint distribu-tion of future observations and actions without explicitly separating policy and world-prediction losses [14]. These approaches illustrate different ways to integrate world modeling and action generation through shared representations or generative objectives. • Learning signals . Recall that direct policy learning estimates πθ(at | ht, g ) from demonstra-tions, typically by minimizing the supervised learning objective in (15). In many robotic set-tings, the action space is relatively low-dimensional. For example, a robotic manipulator may use a 7-dimensional action representation consisting of a 6-DoF end-effector pose and a grip-per command. Consequently, each training example provides supervision over relatively few action variables. In contrast, visual observations, such as images and video frames, are high-dimensional, often containing hundreds of thousands or millions of pixel values. Predicting future observations therefore provides a much higher-dimensional learning signal per training example. Jointly learning action and observation prediction allows the model to leverage both low-dimensional action supervision and high-dimensional visual prediction signals. • Architectural realization. The learning objectives above can be implemented using different de-grees of parameter sharing and interaction. A common design uses a shared encoder or Trans-former backbone with modality-specific prediction heads, allowing world prediction and ac-tion prediction to shape common representations. Other designs use separate video and action modules that exchange information through attention, as in Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) ar-chitectures [71, 72]. The attention mechanism can support different conditional dependencies. For example, allowing video tokens to attend to action tokens enables action-conditioned world prediction, whereas allowing action tokens to attend to future-video representations enables future-conditioned action prediction. Bidirectional interaction can support joint video-action 21 generation. The precise probabilistic interpretation depends not only on attention patterns, but also on the training objective and generation procedure. These architectural choices are not mutually exclusive. For example, DreamZero combines a shared generative backbone with modality-specific output components. Thus, the essential dis-tinction is how information is shared between world prediction and action generation. 

Training versus Inference. Joint learning of world models and policies does not necessarily re-quire explicit world prediction at inference time. Depending on the model architecture and infer-ence procedure, a jointly trained model may directly generate actions, jointly predict future ob-servations and actions, or use predicted futures for planning. For example, Fast-WAM [71] shows that a model jointly trained can generate actions without explicitly predicting future frames at in-ference time, achieving competitive performance with approaches that jointly generate future ob-servations and actions. This suggests that the benefits of world modeling may arise from acquiring useful representations during training, rather than necessarily requiring explicit future prediction during inference. Understanding the respective contributions of joint learning during training and joint prediction during inference remains an important direction for future research. 

## 3.4 Language Models as Implicit World Models and Generalist Agents 

Most of the approaches discussed so far focus on physical intelligence, particularly robotics and embodied agents, where world models aim to capture environmental dynamics and the conse-quences of physical actions. However, the principles of world modeling extend beyond physical environments, and acquiring knowledge about the world does not necessarily require an explicit world-modeling objective. More generally, models trained to predict sequences of observations, actions, and other information may implicitly acquire knowledge about environmental structure and dynamics. LLMs provide an interesting example of this possibility. LLMs are primarily pretrained on large-scale text corpora through next-token prediction. Al-though this objective does not explicitly target environment dynamics, the training data contain extensive descriptions of events, interactions, actions, and their consequences. By learning to pre-dict such sequences, LLMs may develop implicit representations of environmental states, temporal relationships, and action outcomes. More importantly, when observations and actions are repre-sented as sequences of tokens, the same autoregressive framework can learn both world predic-tion and action generation through different conditional predictions. This reveals a connection between language modeling, world modeling, and policy learning: these capabilities can be ac-quired through a unified predictive learning framework, without requiring separate models or explicitly distinct learning objectives. 

Implicit World Modeling through Next-Token Prediction. The standard objective of autoregres-sive language modeling is to learn the joint distribution of a token sequence (x0, . . . , x n) through next-token prediction. By the chain rule of probability, this distribution can be factorized as

The same principle can be extended to interaction trajectories. Consider a sequence of observa-tions and actions serialized as (g, o 0, a 0, o 1, a 1, . . . ), where observations and actions are represented 22 as sequences of tokens, and the task g is included in the context. At the level of complete observa-tions and actions, autoregressive prediction corresponds to learning two conditional distributions, 

πθ(at | ht, g ) and pθ(ot+1 | ht, a t, g ). This corresponds to the first factorization of the joint world-action distribution in (21). Both conditionals can be learned through the same token-level next-token prediction objective, without requiring separate networks or learning mechanisms. Thus, autoregressive modeling of interaction trajectories can jointly provide supervision for policy learn-ing and world modeling. Alternatively, consider a different serialization in which the future observation ot+1 precedes the corresponding action at, given the history ht and task g, i.e., (g, . . . , o t+1 , a t, . . . ). Autoregres-sive prediction then corresponds to learning pθ(ot+1 | ht, g ) and pθ(at | ht, o t+1 , g ), which corre-spond to the second factorization of (21). By changing the serialization order and conditioning context, autoregressive models can therefore learn different conditional distributions underlying world modeling and action prediction. More broadly, this perspective highlights a connection between language modeling, world modeling, and policy learning: all can be formulated as conditional prediction problems, with their roles determined by the prediction targets and available context. This perspective suggests that next-token prediction over sufficiently diverse data may implicitly learn a mixture of predic-tive models, including models of actions, dynamics, and interactions. Evidence for emergent world representations has been observed in language models trained on game sequences, such as Othello-GPT [20]. However, predicting plausible sequences does not necessarily imply learning accurate action-conditioned dynamics. In particular, standard language pretraining does not guarantee re-liable predictions under unfamiliar interventions or over long interaction horizons [73]. 

Explicit World Modeling through Interaction-Based Fine-Tuning. Although next-token predic-tion on general-purpose text may enable LLMs to acquire implicit world knowledge, their ability to predict the consequences of actions can be further improved through explicit world-modeling supervision on interaction trajectories. Recent work follows two complementary approaches. The first treats the world model as a separate simulator for predicting action consequences and supporting planning, as demonstrated by Web Agents with World Models, WebDreamer, WebEvolver, and WebWorld [21, 22, 74, 75]. Reinforcement World Model Learning further explores reinforcement learning to improve the accuracy of predicted transitions [76]. The second approach integrates world modeling into the policy itself , grounding action selection in learned environment dynamics without requiring a separate simulator [23, 77]. This approach is closely related to world-action models, where world modeling and action generation are learned within a unified model. Both approaches can use similar interaction data and prediction objectives. This reinforces the view that world modeling and policy learning are distinct but closely related capabilities that need not be implemented as separate models. More broadly, LLM-based agents suggest a promising path toward generalist intelligence, where large-scale pretraining provides broad world knowledge, and interaction-based learning progressively grounds and refines this knowledge, enabling agents to better predict, adapt to, and act across diverse environments and tasks. 

# 4 Discussion and Conclusion 

In this paper, we have introduced world models from the perspective of learning generalist agents, connecting them to classical sequential decision-making and different approaches to policy learn-ing. We have highlighted how world modeling and policy learning can be understood through a 23 unified conditional prediction perspective, without necessarily requiring separate models. Build-ing on these connections, we conclude by discussing several important challenges and future di-rections toward developing generalist agents. 

Foundation Models Across Embodiments. A central challenge in developing physical intelli-gence is enabling agents to transfer their knowledge and capabilities across different physical em-bodiments. Beyond generalization across environments and tasks, generalist agents may need to operate through different embodiments, including robot arms, mobile manipulators, humanoids, and drones, which differ in their sensing, morphology, actuation, and action interfaces. An impor-tant goal is to develop foundation models whose learned world knowledge and policies can transfer across embodiments, rather than training separate models for each robot. One promising approach is to learn shared representations of world dynamics and actions, while using embodiment-specific components to accommodate different observation and action spaces. For example, Cosmos 3 uses embodiment-specific linear projections to map heterogeneous action representations into a shared latent space, enabling a common model backbone to learn from different embodiments [78]. An important question is which aspects of world models and policies can be shared across embodi-ments, and how much adaptation is needed for new embodiments. 

Scaling Data and Learning Signals for Generalist Agents. The success of foundation models suggests that scaling data, model capacity, and computation can be an effective path toward gen-eralization. For learning generalist agents, we highlight two complementary dimensions: source scaling , which leverages heterogeneous data across environments, tasks, embodiments, and modal-ities, and signal scaling , which extracts complementary learning signals from the available data. • Source scaling aims to exploit diverse data sources, including images, passive videos, egocentric human videos, and robot interaction trajectories [5, 6]. These sources provide complementary information: images capture visual and semantic knowledge, videos reveal temporal relation-ships and physical dynamics, human videos capture goal-directed interactions, and robot tra-jectories additionally provide action labels and their consequences. Similarly, in digital environ-ments, language, code, browser interactions, and tool-use trajectories provide different forms of knowledge about the world and how agents interact with it. Importantly, acquiring world knowl-edge does not necessarily require action-labeled data, making it possible to leverage abundant passive observations alongside relatively scarce interaction trajectories. A key challenge is how to effectively combine these heterogeneous sources despite differences in their representations, supervision, and underlying environments. • Signal scaling aims to extract multiple complementary learning signals from the available data. Beyond action prediction, interaction trajectories can provide supervision for future observation prediction, state-change prediction, inverse dynamics, among others. In particular, predicting future observations or state changes can provide much higher-dimensional supervision than predicting relatively low-dimensional actions. These objectives capture different aspects of en-vironmental structure, dynamics, and action consequences, potentially enabling the model to acquire more transferable representations. An important question is which learning signals are most useful, how they complement one another, and how they should be combined for different data sources and downstream tasks. From this perspective, world modeling provides an important learning signal for acquiring knowl-edge about environmental dynamics, particularly through predicting how the environment evolves 24 in response to actions. However, the resulting predictive model need not be explicitly used for plan-ning at deployment: the acquired knowledge may instead support direct policy learning, or further fine-tuning through reinforcement learning. Thus, the value of world modeling extends beyond its role as a predictive simulator for planning. More broadly, these two dimensions suggest that devel-oping generalist agents requires not only increasing the amount and diversity of available data, but also improving how effectively transferable knowledge is extracted from that data. Understanding how data sources and learning signals jointly affect generalization across environments, tasks, and embodiments remains an important research direction. 

Representations of States and Actions. An important design choice is how states and actions are represented. Although we have distinguished observation-space and latent-space models, the specific representations can substantially affect what an agent learns, how well it generalizes, and how its predictions are translated into executable actions. For example, in robotics, an agent may receive observations from cameras and proprioceptive sensors, while its actions can be represented in different coordinate spaces and at different levels of abstraction. Actions may specify low-level motor torques, joint-space targets, or Cartesian end-effector targets, such as desired positions and orientations, which are converted into executable commands by lower-level controllers [6, 44]. More generally, temporally extended actions can ab-stract away sequences of low-level decisions [79]. Recent studies have also explored using general-purpose foundation models, such as GPT-6 Astra and Claude, for robotic control [80, 81], further highlighting the importance of action representations and control interfaces. Higher-level action representations can simplify learning by delegating low-level control, but may also limit the agent’s ability to exploit fine-grained dynamics. Similarly, state representations may range from raw ob-servations to structured quantities, such as object poses and joint configurations, or learned latent representations [82–84]. Different representations preserve different aspects of the environment and may vary in their suitability for prediction, planning, and control. Compact representations can simplify learning but may discard information needed for accurate dynamics prediction or effective decision-making. More broadly, state and action representations determine the level of abstraction at which an agent models and interacts with the world. In particular, when high-level actions are executed through lower-level controllers, the world model learns the resulting closed-loop dynamics rather than the direct response to motor commands. Understanding how these choices affect learnability, generalization, and control is an important direction toward developing generalist agents across diverse environments and tasks. 

Model-Based Planning vs High-Level Reasoning. While predictive dynamic models support planning and control, many existing approaches focus on short-horizon prediction and action gen-eration, rather than high-level deliberative reasoning. For example, DreamZero jointly predicts future observations and actions for reactive robotic control, while identifying long-horizon rea-soning as an important future direction [14]. Such approaches are often closer to reactive control than to the high-level reasoning capabilities demonstrated by LLMs, which can leverage additional inference-time computation to decompose complex tasks, explore alternative strategies, and rea-son about intermediate goals. Recent explorations of general-purpose reasoning models, such as GPT-6 Astra, for robotic control suggest a promising complementary direction [80]. An important question is how to integrate high-level reasoning with predictive world models, and particularly in which representation space such reasoning should occur: natural language, structured physi-cal states, learned latent representations, or combinations thereof. More broadly, can additional 25 inference-time computation improve an agent’s ability to reason about goals and plan over longer horizons, while retaining the efficiency required for real-time control? 

Evaluating World Models for Decision-Making. Finally, a fundamental challenge is how to eval-uate the quality and usefulness of learned world models, particularly in terms of their contributions to decision-making. While predictive accuracy provides a natural evaluation criterion, it does not necessarily reflect how well a model supports decision-making. A world model may accurately predict observations while failing to capture aspects of the environment most relevant to action selection. Conversely, a compact model that preserves control-relevant information may support effective policies without reconstructing the environment in high fidelity [85]. A related question is whether increasingly high-fidelity world models, such as realistic simulators, necessarily lead to better policies. Moreover, model errors in regions visited by the learned policy may be more con-sequential than larger errors in irrelevant regions [64]. These considerations suggest that world models should be evaluated not only by their predictive fidelity, but also by their ability to support planning, policy improvement, and generalization across environments and tasks.
