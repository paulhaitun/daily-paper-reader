# Resource-Aware Scheduling for Distributed Quantum Computing 

Connor Howe 1, Raghav Mundada 1, Jake O’Shaughnessy 1, Ibrahim Abdullahi 1, Ali Anwar 11Department of Computer Science and Engineering, University of Minnesota-Twin Cities Email: {howe0427, munda059, oshau033, abdul765, aanwar }@umn.edu 

Abstract —Distributed quantum computing (DQC) is a promis-ing near-term approach for executing circuits that exceed the capacity of a single quantum processing unit (QPU). Prior work has made significant progress on circuit partitioning and reconstruction, but practical DQC requires more than cutting a circuit into smaller pieces. A runtime system must determine whether to execute the circuit intact, cut it for sequential exe-cution on one QPU, or distribute it across multiple QPUs, while accounting for queueing, communication, reconstruction, and hardware constraints. We present a resource-aware scheduler for DQC that compares these execution alternatives at runtime using explicit models of QPU execution, communication, and host-side reconstruction, together with hardware-informed timing derived from simulated IBM QPUs. By treating communication and reconstruction as first-class scheduling costs, the scheduler selects execution plans using a more realistic view of end-to-end overhead than cutting-only approaches. Across our experiments, reconstruction accounts for over 83% of cutting overhead, distributed execution reduces P90 latency by up to 39% relative to uncut single-QPU execution, and scheduling overhead itself remains negligible, never exceeding 0.038% of end-to-end job latency. Together, these results show that DQC must be treated as a runtime scheduling problem over both quantum and classical resources, not just circuit partitioning. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The size and complexity of quantum circuits required by many near-term applications increasingly exceed the capacity of individual quantum processing units (QPUs). Although hardware continues to improve, practical devices remain lim-ited by qubit count, connectivity, and noise, making it difficult to execute larger circuits on a single processor. Distributed quantum computing (DQC) offers a promising near-term path forward by enabling a circuit to be partitioned and executed across multiple QPUs [1], [2]. However, enabling DQC in practice requires more than partitioning a circuit. A runtime system must decide whether a circuit should run intact on one QPU, be cut and executed sequentially on a single QPU, or be distributed across multiple QPUs [3]. That decision depends not only on the circuit structure, but also on system conditions such as queueing delay, QPU availability, communication overhead, host-side reconstruction cost, and hardware-specific execution behav-ior [4], [5], [6], [7]. As a result, DQC is not only a partitioning problem, but also a runtime scheduling problem over both quantum and classical resources. Prior work on circuit cutting has developed increasingly effective partitioning and reconstruction methods [8], [9], [10], [11], while related work in DQC architectures and orchestration has highlighted the need for broader execution management [12], [13], [3]. Yet the runtime question of how to choose among circuit execution plans under dynamic resource conditions remains comparatively underexplored. In particular, communication and host-side quasi-probability reconstruction are often treated as secondary overheads rather than explicit scheduling resources, even though they can materially affect both latency and plan quality. [14] In this paper, we present a resource-aware scheduler for DQC that explicitly compares three execution modes: uncut single-QPU execution (Plan A), cut single-QPU sequential execution (Plan B), and cut multi-QPU distributed execution (Plan C). The scheduler models QPU execution, communica-tion, and reconstruction as distinct constrained resources, and uses hardware-informed timing derived from simulated IBM QPUs together with execution in AerSimulator using backend-specific noise models [15], [16] to estimate execution cost under realistic device conditions. Rather than treating circuit cutting as the sole decision point, our approach evaluates candidate plans using a fuller view of end-to-end latency and output quality. This enables the scheduler to choose execution strategies based on both circuit structure and current system conditions, complementing prior compiler and partitioning focused approaches [14]. Our results show that reconstruction dominates cut-execution overhead, distributed execution can reduce tail la-tency, and scheduler overhead remains negligible. Together, these findings support runtime plan selection across both quantum and classical resources.

II. M OTIVATION : U SE CASES 

Several near-term quantum computing settings motivate a resource-aware scheduler for distributed quantum computing. 

a) Large circuits that exceed single-QPU capacity: 

Application classes such as variational quantum eigensolvers (VQE) for molecular simulation [17], [18], [19] and QAOA-style optimization workloads naturally grow in qubit count with problem size. In addition, practical circuits in the emerg-ing fault-tolerant era of quantum computing will subject hard-ware to larger and more complex workloads [20]. For these workloads, circuit cutting and distributed execution may be the only feasible way to run jobs that do not fit on a single available QPU [21], [22], [23]. But feasibility alone is insuffi-cient: the system must still determine whether communication, reconstruction, and fidelity costs justify cutting over waiting or single-device execution [24], [25]. 

b) Heterogeneous quantum-classical workflows: Emerg-ing HPC and hybrid workflows increasingly combine classical processing with diverse streams of quantum jobs [12], [26], [27], [28]. Some jobs fit on a single QPU, while others require cutting or multi-QPU execution under dynamic queue and resource conditions [29], [30]. The scheduler must jointly consider circuit structure, QPU availability, communication contention, and reconstruction load [31], [32], [33], consistent with prior work on quantum workflow orchestration and resource-aware scheduling. 

c) Latency-sensitive quantum services: Cloud-based quantum services and shared quantum platforms often operate under latency or service-level objectives [13], [32], [5]. In these settings, end-to-end job latency depends on more than QPU execution time alone: queuing delay, inter-QPU coordi-nation, and host-side reconstruction can determine whether a cut or distributed plan is worthwhile in practice [34], [35]. A plan feasible from a partitioning perspective may still be undesirable if it misses latency targets or adds excessive overhead, making resource-aware runtime selection essential for shared, latency-sensitive quantum services [31], [33]. III. R ELATED WORK 

a) Circuit Cutting and Reconstruction: Circuit partition-ing and reconstruction are central to distributed quantum com-puting. Prior work studies optimal partitioning under hardware constraints [8], analyzes theoretical costs of wire and gate cuts [10], [11], and improves reconstruction via randomized measurements [9]. These approaches focus on improving cut-ting itself. In contrast, our work addresses a different systems question: how should a runtime system choose among intact execution, single-QPU cut execution, and distributed multi-QPU execution under changing resource conditions? 

b) Overhead-Aware and Adaptive Cutting: Recent efforts explicitly model cutting overheads. ShotQC reduces sampling cost via adaptive shot allocation [36], while operator backprop-agation methods reduce reconstruction overhead [37]. Adap-tive approaches such as MAESTROCUT and online cutting-point detection explore dynamic repartitioning and adaptive strategies [38], [39]. These works show overhead depends on execution choices; we complement them by treating cut execution as one option within a broader runtime scheduling problem. 

c) Hardware-Aware Partitioning: Hardware-aware ap-proaches incorporate topology and noise into partitioning. FitCut combines partitioning with resource-aware schedul-ing [40], while DisMap and DQTetris optimize mapping using device characteristics and communication structure [41], [42]. Unlike these works, which focus on partition quality, we study whether partitioned execution should be selected at runtime. 

d) Quantum Scheduling and Orchestration: Systems-level work considers quantum workload management. SCIM MILQ integrates cutting with scheduling [12], Qonductor explores latency–fidelity tradeoffs in cloud orchestration [13], Ravi et al. study adaptive job and resource management for the growing quantum cloud [32], and prior schedulers improve utilization through workload-level optimization [43], [44], backend prioritization [45], and adaptive orchestration [46], [33], [34]. Related work also considers multi-device schedul-ing for variational workloads and multi-programming in shared quantum environments [47], [24], [25]. These approaches highlight the importance of global orchestration. Our work focuses specifically on execution-plan selection across intact, cut, and distributed strategies. 

e) Distributed Quantum Computing Architectures: Sur-veys and architectural studies define the DQC landscape [3], [4], [48]. Early and recent systems demonstrate distributed execution under communication constraints [49], [50]. Addi-tional architectural work has begun to frame scalable quantum data-center infrastructure as a systems design problem [28], [23]. These works motivate coordinated multi-QPU execution [51], [52], [53]. We build on this by formulating runtime plan selection that jointly considers computation, communication, and reconstruction costs. Overall, prior work advances cutting, partitioning, and or-chestration. Our contribution is a runtime scheduling perspec-tive that selects among execution strategies based on system state and end-to-end cost. IV. S YSTEM DESIGN 

A. Architecture Overview 

Figure 1 shows the overall scheduler architecture. The system sits between incoming quantum applications and a pool of available QPUs, and evaluates each job using three explicit resource classes: QPU execution resources, communication resources, and host-side reconstruction resources. Its purpose is to determine which of the three possible plans a job should run: intact on a single QPU, be cut and executed sequentially on one QPU, or be cut and distributed across multiple QPUs. It can also be deferred to wait until resources are available. The architecture consists of the following main components: Fig. 1: Host-level architecture. For each incoming circuit, the profiler combines circuit characteristics and current QPU state to identify feasible backend and cutting options. The planner forms candidate plans for single-QPU execution, sequential cut execution, distributed multi-QPU execution, or deferral, and ranks them using predicted fidelity and runtime cost. The selected plan is then passed to the executor. 

a) Profiler: The profiler extracts circuit features such as width w, depth d, one- and two-qubit gate counts (n1q , n 2q ),and interaction density ρ. These features estimate cut suit-ability, rank candidate QPUs, and provide structure for plan generation and cost prediction. 

b) Planner: The planner is the core decision-making component. For each job, it enumerates feasible execution alternatives among Plan A, Plan B, Plan C, and WAIT; invokes circuit cutting when required; predicts queueing, execution, communication, and reconstruction costs; and scores feasible plans using the objective in Eq. (2). If service-level objectives are present, infeasible plans are filtered before scoring. The planner then selects the minimum-score feasible plan. 

c) Executor: Once a plan is selected, the executor ma-terializes it as a staged task graph spanning quantum execu-tion, inter-stage communication, and host-side reconstruction. These stages are scheduled over separate worker pools, allow-ing non-QPU costs to be modeled explicitly rather than folded into QPU occupancy. QPU reservations are spatial: multiple jobs may execute concurrently on disjoint connected qubit subsets when the hardware topology permits. 

d) Cutting layer: The cutting layer provides interchange-able partitioning backends used by Plans B and C. In our im-plementation, one backend performs a scheduler-aware search over partition candidates, while another applies a fixed approx-imate partitioning strategy. Both expose a common interface to the planner, allowing the scheduling logic to remain separate from the specific cutting method used. 

e) Hardware abstraction layer: Each QPU is represented through a hardware profile containing qubit count, coupling topology, timing characteristics, and error-rate information. These profiles provide the hardware-informed inputs needed for feasibility checks, timing prediction, quality estimation, and plan comparison. 

B. Execution Plans 

Plan A executes the circuit intact on one QPU with no cutting overhead. Plan B partitions the circuit into subcircuits fitting one QPU and runs all nexec subexperiments sequentially on that backend, then reconstructs on the host CPU. Plan C 

distributes subcircuits across multiple QPUs via Algorithm 1, with quantum tasks executing in parallel; communication and reconstruction follow as post-QPU tasks. WAIT defers the job with exponential backoff. 

C. Sampling Overhead 

Circuit cutting introduces sampling overhead because each cut increases the number of subexperiments required to recon-struct the final result. For kw wire cuts, the quasi-probability sampling factor and total number of subexperiments are 

s = 4 kw , nexec = s · nshots (1) where nshots is the target number of shots for the original circuit. Even a small number of wire cuts can therefore expand the execution workload substantially. This growth directly increases the cost of cut execution, especially communication and host-side reconstruction. It also motivates an important design choice in our scoring objec-tive. When comparing plans, we use predicted per-shot QPU blocking time rather than treating the QPU as occupied by the entire sampling-expanded workload. Sampling overhead is instead captured in communication and reconstruction models, preserving a clearer separation between quantum resource usage and downstream classical cost.

F. Resource Models 

The scheduler models QPU execution, communication, and host-side reconstruction as separate resource classes. This separation allows the system to represent contention and delay more realistically than if all overheads were folded into a single execution-time estimate. 

a) QPU resources: Predicted queue wait ˆWq is estimated by scanning existing reservations on QPU q and identifying the earliest time at which a connected free component of size at least w becomes available. This reflects the fact that feasibility depends not only on total free qubits, but also on whether those qubits form a usable connected region under the device topology. 

b) Communication resources: For cut and distributed execution, communication cost is modeled as 

ˆTcomm = c0 + c1 nexec + δC (|Q p| − 1) (7) where c0 is a fixed communication overhead, c1 scales with the number of subexperiments, and the final term captures additional coordination cost as more QPUs are involved. This model reflects both workload-dependent communication and the overhead of cross-device coordination. 

c) Reconstruction resources: Host-side reconstruction cost is modeled as 

ˆTrecon = r0 + r1 nexec (8) where r0 is a fixed overhead and r1 captures per-subexperiment reconstruction cost. Because nexec grows expo-nentially with wire cuts (Eq. (1)), reconstruction can dominate end-to-end latency even when QPU execution is modest. 

G. Hardware-Informed QPU Timing 

To estimate QPU execution cost, the scheduler uses hardware-informed timing derived from simulated IBM QPUs. In the main experimental configuration, each circuit is tran-spiled to the target device and its scheduled duration is extracted from the resulting hardware-level representation. The predicted per-shot QPU blocking time is therefore 

ˆXq = circuit duration × dt q (9) where dt q is the device time resolution for QPU q. This approach allows timing estimates to reflect hardware-specific connectivity, gate decomposition, and scheduling effects rather than relying only on circuit-level gate counts. For comparison and fallback, the scheduler also supports a simpler analytic timing model: 

ˆXq = d · tlayer + tmeas + tov (10) where d is depth, tlayer per-layer time, tmeas measurement cost, and tov per-shot overhead. The hardware-informed model is used throughout the main experiments because it better reflects realistic device behavior. In particular, it captures the effects of transpilation, topology constraints, and hardware-level scheduling that are not repre-sented in the analytic approximation. 

H. Assignment Policy and Scheduling Loop 

For distributed execution, the scheduler assigns partitions to QPUs to balance parallelism and quality. Plan C uses a makespan-oriented greedy policy (Algorithm 1): labels are or-dered by decreasing cost and assigned to the QPU minimizing projected load, with quality-aware tie-breaking. This reduces the quantum critical path while accounting for device relia-bility At runtime, the scheduler operates in discrete decision steps. At each step, it releases completed QPU reservations, updates the set of pending jobs, re-ranks candidate devices for each job, and invokes the planner to choose among Plans A, B, C, and WAIT. Jobs assigned WAIT remain pending and are reconsidered after backoff, while accepted plans are passed to the executor for staged execution. V. I MPLEMENTATION 

The scheduler is implemented in Python 3.10 with Qiskit 2.x, including planning, resource modeling, and execu-tion components to evaluate Plans A, B, C, and WAIT under simulated multi-QPU conditions. QPU behavior is modeled using simulated IBM QPUs, which provide device topologies, gate timing information, and noise characteristics used for hardware-informed timing and quality estimation. Quantum execution is simulated with AerSimulator using backend-specific noise models, allowing the scheduler to evaluate plan choices under device-dependent conditions without requiring access to live hardware. Circuit cutting and quasi-probability reconstruction use Qiskit’s Circuit Cutting Addon. The scheduler interfaces with backends via a common abstraction, enabling different parti-tioning strategies without changing scheduling logic. VI. E XPERIMENTAL SETUP

B. Reconstruction Dominates Cutting Overhead 

Figure 3 isolates the overhead introduced by cut execu-tion. In the representative breakdown shown, reconstruction accounts for 87% of total cutting overhead (805 ms of 925 ms), while communication contributes the remaining 13%. This pattern remains stable across repeated runs: across 20 matched seeds, reconstruction accounts for 83.1% of cutting overhead on average (95% CI [82.8%, 83.4%]). Scheduler decision time is negligible by comparison. Together, these results show that the dominant cost of cut-enabled DQC is not the scheduling decision itself, but the host-side work required to reconstruct the final result. This result directly justifies the modeling choices in Sec-tion IV-F and Section IV-D. If reconstruction were treated as a minor secondary cost, the scheduler would systematically underestimate the true burden of cut execution. Instead, our results show that reconstruction must be treated as a first-class resource when evaluating distributed quantum execution.

D. Adaptive Plan Selection Yields Non-Monotonic Congestion 

Figure 5 and Table II reveal one of the more surprising behaviors in the evaluation. Fidelity degrades smoothly as congestion increases, but latency does not. Instead, median latency peaks sharply at 75% congestion and then recovers at 100% congestion. The reason is that congestion changes which plan the scheduler prefers. Under partial congestion, some jobs con-tinue to queue for preferred single-device execution, creating large latency spikes. Under full congestion, the scheduler shifts more aggressively toward Plan C, distributing work across the remaining available QPUs and restoring parallelism. This produces a counterintuitive but important result: under adaptive plan selection, full congestion on the preferred device can be less damaging than partial congestion if it forces the system into a better distributed execution regime. I. Latency Constraints Change Which Plans Remain Viable 

Figure 10 shows how service-level objectives (SLOs) change the set of execution plans the scheduler can consider. As latency targets become more restrictive, cut-enabled plans are filtered out first because their predicted end-to-end latency includes not only QPU execution, but also communication and host-side reconstruction. Under loose or absent SLO constraints, the scheduler can choose among Plans A, B, and C based on their overall score. Under tighter constraints, however, some plans are rejected before scoring because they are predicted to miss the latency target. This changes scheduler behavior in two ways. First, the number of completed jobs decreases as the SLO becomes tighter, because fewer jobs retain any feasible plan that can satisfy the deadline. At SLO = 0 .5 s , only 12 of 40 jobs complete, all under Plan A. Second, the compliance CDF shows that the 1.0 s and looser settings converge toward full compliance by approximately 1.5 s , whereas the SLO = 0 .5 s 

case lags substantially behind. Tight deadlines therefore reduce not only plan diversity, but also overall throughput. This result highlights why makespan-based feasibility fil-tering must remain separate from plan scoring. A plan may appear attractive from a resource-occupancy perspective, yet still be infeasible once end-to-end latency, including commu-nication and reconstruction, is taken into account. SLO-aware scheduling therefore does not merely change which plan is preferred; it changes which plans are feasible at all. This is precisely why explicit end-to-end modeling is necessary for latency-sensitive distributed quantum computing. 

J. Scaling to Large-Scale Systems 

To this point, our evaluation has focused on scales repre-sentative of near-term hardware (Section VI). We additionally evaluate scheduler behavior far beyond this range — up to 128 QPUs and 2688 qubits — to understand how plan selection and latency evolve toward larger, future systems. Fleet-size scaling. Under a workload that always requires cutting by construction (8–14-qubit circuits against 7-qubit QPUs), Plan C adoption is highest at the smallest fleet sizes (60.0% at N = 2 ), passes through a transitional region at 

N = 3 –8, then stabilizes into a plateau of roughly 31–43% from N = 16 through N = 128 . Scheduling overhead itself remains flat across the entire range (Figure 11, 0.36–0.46 ms), extending the negligible-overhead result of Section VII-F two orders of magnitude further in fleet size. Recovering this trend required correcting three scheduler bugs uncovered by treating an initially plausible-looking result — “small fleets favor Plan C, large fleets collapse to Plan B” — with suspicion rather than accepting it at face value: an O(N 3) candidate search that would not have completed in reasonable time at N = 128 ; a candidate-ranking criterion that could not distinguish an idle QPU from one already holding a same-batch reservation; and a queue-wait estimator that silently returned an infinite, rather than a finite fallback, wait once a QPU’s backlog exceeded a fixed probe cap, corrupting plan scoring for 32–53% of jobs at fleet sizes 2–16. Each fix was verified via isolated, congestion-free control tests and 5-seed reproductions per fleet size before being trusted. 

Circuit-width scaling. Figure 12 extends circuit width to 2688 qubits (4-QPU fleet, QPU capacity and circuit width scaled together). Under batch submission (solid line, right panel), Plan C share is non-monotonic: 63.3% at 21 qubits, rising to 93.3% at 168 qubits, dipping to 63.3% at 336 qubits, recovering to 80.0% at 672 qubits, then declining sharply to 52.8% and 20.6% at the two largest tiers. This drop is a resource-contention artifact of batch submission, not a cutting or reconstruction cost : kw and kg are zero throughout this sweep (no real cutting executes in this configuration), and on an otherwise-idle fleet Plan C scores strictly better than Plan B at every width tested. The mechanism is straight-forward queueing pressure: Plan C requires multiple QPUs simultaneously free, while Plan B needs only one; under batch submission, per-job execution time grows with circuit width at a fixed job count and fixed 4-QPU fleet, so the fleet becomes progressively more congested as width grows, and the harder “ K QPUs free at once” condition for Plan C becomes increasingly unmet. Disabling 3 of 4 QPUs on an otherwise-idle fleet reproduces this directly, causing Plan C candidates to disappear entirely even at small widths. Repeating the sweep under realistic Poisson-arrival stream-ing rather than batch submission (dashed line, right panel) isolates how much of the drop is this contention artifact versus a genuine capacity limit: Plan C share is higher at every tier under streaming (e.g., 37.2% vs. 20.6% at 2688 qubits; 86.7– 98.3% across the 168–672-qubit tiers vs. 63.3–93.3% under batch), confirming that batch-induced congestion — not an intrinsic scaling limit — suppressed Plan C through most of the range. The decline at the largest widths survives even under streaming (37.2% at 2688 qubits vs. 86–98% at 168– 672 qubits), so a genuine, if far softer, capacity effect remains at the extreme end of the range studied: even with gradual arrivals, a 4-QPU fleet eventually struggles to keep multiple QPUs simultaneously free against circuits this wide. 

End-to-end latency. Figure 13 plots P50 latency (P50– P90 band shaded) across both sweeps as continuous curves. Latency falls monotonically with fleet size, from 9.0 s (P50) / 10.3 s (P90) at N = 2 to 1.5 s / 2.0 s at N = 64 –128 —a smooth improvement, unlike the non-monotonic plan-mix curve over the same range. Against circuit width, P50 latency stays comparatively flat (5.1–7.4 s) from 21 through 1344 qubits, then jumps sharply to 10.6 s at 2688 qubits (P90: 23.6 s, versus 10.0 s at the next tier down) — the same contention mechanism as above: as Plan C becomes harder to obtain at the largest widths, more jobs fall back to slower sequential execution.

X. L IMITATIONS AND DISCUSSION 

While our evaluation is designed to reflect realistic operating conditions, several factors limit generality of our findings. First, we rely on simulated rather than live quantum hardware. Although the simulated IBM QPUs and AerSimulator noise models capture hardware-informed timing, topology, and error characteristics, real systems may introduce additional effects, such as calibration drift, queue variability, additional noise overheads, and network-induced latency that are not fully represented in our setup. Second, our workloads are synthetic benchmarks rather than production traces. This enables con-trolled and interpretable comparisons across execution plans; however, different circuit families, workload compositions, or production job mixes may shift the relative balance and observed trade-offs among the plans. Third, our communication and reconstruction models are explicitly modeled through simplified cost models. The sched-uler therefore captures the existence and scheduling impact of these non-QPU resources, but the absolute magnitude of those costs could differ under other systems, host implementations, or assumptions. Fourth, output quality is represented through a fidelity proxy rather than direct hardware-validated fidelity estimation for every run. This proxy is intentionally inexpen-sive and is validated for trend behavior in Figures 17 and 18, but it should not be interpreted as a replacement for a full application-level accuracy analysis. Finally, our experiments focus on the hardware, workloads, and system sizes in Section VI. As scales, heterogeneity, or QPU counts grow, break-even points among intact, cut, and distributed execution may shift. We therefore view these results as evidence that runtime scheduling over quantum and classical resources is necessary, while exact plan boundaries depend on workload scale and deployment architecture. XI. C ONCLUSION 

We present a resource-aware scheduler for distributed quan-tum computing that treats execution-plan selection as a runtime systems problem rather than a circuit-partitioning task. The scheduler compares intact, sequential cut, distributed cut, and deferral using models of QPU occupancy, communication, reconstruction, and backend quality. Our evaluation shows that plan choice strongly impacts latency and fidelity, with Plan A best on average and Plan C offering better tail performance; reconstruction dominates overhead (over 83%), making CPU-side processing a first-class concern; and the scheduler adapts to system dynamics, revealing clear break-even points, non-monotonic latency under congestion, and benefits from quality-aware routing. Decision overhead re-mains negligible ( ≤0.038%), while submission strategies and latency targets significantly influence performance. Overall, our results demonstrate that DQC must be treated as a runtime scheduling problem across quantum and classical resources, where communication and reconstruction are first-class, and plan boundaries depend on workload scale and system design.
