# Dynamic Control of a Make-to-Order Manufacturing System under Throughput Time Constraints: An Effective Computational Method in the High-Dimensional Case 

Baris Ata ∗ and Chun Li †

Booth School of Business, University of Chicago 

Abstract 

We consider dynamic control of a make-to-order production system that serves price- and delay-sensitive customers. The production system is modeled as a multiclass queueing network where the customers’ delay sensitivity is modeled via throughput-time constraints. To maximize her profit while meeting these throughput time constraints, the system manager uses three dif-ferent types of control: i) dynamic pricing decisions; ii) dynamic order-of-service (scheduling) decisions; and iii) outsourcing decisions upon arrival of new orders. We approximate this problem by a multi-dimensional Brownian control problem in heavy traffic, and develop a neural network-based computational method tailored for high-dimensional problems. By interpreting the solution in the context of the original model, we propose an integrated dynamic pricing, outsourcing, and scheduling policy. The effectiveness of this policy is demonstrated through a simulation study of two test problems drawn from the literature. 

Keywords: Dynamic pricing; Throughput time constraints; Outsourcing; High-dimensional stochastic control; Neural networks; Make-to-order manufacturing systems; Queueing networks; Heavy-traffic analysis

Two important features of our formulation are the following: First, the customer or-ders of different types follow deterministic routes in the system. Second, there are no holding costs. Instead, congestion concerns are captured through the aforementioned throughput time constraints. Arguably, such constraints are more realistic and easier to estimate for the purpose of model cali-bration than the more commonly used formulation with holding costs that is analytically convenient (Harrison 2024). Because the dynamic control problem is intractable in its original form, we resort to an ap-proximation. We follow the heavy-traffic approach pioneered by Harrison (1988) and derive the approximating Brownian control problem. An important simplification that occurs in the heavy traffic limit is that one can replace the throughput time constraints by upper bounds on the sum of certain (scaled) queue lengths, as observed by Plambeck et al. (2001). The approximating Brownian control problem is equivalent to a lower-dimensional workload problem. In our setting, the equivalent workload problem is a multi-dimensional control problem with two salient features: First, it includes both drift and singular controls, inheriting them from the pricing, outsourcing and scheduling controls in the original queueing system. Second, its state space is a bounded convex polyhedron. Although it is simpler than the original control problem, the workload problem remains multi-dimensional and does not admit a closed-form solution. Thus, we develop a neural network-based method to solve this control problem numerically and obtain an effective control policy. The resulting control policy has two distinct features: First, the pushing controls (i.e., singular controls) are active only on the boundary of the state space and they partition it with respect to different active pushing controls. The pushing controls correspond to either idling the servers or outsourcing activities. Second, in the interior of the state space, the control is a state-dependent drift rate control that corresponds to the system manager’s dynamic pricing policy. We interpret the solution of the workload problem in the context of the original queueing network by proposing a policy for it. We illustrate our solution approach and test its effectiveness in two examples: The Rybko-Stolyar network (Rybko and Stolyar 1992, Maglaras 2000) and the extended criss-cross network. The latter builds on the criss-cross network and provides us with a set of high-dimensional test problems (Harrison and Wein 1989, Martins et al. 1996, Budhiraja and Ross 2008, Budhiraja et al. 2017, Kumar and Muthuraman 2004). To solve the multi-dimensional dynamic control problem, we develop a computational method that relies heavily on deep neural network technology. Two important antecedents of our work are Ata et al. (2025a, 2024a). Ata et al. (2025a) builds on the seminal work of Han et al. (2018) to develop neural network-based method for solving drift control problems for reflected Brownian motion. Ata et al. (2024a) extends it to solve singular control problems, by approximating singular controls using drift controls, and shows its effectiveness in queueing applications. Our computational method builds 2on Ata et al. (2025a, 2024a), and extends them to the setting with bounded convex polyhedral state space, which is not covered by the positive orthant considered in Ata et al. (2025a, 2024a). 

# 2 Literature Review 

Our work relates to several streams of literature. The first one uses the heavy traffic approximations to study dynamic control of stochastic networks. Pioneered by Harrison (1988, 2000, 2003), the dynamic control problems are approximated by control problems involving Brownian motions to obtain more tractable solutions in this literature. An important group of papers in this literature study the criss-cross network in the heavy traffic asymptotic regime. Harrison and Wein (1989) study the criss-cross network for a set of cost parameters under which the problem admits a pathwise solution. Martins et al. (1996) revisits the problem and identifies a total of five cases in terms of possible cost parameters of the problem. The authors label them as Case I and Cases IIA–IID. Case I is studied by Chen et al. (1994). Martins et al. (1996) prove the asymptotic optimality of the solution proposed by Harrison and Wein (1989) for Case IIA, and they provide conjectures for the other cases. Two of these cases (Cases IIB and IIC) were rigorously justified in later papers by Budhiraja and Ross (2008) and Budhiraja et al. (2017). The criss-cross network constitutes the basis for our second test problem, whereas our first test problem is based on the Rybko-Stolyar network; see Rybko and Stolyar (1992). Among papers studying dynamic control of queueing networks in the heavy traffic regime, ours is closely related to those involving throughput time constraints. Plambeck et al. (2001) incorporates such throughput-time constraints and replaces them with upper bounds on queue lengths; also see Maglaras and Van Mieghem (2005), Ata (2006), C ¸ elik and Maglaras (2008) and Rubino and Ata (2009). Other related papers include those studying dynamic pricing and outsourcing controls. Many researchers have studied models concerning pricing, outsourcing or admission control for queueing systems; see for example, Low (1974), Stidham (1985, 2004), Lewis et al. (1999), Carr and Duenyas (2000), Yoon and Lewis (2004), Ziya et al. (2006), Maoui et al. (2009), Zhang and Ayhan (2012). The closest work to ours is Ata and Barjesteh (2023) that studies dynamic pricing and outsourcing in a multiclass, single-server make-to-stock manufacturing system. Similar to our paper, their pricing (resp. outsourcing) control becomes drift control within state space (resp. singular control on reflecting barriers) in limiting formulation. Our proposed pricing policy is similar to the one in Ata and Barjesteh (2023), whose limiting formulation is one-dimensional, whereas our multiclass queueing network setting leads to a multi-dimensional formulation. Another related stream of literature develops computational methods for dynamic control of queueing networks. The most closely related papers to ours are Ata et al. (2025a, 2024a). The authors develop neural network-based computational methods for solving high-dimensional drift-rate 3and singular control problems. To be specific, Ata et al. (2025a) develops a neural network-based method for solving drift control problems for reflected Brownian motion, and Ata et al. (2024a) extends it to solve singular control problems, by approximating singular controls using drift controls, and shows its effectiveness in queueing applications. We build on Ata et al. (2025a, 2024a) by extending their work to allow a bounded convex polyhedron as the state space. Other closely related papers also develop neural network-based computational method to solve control problems. Motivated by call center applications, Ata and Ka¸ sıkaralar (2026a) focuses on limiting control problems in Halfin-Whitt heavy traffic regime, and develops a computational method for finite-horizon formulation with non-stationary data; also see Ata and Ka¸ sıkaralar (2026b). Ata and Zhou (2025) solves a sequential vehicle routing problem motivated by an eviction enforcement application following a similar approach. Similarly, Ata et al. (2025b) proposes a computational method for solving the stochastic joint replenishment problem in high dimensions by exploiting a connection among multi-dimensional impulse control problems, backward stochastic differential equations and the stochastic target problem. Those researchers build on the literature started by the seminal paper Han et al. (2018), that focuses on solving (semilinear) partial differential equations using neural networks, relying on a connection between (semilinear) partial differential equations and backward stochastic differential equations; see E et al. (2021), Beck et al. (2023), Chessari et al. (2023) and Han et al. (2025) for surveys of that literature. Prior to neural network-based methods for solving Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equations, solution to multi-dimensional stochastic control problems was usually restricted to low-dimensional case. Two numerical methods for solving singular control problems are proposed in Kushner and Martins (1991) and Kumar and Muthuraman (2004). Kushner and Martins (1991) approximates the singular control problems by control problems of discrete-time Markov chain that is readily solvable, which is an extension of Markov chain approximation method in Kushner (1990) for stochastic control problems. Kumar and Muthuraman (2004) combines a finite element method with policy iteration to solve their HJB equation that is based on Taksar (1997). Drift and singular control problems arise in various manufacturing applications; see for ex-ample Ata (2006), C ¸ elik and Maglaras (2008), Ata and Olsen (2009), Rubino and Ata (2009), Ormeci Matoglu and Vande Vate (2011) and Ormeci Matoglu et al. (2015) for related models of make-to-order manufacturing system, and Wein (1992), Veatch and Wein (1996), Caldentey and Wein (2006), Ata and Barjesteh (2023) for related models of make-to-stock systems. Also see Dai and Yao (2013) for a summary of other applications. Other applications of drift and singular control problems include power control in wireless communication (Ata et al. 2005), ride hailing (Ata et al. 2020, Alwan et al. 2024), volunteer capacity management for nonprofits (Ata et al. 2019, 2024b), flexible staffing in call center (Sun and Liu 2025), and drone delivery systems (Liu and Sun 2022). 43 Model 

We consider a make-to-order manufacturing system selling K different products to price-sensitive customers. We model this system as a multiclass queueing network, serving K customer types. In our model, type k customer orders follow a pre-determined route through a sequence of buffers, denoted by ( k, 1) , . . . , (k, n k), denoting their manufacturing process ( k = 1 , . . . , K ). In other words, they go through nk production stages. During stage l (l = 1 , . . . , n k), they are held in buffer ( k, l ) that is dedicated to holding them. Thus, a total of m = PKk=1 nk buffers hold jobs of various types that are in different stages of production. In what follows, we use the terms buffer and class interchangeably. To repeat, the class of a job corresponds to the combination of the product type and the stage of production it is in currently. We assume jobs of various classes are served by a total of p servers. 6.4 Reference Process 

A key element of the computational method is the reference policy that we choose as one involving constant drift controls, i.e., θ(t) = ˜θ and ψ(t) = ˜ψ for t ≥ 0. Also letting B(t) = ξ(t) − γt for t ≥ 0so that B is a driftless Brownian motion with covariance matrix Γ, the evolution of the controlled workload process ˜W under the reference policy can then be expressed as follows: ˜W (t) = ˜W (0) + γt + B(t) + ˜θt + ˜G ˜ψt + ˆG ˜Υ( t), (62) 16 where ˜Υ is the backup singular process that uses the pushing direction ˆ gj on face Fj of the state space W for j = 1 , . . . , J . We refer to the process ˜W (·) as the reference process. Intuitively, one seeks to choose a reference policy ( ˜θ, ˜ψ) so that the resulting sample paths of the reference process tend to occupy parts of the state space that the optimal policy is expected to visit frequently. This has been observed in prior literature, e.g., Ata et al. (2025a) and Ata and Ka¸ sıkaralar (2026a). In our context, the reference process should also generate sufficiently many samples near the boundary, especially the upper faces where the costly controls may be used. Thus, learning the gradient is essential for making sound control decisions. Appendix C.1 argues that one can choose the pushing directions of the backup singular control ˜Υ so that the reference process ˜W (·) is well defined for our problem.

7.3 Proposed Scheduling Policy

7.4 Choosing Policy Parameters 

Our proposed policy in Sections 7.1–7.3 includes policy parameters ρ, ϵn, s, ˜ τ , ˜λ, ˜ c. Regarding band-width ϵn, we choose it so that 

ϵn = ϵ · nκ, κ ∈ (0 , 1/2) , (87) where κ is a constant and ϵ is a tuning parameter. The requirement κ ∈ (0 , 1/2) balances two considerations. First, since κ − 1/2 < 0, ϵn is small relative to √n, thus the scaled band enclosing the state space is also small. Second, because ϵn ≫ 1 as n becomes large, the probability that the workload process moves beyond the band is negligible. Now we have a family of parameters to determine: ρ, ϵ, s, ˜ τ , ˜λ, ˜ c. For simplicity, we use a common safety-stock parameter s; that is, sk,l = s for all ( k, l ). We determine the best value of these parameters via simulation-based search, where the objective is to ensure delay probabilities of all routes do not exceed ε = 1% while minimizing cost in the original system. To be specific, for each candidate value of ρ, we first solve the corresponding Brownian control problem and obtain the neural-network approximation of the gradient function Gw2 (·; ρ) (see Section 6). We then evaluate the full grid of the remaining five parameters using this gradient Gw2 (·; ρ). Repeating the above procedure for every candidate value of ρ amounts to an exhaustive search over the full six-dimensional parameter grid. Although the search is six-dimensional, the candidate sets for several parameters are small (e.g., safety stock s and scheduling form ˜ τ ∈ { abs , rel }), resulting in a total number of parameter combinations on the order of 10 4 in our test problems, making this brute-force search feasible; see search grid of each parameter in Table 9 of Appendix E.3.

We study the dynamic control problem of multiclass queueing networks with throughput-time con-straints, and develop an effective policy in high-dimensional settings. Our approach approximates the original problem by a Brownian control problem, reduces it to a lower-dimensional workload problem, and solves the resulting HJB equation using neural networks. This reduction provides both computational tractability and structural interpretability. Computationally, we are able to extend existing computational methods in Ata et al. (2025a, 2024a) to the bounded convex polyhedral state spaces arising in our setting. Despite its tractability and interpretability, translating the solution of the workload problem into implementable policy in the original system requires an intricate policy design. First, we select the proposed singular control only among those pushing towards interior of the state space. Second, we introduce necessary modifications in the prelimit policy, such as bands and safety stocks, which allow the workload solution to be implemented in the original queueing network. Third, we propose a scheduling policy that builds on maximum-pressure policy and is tailored for our setting with throughput-time constraints. Overall, our policy consistently outperforms the benchmark policies by 5%–10% in our test problems. In extending the computational method in Ata et al. (2025a, 2024a) to our bounded-state-space setting, we observe an additional tradeoff in reference-policy design. Besides adequately covering regions frequently visited under the optimal policy, the reference process should also generate suffi-ciently many samples near the boundary, particularly the upper faces. Such coverage is important for two reasons. First, the boundary conditions play a major role in shaping the gradient func-tion throughout the state space. Second, costly singular controls are activated around the boundary, making accurate approximation there essential for control decisions. Our numerical results illustrates this tradeoff: moderately positive reference drifts lead to successful training, whereas the proposed drift controls are mostly negative. Intuitively, the former generates more visits to the upper faces, while the latter tends to avoid such costly visits.

D.1 Outsourcing and Discretionary Idling Policies 

In this subsection, we introduce two alternative benchmark policies for outsourcing and discretionary idling. We refer to these alternatives as the single-pushing-control-per-face policy and the threshold-based policy. The former assigns a single pushing control to each face of the state space of the workload process but otherwise mimics our proposed policy. Thus, unlike our proposed policy, it does not partition each face into subsets and assign a different pushing direction to each subset. The threshold-based policy outsources an arriving job of a product whenever the total number of jobs of that product in the system exceeds a specified threshold.

D.4 Choosing Policy Parameters 

The policy parameters for each of our 12 benchmark policies include policy parameters for its three components; see Table 6. We determine these parameters via simulation-based search, where we seek to minimize costs while ensuring that the probability of violating the delay constraints does not exceed ε = 1%. Let us elaborate by separately considering two groups of benchmark policies. The first group includes 8 benchmark policies using least-relative-slack policy or least-relative-slack policy based on queue lengths. In this group, the policy parameters include ρ, s, ϵ (if using single-pushing policy), and pricing parameters (see Panel B of Table 6). For these benchmark policies, we perform a joint search for all policy parameters, and select the one with the lowest cost subject to the delay probability constraint ε = 1%. The second group includes 4 benchmark policies that use the proposed scheduling policy. In this group, the policy parameters include ρ, s, ϵ (if using single-pushing policy), pricing parameters (see Panel B of Table 6), and scheduling parameters ˜ τ , ˜λ, ˜ c. Due to the large number of policy parameters, a joint search over all parameters is computationally intractable. We therefore adopt the sequential approach. E.3.2 Shared-parameter Assumption in Pricing Benchmark Policies. 

As said in Remark 5, we impose a shared-parameter assumption across routes for pricing benchmark parameters. We consider two variants. The idea of first variant lies in applying a common percentage price change relative to the nominal price across routes during congestion periods. For each test problem, policy and tuning parameter, we simulate under the following setup. 

Number of paths and simulation length. We use 5 ,000 simulation paths, and each path is simulated for 100 ,000 events, where an event represents new job arrival or service completion. The discounted cost beyond the simulation horizon is approximated using a steady-state cost rate, which is estimated by the average cost rate over the last 20% of the simulation events. The number of paths and simulation length are limited by the computation burden; see Appendix E.4. 

Initial state. The initial state is set to 10 jobs at the first buffer of each route and 0 jobs elsewhere. The reported discounted cost depends on this initial state. 

Delay probability. In computing delay probability of each route, we discard jobs departing during the first 20% of the simulation events, and only include those departing during the remaining 80% simulation events. 

Random seed. For each test problem, all policies and parameter combinations share the same random seed, ensuring that relative performance differences reflect parameter choices rather than simulation noise.
