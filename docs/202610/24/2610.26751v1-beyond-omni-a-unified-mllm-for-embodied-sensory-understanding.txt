ABSTRACT
Recent embodied systems increasingly build on multimodal large language mod-
els (MLLMs) for their pretrained understanding and reasoning capabilities, but
their perception of the physical world remains largely limited to basic modalities
such as vision and audio. However, broadening MLLMs to more diverse sen-
sory modalities from the physical environment is challenging: their representa-
tions are often weakly connected to the MLLM and vary substantially in structure
and statistics, while their interpretation still requires shared high-level understand-
ing and reasoning. We therefore propose UniSense, a unified embodied MLLM
framework that supports diverse physical sensory modalities within a shared mul-
timodal architecture. UniSense follows a two-stage training process: (i) aligning
sensory representations with language to establish a language-accessible inter-
face; and (ii) tuning modality-specific experts within the shared LLM to provide
specialized processing for different sensory modalities. To support broad sen-
sory learning, we curate UniSense–1M, a large-scale embodied sensory training

INTRODUCTION
The rapid progress of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) is reshaping Embodied AI,
driving a growing line of generalist embodied models that harness MLLMs’ multimodal under-
standing and reasoning capabilities for physical-world perception and interaction. Representative
systems such as PaLM-E (Driess et al., 2023), RoboBrain (Ji et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2025a),
RynnBrain (Dang et al., 2026) and Vesta (Bjorck et al., 2026) demonstrate the potential of this
paradigm. However, while modern MLLMs have developed strong capabilities across modalities
such as images, video, and audio, their support for diverse physical signals remains limited.
The real world contains rich information that can be captured by embodied sensory modalities,
such as depth, thermal, tactile, and radar. These modalities provide complementary physical cues
about geometry, contact, temperature, and dynamics beyond basic modality inputs. As Figure 1
illustrates, such cues can clarify physical states that remain ambiguous in basic observations. Ac-
cordingly, recent work has explored three main strategies for incorporating sensory modalities into
language-centric models. (i) Specialists develop dedicated interfaces or adaptation mechanisms for
individual sensory modalities (Li et al., 2025d; Lai et al., 2026; Asif Imran Shouborno et al., 2025).
(ii) Binders (Girdhar et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024) use image or language representations as seman-
tic anchors to align different modalities within a shared representation space. (iii) Generalists (Han
et al., 2024; Moon et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b) integrate multiple modalities
through modality-specific encoders, projectors, or routing modules. Despite these differences, most
sensory modality adaptation is still concentrated at the modality interface, before entering the LLM.
A more detailed discussion of related work is provided in Appendix §A.
This emphasis largely reflects the maturity gap in modality interfaces between embodied sensory and
widely studied basic modalities. On the one hand, the latter benefit from well-established modality-
specific encoders, web-scale multimodal data, and widely adopted alignment recipes (Radford et al.,
2021; Chu et al., 2024; Tschannen et al., 2025), whereas many embodied sensory modalities still
lack comparable resources, making sensory–language grounding a primary challenge. On the other
hand, despite heterogeneous low-level representations, these modalities ultimately rely on shared
high-level understanding and reasoning, motivating a unified LLM backbone. For basic modal-
ities, mature interfaces have further enabled omni-modal models to move beyond and introduce
modality-dependent computation within the LLM (Li et al., 2025c; Inclusion AI, 2025; Shukor et al.,
2025). For embodied sensory modalities, such LLM-side specialization remains limited: existing
methods largely share the same parameters across representations with substantially different struc-
tures, statistics, and spatiotemporal characteristics. Prior studies suggest that such parameter sharing
across modalities or tasks can introduce conflicting learning signals (Chen et al., 2024c; Liang et al.,
2025; Hao et al., 2026). We therefore view unified embodied sensory modeling across two levels:
semantic grounding at the modality–language interface, followed by computational specialization
within the shared LLM.
In this paper, we present UniSense, a unified MLLM for embodied sensory understanding. UniSense
supports diverse embodied sensory modalities through lightweight modality tokenizers, a unified
encoder, modality projectors, and a shared LLM. Building on this architecture, UniSense follows a
two-stage training process to address the above challenges. (i) Aligning different sensory representa-
tions with language through caption supervision, making physical sensory information accessible to
the LLM, and then (ii) tuning modality-specific experts within the LLM, allowing different sensory
modalities to specialize beyond the shared backbone computation.
To support UniSense, we develop a data construction pipeline that applies modality-specific prepro-
cessing to heterogeneous sensory observations and pairs the processed inputs with modality-aware
captions and instruction–response pairs. This results in a large-scale training corpus of 1M samples
spanning 8 embodied sensory modalities, alongside general multimodal data. We further evaluate

METHOD
2.1
PROBLEM DEFINITION
We consider a unified embodied sensory understanding task, where a multimodal LLM supports
heterogeneous modalities under a shared framework. Let M denote the set of supported non-text
modalities, including basic modalities (image, video, and audio), and embodied sensory modalities
Ms ⊆M. In this work, Ms comprises depth, point cloud, IMU, tactile, thermal, radar, motion,
and smell. For modality m ∈M, we denote its input by xm. The language instruction provided to
the model is denoted by q.
Our goal is to incorporate embodied sensory modalities in Ms into a MLLM while considering both
how their representations are grounded in the model’s language space and how they are subsequently
processed within the shared backbone.
2.2
MODEL ARCHITECTURE
Figure 2 illustrates the architecture of UniSense, which consists of modality-specific tokenizers, a
unified encoder, modality-specific projectors, and a shared LLM. We build UniSense on pretrained
Qwen3.5 models (Qwen, 2026) at 2B and 9B scales, with preliminary multimodal initialization on
image and audio data but no additional video-specific training (see Appendix §C.3 and §C.6).
For an embodied sensory modality s ∈Ms, the input xs is first transformed into encoder-compatible
tokens by a tokenizer Ts. We adopt two tokenization pathways: pixel-aligned modalities (e.g., depth,
thermal, and tactile) reuse the standard visual patch tokenizer, whereas non-pixel modalities (e.g.,
point cloud, IMU, and radar) employ dedicated modality-specific tokenizers. The resulting tokens

are then processed by a unified encoder E and mapped to the hidden space of the language model
through a modality-specific projector Ps:
Zs = Ps(E(Ts(xs))) ,
(1)
where Zs denotes the resulting sensory representation. Image, video, and audio are processed
through their corresponding input pathways. Accordingly, we use Zm to denote the resulting repre-
sentation of any non-text modality m ∈M provided to the LLM. Detailed tokenizer and projector
designs are provided in Appendix §C.2.
The resulting modality representations {Zm} are combined with the language instruction q and
processed by the LLM to generate the response. Although these interfaces make heterogeneous
inputs compatible with the pretrained backbone, they do not ensure effective language grounding or
appropriate processing. We address these two aspects through the two training stages described in
§2.3 and §2.4.

Unlike basic modalities, which have been well studied,
embodied sensory modalities lack comparably mature
language-aligned representations, making their physical
semantics difficult to ground in the target MLLM. Prior
work aligns heterogeneous modalities in shared embed-
ding spaces (Girdhar et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024),
while generative–contrastive frameworks further show
that generative supervision and contrastive learning pro-
vide complementary semantic signals (Yu et al., 2022;
Xiao et al., 2025).
However, such alignment is typi-
cally defined outside the representation structure of the
target MLLM that ultimately consumes these modality
features. Recent MLLM studies suggest that grounding
modality representations toward the pretrained linguistic
structure of the target LLM can improve their integra-
tion (Masry et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026).
To align embodied sensory representations with lan-
guage, we construct a modality-aware caption for each
sensory input that explicitly verbalizes its physical in-
formation. We use each caption to supervise sensory
representations in two complementary ways:
condi-
tional captioning encourages sensory representations to
retain physical semantics recoverable through the tar-
get MLLM’s native language interface, while contrastive

MLLM itself to establish explicit sensory–language cor-

For a sensory modality s ∈Ms, let Zs ∈RNs×d denote its projected sensory representation and
C = (c1, . . . , cL) the corresponding modality-aware caption.
Conditional Captioning. We impose language-level supervision on sensory representations by
predicting their paired modality-aware captions. Given the projected sensory tokens Zs, a modality-
specific caption decoder Cs predicts each token of C using causal self-attention over preceding
caption tokens and cross-attention to Zs. The decoder reuses the token embeddings and vocabulary
projection of the target LLM, encouraging Zs to preserve physical semantics that can be recovered
through the model’s native language interface. We optimize this branch with the standard autore-
gressive token-level cross-entropy loss, denoted by Lcap.
Contrastive Alignment. We further establish representation-level correspondence between sensory
inputs and their captions using representations induced by the target LLM. Specifically, Zs and the

embedded caption Emb(C) are independently processed by the same first k frozen LLM layers,
followed by modality-specific aggregation and separate lightweight projection heads:
us = Norm(ϕs(ρs(Q1:k(Zs)))) ,
uc = Norm(ϕc(ρc(Q1:k(Emb(C))))) .
(2)
Here, ρs and ρc denote the sensory and caption aggregation operations, respectively, ϕs and ϕc
are separate projection heads, and Norm(·) denotes ℓ2 normalization. For a batch of B sensory–
caption pairs, representations from the same sensory instance form a positive pair, while those from
different sensory instances serve as in-batch negatives. We optimize the resulting representations
with a standard symmetric InfoNCE loss, denoted by Lcon.

TRAINING
After the preliminary image–audio initialization described in §2.2, we train UniSense on a uni-
fied collection of embodied sensory datasets spanning 8 sensory modalities. Each sensory sample
is paired with a modality-aware caption used for sensory representation alignment. Detailed data
sources and sampling strategies are provided in §B.2.
Training proceeds in two stages. The first aligns sensory representations with language, while the
second tunes modality-specific experts within the shared LLM.
Alignment Stage. We freeze the pretrained LLM backbone and optimize the newly introduced
sensory tokenizers, projectors, caption decoders, and contrastive projection heads. Given a sensory
input and its instruction, LLM is the autoregressive cross-entropy loss over the target response to-
kens. The paired modality-aware caption further provides the captioning objective Lcap and the
contrastive objective Lcon introduced in §2.3. The overall objective is:
L(1) = LLM + λalign (Lcap + λconLcon) ,
(5)
where λalign controls the overall contribution of the alignment objectives, and λcon determines the
relative weight of the contrastive objective.
Tuning Stage. The pretrained LLM backbone remains frozen, while we optimize the sensory mod-
ules, modality routers, and residual experts. We retain the language-modeling and captioning objec-
tives, but disable the contrastive branch to permit task-driven specialization. We further apply the
load-balancing loss Lbal and expert-diversity loss Ldiv to regularize expert learning. The second-
stage objective is:
L(2) = LLM + λalignLcap + λbalLbal + λdivLdiv,
(6)
where λalign, λbal, and λdiv weight the captioning, load-balancing, and expert-diversity terms, re-
spectively. Detailed settings are provided in Appendix §C.6.

Alignment Objectives. Figure 5(a) compares conditional captioning and contrastive alignment.

suggesting that modality-specific computation reduces interference across heterogeneous modalities
and better preserves complementary cross-modal information.

CONCLUSION
We present UniSense, a unified MLLM for understanding diverse embodied sensory signals beyond
basic multimodal inputs. By grounding heterogeneous sensory representations in language and in-
troducing modality-specific processing within the shared LLM, UniSense enables 8 physical sensing
modalities to be modeled under a common architecture. Extensive experiments show that this uni-
9


fied formulation performs strongly across heterogeneous sensory tasks and can effectively integrate
sensory signals with existing multimodal inputs. Taken together, these results suggest that pretrained
MLLMs can move beyond serving as interfaces for new modalities, providing a shared foundation
for understanding heterogeneous physical signals without treating each modality as an isolated sys-
tem. More broadly, our findings point toward embodied multimodal models that share a common
reasoning backbone while retaining the specialization required by different sensing processes.

RELATED WORK
Generalist Embodied Models. Recent advances in MLLMs have motivated a growing line of gen-
eralist embodied models that leverage pretrained multimodal backbones for unified perception and
reasoning in physical environments. Early systems such as PaLM-E (Driess et al., 2023) demon-
strated the potential of integrating embodied observations into large language models, while subse-
quent models such as RoboBrain (Ji et al., 2025; Cao et al., 2025a), RynnBrain (Dang et al., 2026),
and Vesta (Bjorck et al., 2026) further broaden this paradigm across diverse physical-world tasks
and environments. More recent efforts further emphasize spatial-temporal perception and high-level
reasoning (Zhao et al., 2025; Mo et al., 2025; Team et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2026a; Yuan et al.,
2026), while benchmarks such as OpenEQA (Majumdar et al., 2024) and FoMER (Dissanayake
et al., 2025) reflect the growing focus on embodied reasoning. In parallel, vision-language-action
models map visual observations directly to low-level robot control (Ghosh et al., 2024; Black et al.,
2024; Kim et al., 2025), targeting action generation rather than the high-level sensory understanding
considered in this work. Despite this progress, these generalist models still rely largely on ba-
sic modalities such as vision and audio, and their extensions beyond these modalities are typically
restricted to a single additional modality. Heterogeneous physical signals such as depth, contact,
temperature, inertial dynamics, and fine-grained geometry are therefore rarely available to a single
embodied reasoner, even though dedicated models exist for many of them individually.
Heterogeneous Multimodal LLMs. A parallel line of work extends LLMs to increasingly di-
verse input modalities. Image-text pretraining first provides a language-anchored semantic space
through large-scale cross-modal alignment (Yu et al., 2022; Tschannen et al., 2025), which sub-
sequent binding-based methods (Girdhar et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024) reuse as a pivot to align
heterogeneous modalities. Unified multimodal frameworks (Moon et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b)
instead attach multiple modality encoders to a common LLM, enabling a broader set of input types
and tasks within one framework. Audio and speech models likewise benefit from large-scale paired
supervision, providing mature language-aligned interfaces for acoustic inputs and spoken instruc-
tions (Chu et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2026). Other works develop dedicated interfaces
for individual physical signals, including depth (Cai et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2024; Daxberger et al.,
2025), point clouds (Hong et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024), inertial and time-series
data (Asif Imran Shouborno et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025d), touch (Fu et al., 2024), thermal (Jiang
et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025b; Thushara et al., 2026), radar (Lai et al., 2026; Zhou & Yang, 2026),
smell (Zhou et al., 2025c;b), and motion (Jiang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026a;b).
However, sensory data generally receive far less linguistic supervision than web-scale image-text
data, making their physical content difficult to expose fully to language-based reasoning, and each
of these interfaces is designed for one modality in isolation. In contrast, UniSense uses caption-
guided supervision to make sensory semantics language-accessible and covers 8 embodied sensory
modalities within a single model.
Multimodal Fusion and Specialization. Beyond modality alignment, multimodal models also dif-
fer in how heterogeneous representations are integrated into language reasoning. Encoder-projector
architectures (Liu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2023) typically perform late fusion, offer-
ing a simple interface but limiting deeper and fine-grained cross-modal interaction. A related line
of work unifies modalities on the encoder side, reusing a shared encoder or a pre-aligned binding
space as a single interface to the language model (Han et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024), which makes
modality extension straightforward but leaves modality-specific structure largely unmodeled. Na-
tive early-fusion models (Chameleon, 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b) process multiple
modalities within a shared representation space, enabling richer interaction but potentially increas-
ing cross-modal interference. Recent sparse architectures seek a better balance between sharing
and specialization: mixture-of-experts methods (Shen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025c; Lin et al., 2026;
Zhou et al., 2025a) introduce specialized expert pathways, with low-rank expert variants (Chen
et al., 2024c) reducing the cost of such specialization, while Mixture-of-Transformers and related
architectures (Liang et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026) more explicitly decouple
modality-specific computation while retaining shared attention. These mechanisms, however, have
been developed primarily for semantically well-aligned modalities such as image, video, and audio.
UniSense instead targets weakly grounded embodied sensory signals, keeping self-attention shared
while adding lightweight modality-specific residual experts to selected feed-forward layers.

B.2.1
GENERAL MULTIMODAL
Following the multimodal initialization pipeline of Zhong et al. (2025), we first establish the image–
speech–text capabilities of UniSense before sensory training.
Speech alignment uses Common
Voice (Ardila et al., 2020) and LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015), comprising approximately
1.19M speech–text pairs. We then perform image–speech–text instruction tuning on a mixture of
Lyra-SFT (Zhong et al., 2025) and selected subsets of FineVision (Wiedmann et al., 2025), contain-
ing approximately 3.12M conversations. The mixture consists primarily of Lyra-SFT together with
additional FineVision image–text data.
We evaluate the general multimodal capabilities on image–text, image–speech, and video bench-
marks.
Specifically, we use TextVQA (Singh et al., 2019), MME (Fu et al., 2025a), and
MMBench (Liu et al., 2024c) for image–text understanding; the speech-conditioned variants of
TextVQA, DocVQA (Mathew et al., 2021), and ChartQA (Masry et al., 2022) for image–speech un-
derstanding; and Video-MME (Fu et al., 2025b) and EgoSchema (Mangalam et al., 2023) for video
understanding. We follow the standard evaluation protocols for all benchmarks, with Video-MME
evaluated without subtitles.
B.2.2
DEPTH
For depth training, we use SpatialQA (Cai et al., 2025), SPAR (Zhang et al., 2026b), and CA-
VQA (Daxberger et al., 2025). We additionally construct depth-oriented training samples from
COCO (Mishra et al., 2019), Visual Genome (VG) (Krishna et al., 2017), and SA-1B (Kirillov et al.,
2023). These datasets cover diverse depth-centric scenes and task formats, including spatial question
answering, 3D reasoning, captioning, and general multimodal instruction tuning. We synthesize
speech with CosyVoice (Du et al., 2024) for SpatialQA, SPAR, CA-VQA, and part of the constructed
depth subset. For samples without depth maps, we generate them using Depth Anything V2 (Yang
et al., 2024).
For sensory representation alignment, each RGB–D sample is paired with a depth-aware description

jects from RGB and then generates a depth-focused caption conditioned on the RGB image, depth
map, and extracted objects.
We evaluate depth understanding on SpatialBench and RoboSpatial-Home (Song et al., 2025) using
paired RGB–D observations. SpatialBench comprises five subtasks: positional reasoning, object
existence, counting, reaching, and size comparison. We report each subtask score, together with

B.2.3
POINT CLOUD
For point-cloud training, we use CLEVR3D-REAL (Yan et al., 2023), ScanQA (Azuma et al., 2022),
and SceneVerse (Jia et al., 2024). Together, these datasets cover scene-level descriptions, question
answering, and compositional reasoning over indoor 3D scenes. We use the official training splits
of ScanQA and CLEVR3D-REAL. For ScanQA, we further construct RGB-augmented examples
using single-view and four-view images from MV-ScanQA (Mo et al., 2025), yielding both point-
cloud-only and point-cloud–image training inputs. For sensory representation alignment, scene-
level descriptions from SceneVerse are matched to ScanQA and CLEVR3D-REAL samples by scene
identity.
We evaluate point-cloud understanding on ScanQA and CLEVR3D-REAL (results reported in Ta-
ble D15), using their official validation splits. On ScanQA, we evaluate both point-cloud-only (xyz)
and point-cloud–image (xyz+img) inputs. Following the standard ScanQA protocol, we report
CIDEr, METEOR (MET.), ROUGE-L (R-L), BLEU-1 (B-1), and BLEU-4 (B-4) for open-ended 3D
question answering. CLEVR3D-REAL evaluates compositional reasoning over point-cloud scenes
across existence, counting, comparison, query-attribute, query-object, and compare-attribute ques-
tions. We report accuracy for each question category, together with Overall, denoting accuracy
over all test questions, and Cls. Avg., denoting the unweighted average accuracy across question
categories.
B.2.4
IMU
For IMU training, we use the SensorCaps and OpenSQA annotations (Asif Imran Shouborno et al.,
2025) associated with four human activity recognition datasets: HHAR (Stisen et al., 2015), Motion-
Sense (Malekzadeh et al., 2019), Shoaib (Shoaib et al., 2014), and UCI-HAR (Anguita et al., 2013).
SensorCaps provides a natural-language description for each IMU window and is used for sensory
representation alignment, while OpenSQA provides question–answer pairs covering activity-related
understanding and reasoning. We construct the training and evaluation sets using stratified window-
25


level hold-out within each source dataset, reserving approximately 100 windows per activity class
for evaluation and using the remaining windows for training, with no overlap between the two splits.
This results in approximately 23.8k training windows and 2.5k held-out evaluation windows, with
multiple OpenSQA questions associated with each training window.
We evaluate IMU understanding as open-ended activity classification on the held-out windows from
HHAR, MotionSense, Shoaib, and UCI-HAR, following the LLaSA (Asif Imran Shouborno et al.,
2025) classification protocol. For each dataset, the model is provided with the IMU sequence and
generates the predicted class name directly from the input. We report results separately for the four
datasets using accuracy and macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1.
B.2.5
TACTILE
For tactile training, we use Octopi (Yu et al., 2024) and the Universal Visuo-Tactile Video Under-
standing corpus (VTBS) (Xie et al., 2025). Both datasets follow object-level splits, such that objects
used for evaluation are excluded from training. Following the official data generation protocols,
we construct 50k instruction–response pairs from each corpus, covering tactile property description,
comparison, selection, and object–property reasoning, resulting in approximately 100k training ex-
amples in total. The original GelSight (Yuan et al., 2017) videos are converted into a unified image-
based representation by first selecting informative motion frames and then uniformly sampling three
RGB frames from each video, which are treated as tactile observations during training. For sensory
representation alignment, we extract the descriptive property text from the generated responses and
use it as the corresponding tactile caption.
We evaluate on the official held-out-object splits of Octopi using the same three-frame tactile repre-
sentation as in training. Octopi contains 498 questions across property comparison (PC), property
superlative selection (PSS), and property–object matching (POM). Following the official protocol,
we report accuracy separately for the three tasks.
B.2.6
THERMAL
For thermal training, we use a partial subset of IR-TD from IRGPT (Cao et al., 2025b), the ther-
mal subset of VS-TDX (Chung et al., 2026), and Infrared-LLaVA (Jiang et al., 2024). Together,
these datasets cover thermal captioning, question answering, recognition, counting, and localiza-
tion. For IR-TD, we use its caption, instruction-QA, and task-oriented training subsets, and pair
thermal images with the corresponding RGB observations when available. For sensory representa-
tion alignment, we use the paired RGB–thermal captions provided by IR-TD.
We evaluate thermal understanding on IF-Bench (Zhang et al., 2026c) (results reported in Ta-
ble D14), and the Benchmark from Infrared-LLaVA, using thermal inputs for both benchmarks.
IF-Bench is evaluated in a zero-shot setting and groups its tasks into three categories: coarse-grained
perception (Coarse), fine-grained perception (Fine), and image reasoning (Reason). We report ac-
curacy for each of the ten tasks and the overall average. Infrared-LLaVA Benchmark contains six
infrared question-answering tasks covering localization, aerial counting, pedestrian counting, recog-
nition, scene understanding, and safety assessment. We report the average accuracy across the six
tasks under both zero-shot and fine-tuning settings.
B.2.7
RADAR
For radar training, we use mmWave point-cloud sequences from MM-Fi (Yang et al., 2023) to-
gether with the language annotations introduced by HoloLLM (Zhou & Yang, 2026). We follow the
HoloLLM Cross-Environment protocol, using environments E01–E03 for training and holding out
E04 for evaluation, such that the training and test sets contain disjoint environments and subjects.
The training split contains approximately 12.6k temporal radar segments. Based on the HoloLLM
action annotations, we construct a mixture of action captions and question–answering examples, and
further augment the instruction data with laterality questions and multiple-choice questions target-
ing confusable action categories, resulting in approximately 121k training examples. The original
HoloLLM captions are further refined offline using GPT-5.1 (OpenAI, 2025) with action labels as
guidance. The refined captions are used for sensory representation alignment.
26


We evaluate radar understanding following the HoloLLM Cross-Environment protocol on the held-
out E04 environment. We report results on two tasks using radar-only input: Human Action QA
and Action Caption. For Human Action QA, the model predicts one of the predefined human action
categories from the radar sequence, and performance is measured using accuracy following the
HoloLLM matching protocol. For Action Caption, the model generates a free-form description of
the observed action, and we report METEOR as the primary metric.
B.2.8
MOTION
For skeleton-motion training, we use QuAnTS (Divo et al., 2025), which contains synthetic
SMPL (Loper et al., 2023) motion sequences paired with binary, multiple-choice, and open-ended
question answering. We follow the official 80/10/10 trajectory-level split and use only the training
partition, corresponding to 24k motion sequences and 120k QA pairs. For sensory representation
alignment, we use the scene-level textual descriptions provided by QuAnTS as auxiliary captions.
We evaluate on the official QuAnTS test split, which contains 3k held-out motion trajectories and
15k questions spanning binary, multiple-choice, and open-ended QA. At inference time, the model
receives only the skeleton-motion sequence and the corresponding question, without access to scene
descriptions or ground-truth action sequences. We report binary and multiple-choice performance
using accuracy and macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1, while open-ended responses are eval-
uated using ROUGE-L and METEOR.
B.2.9
SMELL
For smell training, we use SmellNet-Base (Feng et al., 2026), which contains 50 single-substance
categories recorded with a six-channel metal-oxide (MOX) sensor array. We follow the official day-
wise split, using recordings from the first five days for training and the sixth day for evaluation,
yielding approximately 2.5k training windows and 0.5k held-out test windows after temporal seg-
mentation. We segment the aligned sensor readings into fixed-length windows and normalize each
channel using training-set statistics. From these windows, we construct approximately 50.4k instruc-
tion samples spanning classification, reasoning, and captioning, and generate temporal descriptions
that exclude the substance identity for the alignment stage.
We evaluate on the held-out day-6 split of SmellNet-Base (502 windows across 50 classes), using
training-set normalization statistics. Given a sensor sequence and the full candidate set, the model
predicts the substance name; we report Acc@1 and macro F1.

Pixel-aligned modalities. Depth, tactile, and thermal signals are represented as image-like inputs

temporal structures that cannot be directly processed by visual patch embedding. We therefore
introduce a lightweight tokenizer for each modality to map its native input into a sequence of em-

C.3
BASIC MODALITY PROCESSING
Unlike embodied sensory inputs, basic modalities retain the processing pathways inherited from the
pretrained multimodal backbone.
Image pathway. RGB images are processed by the native Qwen3.5 visual encoder and merger.
The resulting image tokens are directly inserted into the language sequence without an additional
learnable projector, and the entire pathway remains unchanged during sensory training.
Speech pathway. Speech signals are encoded using Whisper-large-v3-turbo (Radford et al., 2023).
The resulting acoustic features are mapped to the language hidden dimension through a speech
projector. The Whisper encoder remains frozen, while the speech projector is pretrained to align
acoustic representations with the language model.
Multimodal initialization. Before sensory training, UniSense is initialized from an image–speech–
text instruction-tuned Qwen3.5 checkpoint, with its original image and speech pathways retained.
C.4
SENSORY REPRESENTATION ALIGNMENT
We provide additional implementation details for the sensory representation alignment stage intro-
duced in Sec. 2.3. Unless otherwise specified, the following settings correspond to the Qwen3.5-9B
configuration.
Conditional captioning. We use a lightweight 6-layer caption decoder with a hidden dimension
of 1,024 and 16 attention heads. The decoder reuses the frozen token embeddings and language-
modeling head of Qwen, while its remaining parameters are trainable. Cross-attention uses only the
projected tokens of the target sensory modality as memory, and the decoder output is used solely
for auxiliary caption reconstruction rather than the main response-generation pathway. The caption
decoder remains trainable in both sensory-training stages.
Contrastive alignment. The projected sensory tokens and caption tokens are independently pro-
cessed by the first k frozen layers of the language backbone. In the default configuration, we use
k = 8. We apply mean pooling over valid sensory tokens and use the final valid caption token as
the textual representation. The two representations are then passed through separate LayerNorm
and linear projection heads, mapped to 512 dimensions, and ℓ2-normalized before computing the
symmetric InfoNCE loss.
Representations are gathered across all distributed workers to construct global in-batch negatives.
Pairs corresponding to the same underlying sensory instance or sharing an identical normalized
caption are excluded from the negative set to reduce false-negative supervision. The contrastive
branch is used only during the first sensory-training stage and is disabled after the transition to
modality-specific expert tuning.
C.5
MODALITY-SPECIFIC EXPERT TUNING
During the second sensory-training stage, modality-specific residual experts are activated in the se-
lected full-attention decoder layers, while the pretrained self-attention and base FFN remain frozen.
For the 9B model, expert modules are inserted into the eight full-attention layers at indices 3, 7, 11,
15, 19, 23, 27, and 31.
Expert configuration. Each embodied sensory modality uses an independent router and a pool of
E = 3 residual experts. The router produces softmax weights over all experts in the corresponding
modality-specific pool. Text, image, and speech tokens bypass these expert pools and retain the
original pretrained FFN computation.
Residual parameterization. Each expert applies low-rank residual adaptations to the gate, up, and
down projections of the backbone SwiGLU FFN. We use rank r = 32 and scaling factor α = 64,
corresponding to a residual scaling factor of α/r = 2.
Regularization. We use router load-balancing together with z-loss regularization, with a combined
weight of 10−3. An additional expert-diversity objective is applied with a target weight of 0.07,
which is linearly increased from zero during the early expert-tuning stage to stabilize optimization.

C.6
TRAINING DETAILS
Unless otherwise specified, the following settings correspond to the Qwen3.5-9B configuration; the
2B model follows the same two-stage training procedure with scale-specific configurations.
Multimodal initialization. We construct a common multimodal initialization checkpoint before
sensory training. We first connect a frozen Whisper encoder to the language model by pretraining
the speech projector. We then perform image–speech–text instruction tuning, where the language
model is adapted with rank-64 LoRA while the speech projector remains trainable. No additional
video-specific training is introduced during these preliminary stages. These stages establish the
basic-modality pathways and are not counted as part of the two-stage sensory training of UniSense.
Two-stage training. Starting from the merged multimodal checkpoint, the pretrained language
backbone and modality encoders remain frozen throughout sensory training. In the alignment Stage,
we train the sensory tokenizers, modality projectors, caption decoders, and contrastive projection
heads for 1 epoch using language-modeling, caption-reconstruction, and contrastive objectives.
The tuning Stage continues optimizing the sensory tokenizers, projectors, and caption decoders,
while activating the modality-specific routers and residual experts. The contrastive branch is dis-
abled after the stage transition, whereas caption supervision is retained together with the expert
regularization objectives. Stage 2 is trained for 2 additional epochs.
Loss configuration. During the alignment Stage, the caption and contrastive objectives are jointly
applied with the language-modeling objective. We set the contrastive weight to λcon = 0.5 and use
an overall alignment weight of λalign = 0.08 for all modalities. In the tuning Stage, the contrastive
objective is removed while caption reconstruction is retained. The router regularization is weighted
by 10−3, and the expert-diversity weight is linearly increased from 0 to 0.07 during the early expert-
tuning stage.
Optimization. We use AdamW with cosine learning-rate decay and a 5% warm-up ratio. Modality
projectors use a learning rate of 1.5 × 10−4, while the remaining trainable sensory modules use a
base learning rate of 1 × 10−4. The maximum sequence length is 2,048 for speech alignment and
4,096 for both multimodal initialization and sensory training. All models are trained in bfloat16 on
4 NVIDIA H200 GPUs.
