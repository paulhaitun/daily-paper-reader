Abstract. Real-time directed acyclic graph (DAG) scheduling provides a rigorous basis for timing analysis and efficiency in component-oriented real-time systems (CORTS) such as autonomous driving systems, yet chal-lenges remain from the perspectives of both theorists and practitioners. For theorists, no OS natively supports the DAG task model; algorithms are therefore evaluated only analytically or in simulation, ignoring real-hardware overheads. For practitioners, today’s CORTS platforms (ROS 2 and AUTOSAR Adaptive Platform) are designed for service composition rather than for timing analyzability; adopting DAG scheduling research therefore demands substantial code changes and developer discipline. This paper proposes Awkernel, the first open-source real-time operat-ing system (RTOS) that natively supports real-time DAG scheduling research, bridging DAG scheduling theory and CORTS practice. For theorists, Awkernel is a real-hardware testbed: it exposes a modular OS-native DAG scheduler for easy algorithm implementation, plus a random workload generator and built-in metrics for evaluation. For practitioners, Awkernel is not a full replacement for today’s platforms but a CORTS platform maximizing timing analyzability: its familiar publish/subscribe model keeps porting mechanical, mapping each callback to one subtask. Moreover, its function-as-subtask (FasS) API enforces DAG semantics (precedence constraints) without programmer discipline, yielding appli-cations that match the theoretical DAG model and directly inherit its efficiency and safety guarantees. On a Raspberry Pi 4, microbenchmarks confirm microsecond-level scheduling overheads; a global earliest deadline first (GEDF) scheduler fits in 226 lines; and a practitioner ports part of an autonomous driving system using only the FasS API, without reasoning about DAG semantics.

# 1 Introduction 

Modern cyber-physical systems, exemplified by autonomous driving systems, are typically realized as component-oriented real-time systems (CORTS) in which a collection of units implementing minimal functionalities cooperatively achieve the system-level objectives through mutual message-passing communication [1, 2]. Representative CORTS platforms include ROS 2 [3, 4] (used in autonomous driv-ing stacks such as Autoware [5]) and the AUTOSAR Adaptive Platform (AP) [6] (the automotive-industry standard for high-performance electronic control units (ECUs)). Both are POSIX-based middleware layers that delegate scheduling deci-sions to the underlying OS [7, 8]. Each unit is implemented as callbacks triggered by timers or incoming messages that publish to downstream channels, forming a system-wide dataflow whose timing requirements the underlying platform must satisfy efficiently. Real-time directed acyclic graph (DAG) scheduling offers a rigorous foundation for timing analysis in such systems by explicitly representing intra-task parallelism and dependencies [9]. Yet no publicly available OS or runtime currently treats the DAG task model as a first-class OS abstraction, that is, a concept the kernel itself represents in its internal data structures, exposes through dedicated scheduling APIs, and whose semantic constraints are enforced by the OS scheduler rather than by a user-space runtime. DAG-aware techniques (global earliest deadline first (GEDF) [10], reservation-based scheduling [11], fluid scheduling [12], and adaptive scheduling [13]) therefore cannot run on real hardware, and proposed DAG algorithms are evaluated only by analysis or simulation, hiding overheads that only real hardware exposes. Although a ROS 2 system superficially resembles a DAG, its execution semantics diverge from the theoretical DAG model due to middleware-level behaviors such as executor-specific callback scheduling [14–16] and customizable message synchronization [17]. A large body of work has analyzed and improved the timing behavior of ROS 2 executors [14–19], but the resulting two-level scheduling complicates analysis and yields ROS 2-specific models that cannot reuse the DAG scheduling literature at the OS scheduling layer. This is also a practical concern: modern CORTS such as autonomous driving stacks already carry substantial application-level complexity, so it is preferable that the platform beneath them contribute as little complexity as possible to the timing argument on which safety assurance rests. From this perspective, scheduling behavior arising from the interaction of two independently designed layers leaves room for improvement. Efforts to apply DAG scheduling directly to ROS 2 [1, 2, 20] face two further obstacles: callback description is free enough to break strict DAG semantics [1], and no platform offers runtime control based on DAG information. Consequently, CORTS practitioners cannot readily exploit the rich legacy of real-time DAG scheduling. 

Research questions (RQ): Building on the above background, this work formulates the following three research questions: 1. What differences exist between the DAG model and the models adopted by representative CORTS platforms, and what requirements must system Awkernel: Bridging DAG Scheduling Theory and Practice 3

software satisfy to bridge these differences and enable DAG scheduling on real hardware? 2. How can DAG scheduling theorists evaluate their algorithms on real hardware with low implementation effort? 3. How can practitioners of CORTS leverage the existing body of research on DAG scheduling? 

Awkernel is the first open-source real-time operating system (RTOS) that natively supports real-time DAG scheduling research, bridging DAG scheduling theorists and practitioners of CORTS. For theorists, it is a real-hardware testbed for DAG scheduling algorithms; for practitioners, it is not a complete replacement for ROS 2 but a CORTS platform maximizing timing analyzability and direct reuse of the DAG scheduling literature. Concretely, Awkernel (i) is open-source, unlike commercial AP implementations; (ii) treats DAG structure as a first-class OS abstraction, unlike platforms that delegate scheduling to a generic POSIX OS; and (iii) exposes a scheduler interface for implementing, swapping, and measuring DAG scheduling algorithms on real hardware. Awkernel provides a ROS 2-like publish/subscribe model and the function-as-subtask (FasS) API [1], an OS-level interface for native DAG scheduling that represents each subtask as a function whose arguments and return values are the input and output edges of the DAG. By minimizing the freedom of subtask description, the OS enforces DAG semantics without relying on programmer discipline. Awkernel further pairs a scheduler with arbitrary preemption granularity, a DAG management substrate tracking runtime metadata, and a scheduler interface decoupling algorithms from OS internals; practitioners apply any DAG scheduling algorithm via the FasS API, and theorists evaluate algorithms on real hardware with minimal effort. It further includes a random DAG workload generator built on RD-Gen [21] and in-kernel metrics covering context-switch counts, preemption overhead, and DAG response times, enabling comprehensive evaluation of algorithm generality and performance. 

Contributions: The main contributions of this paper include the following: 

– We analyze the structural differences between the theoretical DAG model and POSIX-based CORTS platforms (ROS 2 and AUTOSAR Adaptive Platform), derive the conditions and abstractions required to realize DAG scheduling on real hardware, and confirm Awkernel satisfies them. 

– The integration of a scheduler that supports arbitrary preemption granularity, together with a DAG management substrate, the FasS API, and a random DAG workload generation facility into Awkernel establishes a comprehensive platform. This environment facilitates the straightforward implementation of DAG scheduling algorithms and enables evaluation on real hardware.

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Theoretical Real-Time DAG Scheduling 

Theoretical real-time DAG scheduling addresses computational complexity, re-source heterogeneity, and multicore timing guarantees across autonomous, auto-motive/avionics, edge/IoT, and high-performance computing workloads, yet how these advances are validated in practice remains unclear. To address this ques-tion, we survey 27 papers on real-time scheduling for DAG tasks from ECRTS, RTSS, and RTAS over the past 15 years [11, 20, 22–46]. Table 1 shows all rely on simulations and schedulability tests; none use real hardware. This reinforces the problem in Section 1. The only widely accepted real-time scheduling testbed, LITMUS RT [47], is a Linux-based plugin framework for empirically comparing partitioned, global, and Pfair multiprocessor algorithms under realistic overheads (scheduling latencies, context-switch costs), but it targets independent sequential tasks and lacks DAG support; no DAG-scheduling testbed exists, a gap Awkernel fills. Awkernel continues this testbed lineage, extending the target model to DAG tasks. 

2.2 Real-Time Support in CORTS Platforms 

A CORTS platform is a service-oriented middleware atop a POSIX OS, where mod-ular components communicate by asynchronous message passing. Its two dominant instances, ROS 2 and AP, share a Data Distribution Service (DDS)/SOME/IP-on-POSIX architecture [6, 7]. The single-threaded executor of ROS 2 non-preemptively dispatches callbacks in fixed-priority order [14], diverging from classical real-time schedulers and prompting dedicated analyses: response-time analysis for single-threaded [14, 15, 19] and multi-threaded [16, 48] executors, end-to-end metrics such as reaction time and data age [18], and DDS-layer synchronization bounds [17]. Motivated by these analyses, numerous enhancements have been proposed to improve the real-time performance of ROS 2: chain-aware fixed-priority scheduling (PiCAS [49]), dynamic-priority earliest deadline first (EDF) executors [50], lock-free multi-threaded execution (RTeX [51]), reconciliation with classical non-preemptive scheduling [52], flexible scheduling policies (ROS RT [53]), fully preemptive per-callback thread designs [2, 54], and zero-copy inter-process communication (IPC) via shared memory (Agnocast [55]). The AUTOSAR Adaptive Platform, introduced in 2017 for advanced driver-assistance and autonomous driving ECUs [6], layers SOME/IP and ara::com over POSIX (Linux/QNX). Lacking timing determinism, it has spawned middleware research: SL-LET [8], Logical Execution Time on POSIX [56], and response-time analysis of QNX Adaptive Partitioning [57]. Awkernel: Bridging DAG Scheduling Theory and Practice 5

YASMIN [58] is an open-source real-time middleware natively supporting a DAG task model with FIFO-channel precedence, and global and partitioned variants of EDF and fixed-priority scheduling. However, as a user-space C library on POSIX [58, §3], thread-scheduling ultimately falls to the POSIX scheduler, which lacks any notion of DAG precedence, the same two-level structure as ROS 2 and AP; Section 7.2 quantifies the cost of this structure empirically. None of ROS 2, AP, or YASMIN treats the DAG model as a first-class OS abstraction, leaving structural gaps that block practitioners from leveraging real-time DAG scheduling research (see Section 3). 

2.3 Real-Time Parallel Programming Models 

OpenMP is the de facto programming model for precedence-constrained par-allel tasks within a single process; real-time research on it includes task-graph derivation (ompTG [59]), worst-case response-time analyses for OpenMP with loops [60] and conditional branches [61], and predictable parallel programming methodologies [62]. These efforts share the goal of Awkernel but operate at a different granularity: OpenMP research addresses intra-process parallelism (subtasks sharing one address space under a runtime library), whereas Awkernel targets CORTS, where independent components cooperate via publish/subscribe (e.g., ROS 2-based autonomous driving systems). 

# 3 Differences between DAG Model and ROS 2 Model 

This paper focuses on ROS 2 within existing CORTS platforms because its implementation is open for API-level analysis and it dominates timing analyses at top real-time venues. AP is restricted by licensing and tooling [63], but shares the same two-level (middleware + POSIX OS) architecture, so our ROS 2 observations transfer directly to AP. Linux/ROS 2 is the primary CORTS development platform, and prior work has documented its structural differences from the theoretical DAG model. These differences are expected, since ROS 2 is a component middleware, not a DAG scheduling platform. Rather than cataloging them, we identify which platform properties invalidate DAG scheduling theory’s assumptions: exactly those that system software must restore for the theory to transfer (Section 4 derives the corresponding design principles). We characterize these properties along two axes, 

task model and scheduling model , contrasting the DAG and ROS 2 sides of each.

ROS 2 Task Model A ROS 2 application operates as a chain of callbacks connected through topics. Treating each callback as a subtask and message flow as precedence constraints, the callback chain resembles a DAG task; however, the ROS 2 API does not express these constraints directly and can inadvertently break them depending on usage. First, ROS 2 allows publish() anywhere in a callback; a mid-callback publish can therefore release a successor before its predecessor has logically completed. Even when all publish() calls sit at the end, blocking of earlier publishes induced by quality-of-service (QoS) settings can delay later ones, again breaking precedence. This breaks the completion boundary of Eq. (1), so any response-time bound that assumes edge activation at subtask completion no longer holds. Second, multi-input callbacks require a developer-selected message-matching policy via 

message_filters [64]; common policies ( ExactTime , ApproximateTime ) join on timestamps, not the strict AND synchronization the theoretical model assumes, so which messages form one job instance, the very object DAG analyses reason about, is decided at runtime rather than statically. Third, unless zero-copy IPC middleware (e.g., Agnocast [55]) is employed, ROS 2 incurs non-negligible communication delays; the resulting join delay violates the zero-delay release assumption of Eq. (1), introducing an unmodeled additive term into every path-length argument. 

3.2 Differences in Scheduling Models DAG Scheduling Model The scheduling model centers on a ready queue of executable subtasks, into which released subtasks are immediately enqueued, and a scheduler that selects the highest-priority one using algorithms such as rate monotonic, deadline monotonic, or EDF [9]. DAG task schedulers use three preemption models [65, 66]: fully preemptive 

(preemption anywhere; best responsiveness, highest context-switch and cache cost), non-preemptive (no preemption; zero overhead, blocking risk), and limited preemptive (preemption only between subtasks; an intermediate trade-off). Diverse dynamic algorithms have been proposed for the DAG task model (EDF-based dynamic-priority [67, 68], fluid scheduling [12], dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS)-based energy-aware [69,70], and DAG-level reservation-based scheduling [11, 26]), all assuming the scheduler can maintain and update DAG metadata at runtime. 

ROS 2 Scheduling Model The executor of ROS 2 is a user-space callback scheduler nested under the OS scheduler; SingleThreadedExecutor and Multi-ThreadedExecutor are the defaults. Each maintains a wait set of executable instances and alternates between a polling point and a processing window . # 4 Design Principle 

Building on Section 3, we enumerate seven design principles (P1 to P7) for a DAG scheduling implementation platform from three perspectives. From the real-time scheduling researcher’s perspective :

P1 The platform must provide sufficient freedom to describe arbitrary DAG scheduling algorithms and their parameters. 

P2 An algorithm expressed at the model level should be directly translatable into a working implementation without non-trivial platform-specific adaptations. From the application programmer’s perspective :

P3 The API must allow natural description of CORTS, maintaining continuity with the publish/subscribe paradigm widely adopted in the target ecosystem. 

P4 The API must preserve the DAG task structure and all precedence constraints regardless of programming practices, so programmers can write components without scheduling expertise while the platform enforces the task model. From the system perspective :

P5 Overheads causing divergence between model and runtime behavior (such as inter-subtask communication latency and release-to-enqueueing delay) must be minimized. 

P6 The system must support every mechanism arbitrary DAG scheduling al-gorithms require: arbitrary preemption granularity (fully, limited, and non-preemptive), reentrant subtasks, and OS-level runtime DAG maintenance. 

P7 The system must not introduce platform-induced complexity absent from the scheduling model, such as nested scheduling hierarchies that are artifacts of the system architecture.

Design alternatives. One may ask whether the design principles of Section 4 could be met by extending an existing platform instead of building a new RTOS. We argue they cannot, as Table 4 summarizes. (i) User-space executor extensions of ROS 2, including all recent work summarized in Table 2, remain nested under the POSIX scheduler, violating P7, and cannot make OS-level preemption decisions from DAG metadata (P6). (ii) Extending Linux, in the style of LITMUS RT [47] or sched_ext [71], retains a thread-based scheduler abstraction with no task-graph state; more fundamentally, a POSIX kernel cannot restrict how applications publish or synchronize, so DAG semantics cannot be enforced without programmer discipline (P4), and process isolation makes zero-copy communication (P5) conditional on unsafe shared-memory mechanisms. (iii) Retrofitting an existing RTOS would require replacing its task model, scheduler core, and communication substrate simultaneously, which amounts to a rewrite, while the memory-safe single address space that P5 relies on requires language-level ownership guarantees unavailable in C-based kernels. A new, small, analyzable kernel is therefore not a stylistic choice but the direct consequence of P4 to P7. # 6 Design and Implementation 

6.1 Foundation: Rust, Single Address Space, and async/await 

The minimization-of-overhead principle (P5) demands that inter-subtask commu-nication and the latency from release to enqueueing remain close to the model. A single address space directly satisfies this demand: pointer-based message passing eliminates data copies, address-space switches, and IPC marshaling. The ownership and borrow system of Rust resolves this tension by providing compile-time memory safety without any runtime mechanism, enabling the single address space to be simultaneously low-overhead and memory-safe; the same strategy has been validated by RedLeaf [72] and Theseus [73]. 6.2 Execution Model: Per-Core Workers, Preemption, and Periodic Releases 

Awkernel instantiates one scheduler object per scheduling algorithm (e.g., GEDF, Prioritized FIFO, and round-robin (RR)) as a static kernel-internal singleton that owns a run queue and implements a scheduling rule; it is not a thread, has no dedicated stack, no schedulable entity, and no spawn site, and the precedence among scheduler objects is fixed at compile time. Scheduler invocations occur at two opportunities: (i) on wake (by a publisher, sleep-timer expiration, or IPI), the wake site calls wake_task() on the appropriate scheduler object, which enqueues the woken subtask onto the run queue of that scheduler; (ii) at the top of the run loop of each per-core worker, it calls get_next_task() to dequeue the highest-priority runnable subtask, consulting scheduler objects in the fixed precedence order. Awkernel runs one worker per CPU, and the primary CPU (CPU 0) drives periodic releases of tasks: each iteration walks a time-ordered delta list to release expired periodic releases. Every other CPU runs a tight get_next_task() →

poll loop; thus there are num _cpu − 1 such workers. Preemption in Awkernel is built on a dedicated IPI on PREEMPT_IRQ , reserved at boot, and is exposed at the three granularities of P6. When wake_task() 

finds a woken subtask outranking every running and pending-preemption target across all cores, it enqueues the subtask on the preemption-pending queue of the target core and sends the IPI; the handler performs a stackful context switch to the preempting subtask. Two reduction modes complement this default fully preemptive behavior: the no_preempt Cargo feature compiles the preemption subsystem out (a non-preemptive build where .await yields are the only switch points), and wrapping a critical region in an InterruptGuard , which disables interrupts, makes it limited preemptive on demand. A single kernel-wide MCS lock [74] serializes every wake_task() and 

get_next_task() across all scheduler objects, performing in one critical section (i) the cross-core preemption decision, (ii) the run-queue enqueue/dequeue, and (iii) the per-CPU running-task table update. This eliminates priority inversion across scheduler classes. Subtask-level parallelism is preserved across multi-core: 12 A. Yano et al. 

every subtask of every DAG task is an independent async task (a Future in Rust terminology), and each is enqueued and dispatched on its own merit; this independence is exactly what realizes reentrant subtask execution required by P6, with no need for a dedicated stack pool or worker-thread cache. 

6.3 Programming Model: FasS API and Publish/Subscribe 

The FasS API is a programming model that represents each subtask in the DAG task model as a simple function f . Intuitively, the arguments and return values of f correspond, respectively, to the incoming and outgoing edges of the subtask in the DAG. Strictly speaking, however, they correspond to topics (publish/subscribe data channels), so a single argument may aggregate data flowing in from multiple incoming edges, and a single return value may feed multiple outgoing edges. The publish/subscribe (pub/sub) substrate underlying the FasS API treats each topic, within the single address space (Section 6.1), as a shared-memory channel that passes payloads by pointer; zero-copy is achieved by wrapping each payload in Arc<T> [75]. Concretely, the API is realized as a registration interface through which the application programmer supplies the following three pieces of information for each subtask: 1. A function f representing the subtask : within the body of f , operations that modify or expose the DAG structure (most notably explicit publish() 

calls) are disallowed. 2. Lists of topics : the input topic list, paired with the arguments of f , and the output topic list, paired with its return values. 3. Attributes : additional parameters required by the chosen DAG scheduling algorithm; for example, under a static-priority DAG scheduler, each FasS subtask is assigned its priority through this slot. The kernel wraps every registered f in a control loop of the form loop { args = recv_all.await; results = f(args); pubs.send_all(results).await } ,yielding two consequences that the programmer cannot violate. First, out-going publishes are structurally pinned to the tail of the subtask, match-ing the completion-boundary clause of the DAG task model (P4). Second, 

recv_all.await blocks until every input topic has supplied a message, enforcing AND-join synchronization at the API layer rather than via a developer-chosen matching policy. The FasS API provides three registration forms that map onto the source, internal, and sink roles of a DAG task; all three carry the attributes slot of item 3. The periodic form takes a function with no input topics, an output topic list, and a period; the kernel attaches a tick -based reactor that releases the subtask every period. The intermediate form takes a function with both input and output topic lists and covers every non-source, non-sink vertex. The sink 

form takes a function whose argument tuple corresponds to its own input topics, together with a relative deadline against which the end-to-end timing of the DAG is checked. A single call to finish_create_dags(&[dag]) then finalizes the DAG: it statically verifies the assembled graph (weakly connected, acyclic, one source, one sink) and commits it to the runtime metadata store (Section 6.4). 6.4 Scheduler Interface and DAG Metadata Management 

Awkernel exposes the scheduling algorithm as a Rust trait that every scheduler implementation realizes. A scheduler implementation provides wake_task() and 

get_next_task() as required by the execution model (Section 6.2), together with a priority queue keyed on the priority that the algorithm dictates. Adding a new DAG scheduling algorithm reduces to (i) implementing the trait against an algorithm-specific run queue and (ii) registering it as a new scheduler object; the rest of the kernel (the per-CPU workers, the preemption IPI mechanism, the FasS-driven release path, and the DAG metadata store) is reused without modification. This factoring substantiates P1 (freedom to describe arbitrary DAG scheduling algorithms) and P2 (model-level algorithms directly translatable into a working implementation). The runtime DAG metadata maintained by the kernel mirrors the elements that a DAG scheduling algorithm needs at scheduling time. For each DAG, the metadata records a graph identifier, the nodes (each carrying a subtask 14 A. Yano et al. 

identifier and FasS-declared parameters), the edges (each tagged with its topic name), and the absolute deadline. For each subtask, the metadata maintains a bidirectionally referenceable link to the DAG it belongs to, so a scheduler invoked on a single subtask can in O(1) recover the deadline of the parent DAG, its sibling subtasks, and its sources and sinks. The metadata is mutable at runtime through the scheduler trait (for example, GEDF rewrites the absolute deadline of a subtask at every release), so reservation-based or deadline-rewriting algorithms are first-class. This combination of static structure (committed at 

finish_create_dags ) and mutable per-instance state (updated at scheduling time) embodies the DAG-metadata clause of P6. 

6.5 Evaluation Support: DAG Workload Loader and Metrics Collection 

RD-Gen [21] is an offline random DAG generator (not part of Awkernel) that emits YAML. Awkernel adds an in-kernel DAG workload loader that ingests this YAML and invokes the FasS register_subtask APIs, turning the synthetic workload into a runnable DAG with no manual translation. Together with the scheduler trait (Section 6.4) and the FasS API (Section 6.3), the loader lets a researcher evaluate a new DAG scheduling algorithm on real hardware with engineering effort proportional to the algorithm, not the platform. Awkernel also includes a built-in metrics feature that records, on every context switch and preemption, the execution time of the preceding task, the cumulative idle time of the CPU, the context-switch duration, and the preemption count of each subtask. Statistics are aggregated per CPU and per scheduler type and can be sampled at evaluation time. 

6.6 Extensions to More General Models 

This paper targets the single-rate DAG with AND join (Section 3), the model most commonly assumed in ROS 2-oriented timing analysis [14, 48–50, 52]; more general models (multi-rate DAGs, cause-effect chains, and time-triggered/event-driven mixtures with precedence) are widely studied. Two facts make extending this scope straightforward. First, FasS is not the only work surface on Awkernel: application code can spawn ordinary (non-DAG) async tasks via task::spawn(name, future, SchedulerType) without attaching DAG metadata. Both task kinds share the scheduler interface of Section 6.4, so any installed scheduler (Partitioned EDF, GEDF, Prioritized FIFO, or Prioritized RR) can be selected for non-DAG tasks. The wake_task of GEDF, for example, derives the absolute deadline from the relative deadline carried by SchedulerType::GEDF whenever DAG metadata is absent, so non-DAG tasks are scheduled under deadline-driven priority with no special-case logic. Second, inter-task communication is not bound to the FasS API. The pub/sub substrate that FasS uses is itself a public API: any task may directly instantiate publishers and subscribers to allocate ROS 2-style many-to-many topics with configurable queue depth, flow control, latching, and lifespan, independently of the Awkernel: Bridging DAG Scheduling Theory and Practice 15

7.2 GEDF on Awkernel vs. POSIX-Based Realizations on Linux/ROS 2 and YASMIN 

This section quantitatively compares three ways of realizing DAG-level dynamic scheduling for the same CORTS application: (a) Awkernel, where the OS itself maintains the DAG structure and natively schedules subtasks (P6 of Section 4), (b) Linux/ROS 2, where DAG-level GEDF is approximated through general-purpose Linux EDF facilities ( SCHED_DEADLINE ) combined with recent ROS 2 executor research, and (c) YASMIN, where a DAG-native middleware schedules subtasks in user space above the POSIX scheduler. As a representative DAG-based dynamic scheduling algorithm, we consider global earliest deadline first (GEDF) for DAG tasks. 

GEDF for DAG tasks. We briefly restate the GEDF scheduler for DAG tasks following Li et al. [10]. We consider a task set τ = {τ1, τ 2, . . . , τ n} of n

independent sporadic tasks, where each task τi is represented as a DAG with period Pi and relative deadline Di. Each subtask of task τi has its own WCET 

Cji . Directed edges represent precedence constraints among subtasks within a task. We denote by Jk,a the a-th job instance of task τk during system execution. Let rk,a and dk,a be the absolute release time and absolute deadline of job Jk,a ,respectively, so that dk,a − rk,a = Dk. When scheduled by a GEDF scheduler, individual subtasks carry neither release offsets nor deadlines of their own; they share the absolute deadline of the job to which they belong. At any time t, the GEDF scheduler assigns all ready subtasks to available cores in non-decreasing order of absolute deadline dk,a .

ROS 2 workload construction. To evaluate whether GEDF-like scheduling can be approximated in ROS 2, we rebuild the RD-Gen workload of Awker-nel (Section 7.1) as a ROS 2 application: source subtasks become timer call-backs , triggered at the DAG period; non-source subtasks become subscription Awkernel: Bridging DAG Scheduling Theory and Practice 17 

callbacks , triggered by incoming messages. When a subtask has multiple in-coming edges, the corresponding subscription callbacks are synchronized using 

message_filters::Synchronizer with the ExactTime policy [64]. All callbacks are hosted within a single process to avoid inter-process communication latency and the spawning of additional DDS threads. 

Candidate scheduling approaches. The SCHED_DEADLINE policy of Linux [77] implements the constant bandwidth server (CBS) algorithm combined with EDF, making it a natural choice for approximating EDF-based scheduling in ROS 2. Based on the analysis of the scheduling-model gap in Section 3, we examine three approaches for scheduling the above ROS 2 system: 1. SingleThreadedExecutor with SCHED_DEADLINE . All callbacks forming the DAG are assigned to a single SingleThreadedExecutor , and its thread is configured with SCHED_DEADLINE using period = Pi, runtime = Ci (total WCET of the DAG), and deadline = Di.2. ROS RT with SCHED_EXT EDF scheduler. ROS RT [53] proposes a

SCHED_EXT -based EDF scheduler that applies OS-level EDF scheduling to individual ROS 2 callback threads. 3. CallbackIsolatedExecutor with per-thread SCHED_DEADLINE . Each call-back’s Callback Group is isolated and assigned to a CallbackIsolatedExecu-tor [2], which maps each callback to a dedicated thread. Each thread is then configured with SCHED_DEADLINE , setting period = Pi, runtime = Cji (the individual subtask’s WCET), and deadline = Di.Approach 1 uses a single thread; consequently, all subtasks within a DAG execute sequentially, precluding any intra-DAG parallelism that GEDF would exploit. Approach 2 is conceptually promising but cannot be evaluated in practice: the implementation of the proposed SCHED_EXT EDF scheduler has not been publicly released, and its usage remains undocumented. We therefore adopt Approach 3 as the ROS 2 baseline; the implementation details of GEDF on Awkernel are presented in Section 8.1. We stress that even Approach 3 is not an equivalent implementation of DAG-level GEDF: SCHED_DEADLINE implements CBS scheduling at the thread level, and no principled mapping from DAG-level timing constraints to per-callback CBS parameters exists in the literature. This arm of the experiment is therefore designed as evidence of a semantic mismatch and its configuration burden: it quantifies what happens when DAG-level dynamic scheduling is approximated through the best available combination of general-purpose OS facilities and ROS 2 executor research. 

YASMIN baseline. Beyond approximating GEDF with SCHED_DEADLINE ,we evaluate YASMIN [58], the middleware closest to DAG-native scheduling (Section 2.2): it maintains DAG precedence through FIFO channels and imple-ments GEDF in user space above the POSIX scheduler. We build the identical workload on YASMIN: source subtasks become periodic YASMIN tasks, edges become FIFO channels, and response time is measured as in the ROS 2 workload, propagating the source’s start time along edges to the sink. However, the publicly released YASMIN implementation [78] does not realize EDF scheduling: under its EDF configuration, no code path ever assigns a task priority (the EDF com-18 A. Yano et al. 

parator is dead code), the release thread sleeps for twice the requested duration, and a semaphore leak strands ready tasks in the queue until the next release. We repaired these defects with a patch of fewer than 30 lines and made each data-driven subtask inherit its job’s absolute deadline, so that the repaired YAS-MIN schedules with exactly the GEDF semantics defined above; all modifications strengthen the baseline. Mirroring the Awkernel setup, three SCHED_FIFO worker threads run on cores 1–3 and the release thread on core 0. This arm of the experiment thus complements Approach 3: it preserves the GEDF semantics and instead isolates the architectural cost of executing them in user space above a DAG-unaware POSIX scheduler. 

Response-time comparison. Fig. 4 compares the response times of GEDF on Awkernel, Approach 3 (CallbackIsolatedExecutor with per-thread 

SCHED_DEADLINE ), and the repaired YASMIN across different total utilization levels. As the figure shows, the response times of Approach 3 inflate substantially in a large number of cases. This inflation is not a deficiency of SCHED_DEADLINE 

itself but the manifestation of the semantic mismatch stated above. First, CBS assumes that each task is released sporadically with a known minimum inter-arrival time, whereas subscription callbacks are activated by message arrivals whose spacing is perturbed by scheduling delays, queuing effects, and precedence-induced waiting; when a callback is activated earlier than the CBS mechanism expects, the server defers its deadline replenishment, inflating the observed response time. Second, the per-callback CBS parameters have no principled derivation from the DAG-level deadline, so any chosen configuration, including ours, misrepresents the DAG-level constraint to some degree. Awkernel does not exhibit this inflation because its OS scheduler operates directly on the DAG-level deadline (P6 of Section 4). The repaired YASMIN, which executes the same GEDF logic in user space, comes considerably closer to Awkernel, keeping its response-time tails bounded up to a total utilization of 2.5. Nevertheless, the median response times of YASMIN remain 11 –81% above those of Awkernel, and the gap widens toward saturation: at a total utilization of 3.0, YASMIN yields 1.8× the median response time. The residual cost is architectural: even with identical scheduling logic, YASMIN dispatches subtasks through a user-space ready queue, realizes preemption with POSIX signals, and mediates every release through a periodic scheduler thread, all above a POSIX scheduler that remains unaware of DAG precedence. The comparison thus characterizes two distinct costs of expressing DAG-level GEDF above a POSIX scheduler, relative to a design in which the OS itself maintains the DAG structure and schedules subtasks accordingly: the semantic mismatch and configuration burden of the thread-level CBS approximation, and the archi-tectural overhead of user-space dispatching even when the GEDF semantics are preserved. In particular, the Linux/ROS 2 results should be read as the price of an approximation, not as a ranking between two equivalent GEDF implementations.

# 9 Conclusion 

This paper introduced Awkernel, the first open-source RTOS with native real-time DAG scheduling support: a real-hardware testbed for theorists, and for practitioners a CORTS platform maximizing timing analyzability and reuse of the DAG scheduling literature rather than a replacement for ROS 2. We identified gaps between the DAG model and POSIX-based CORTS, derived seven design principles, and validated them on Raspberry Pi 4: microsecond-range overheads, zero-copy Arc communication, and FasS-enforced precedence by construction (RQ1 ). Baselines on Linux/ROS 2 ( SCHED_DEADLINE -based approximation) and YASMIN (repaired user-space GEDF) exposed, respectively, the mismatch and configuration burden of approximating DAG-level GEDF with thread-level CBS, and the cost of user-space execution. The GEDF case study implemented a DAG algorithm in 226 lines via the scheduler interface, showing low implementation effort for theorists ( RQ2 ). The Autoware MRM case study ported a real-world CORTS application via the FasS API, tracking the Autoware reference within 0.29 m under FasS-enforced semantics ( RQ3 ). These insights, API-enforced DAG semantics and OS-level DAG metadata management, can also be fed back into ROS 2 and AP. 

Future work. First, we will extend the FasS API beyond single-rate DAGs with AND-join to multi-rate DAGs, cause-effect chains, and mixed time-triggered/event-driven nodes with precedence (Section 6.6). Second, we will implement and compare additional DAG scheduling algorithms (federated [40], 22 A. Yano et al. 

reservation-based [28], and fluid [12]) on Awkernel for reproducible real-hardware comparisons. Third, we plan a tool that generates FasS subtask skeletons from ROS 2 node graphs.
