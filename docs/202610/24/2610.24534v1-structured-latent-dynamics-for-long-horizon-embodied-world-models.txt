## ABSTRACT 

Reliable long-horizon prediction remains a central challenge for embodied world models. Although recent generative models can synthesize visually plausible action-conditioned futures, their latent dynamics are typically learned through unconstrained transition modules, which may gradually deviate from coherent phys-ical evolution as predictions are recursively rolled forward. We investigate whether explicitly structuring latent state evolution can improve the stability and utility of such imagined trajectories. To this end, we introduce HWM , a structured dynamics framework that represents backbone latents in a learned phase space consisting of generalized coordinates and momenta. Future states are propagated through dissi-pative Hamiltonian dynamics and mapped back to the original latent representation through a learnable inverse interface. The resulting formulation can be integrated into existing world-model architectures without redesigning their perception or decoding components, while multi-step transition supervision encourages the struc-tured rollout to remain aligned with the latent trajectories induced by real observa-tions. Controlled simulation experiments show that HWM produces more accurate estimates of velocity, acceleration, and jerk and substantially reduces long-horizon energy drift relative to unstructured latent dynamics. We further evaluate the ap-proach across three distinct embodied world-model settings, covering single-arm multi-view prediction with Ctrl-World, dual-arm single-view prediction with EVAC, and task-oriented single-arm prediction with Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B. On RoboCasa, incorporating HWM into Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B yields a 2.7% relative improvement in task success. On real-world AgiBot World trajectories, it improves nDTW by 9.7% with Ctrl-World and Logic by 8.4% with EVAC. Across these settings, the results indicate that introducing explicit dynamical structure into latent evolution can improve both long-horizon predictive stability and the downstream useful-ness of embodied world-model rollouts. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

World models have long been studied as internal simulators for sequential decision making, enabling planning, counterfactual reasoning, and data-efficient policy improvement (Sutton, 1991; Ha & 1arXiv:2610.24534v1 [cs.LG] 17 Oct 2026 Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2019; 2020; 2023). Recent advances in generative video modeling have pushed this paradigm into embodied manipulation, where a model is expected to predict action-conditioned futures for robotic interaction (Hu et al., 2023; Bruce et al., 2024; Seo et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025). In this setting, however, the value of a world model is not determined only by visual realism. A predicted future must also remain physically coherent enough to support downstream policy evaluation and long-horizon execution. This requirement exposes a central limitation of many modern embodied world models. Although current architectures can generate visually plausible short-term videos, their latent transitions are often implemented as generic sequence or generative modules. Such transitions can fit local appearance statistics and short-range correlations, but they do not explicitly encode the dynamical structure that governs physical interaction. As a result, small one-step errors may compound into long-horizon physical drift: objects may penetrate, contact timing may become inconsistent, and action effects may gradually decouple from the commanded motion. For an embodied agent, these failures directly weaken the usefulness of imagined rollouts as substrates for planning and control. Existing approaches address this issue from two directions. One line of work scales generative world models and improves controllability, view consistency, and open-loop prediction quality (Hu et al., 2023; Bruce et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025). These models provide strong perceptual and rendering capabilities, but the physical regularities of manipulation are still mostly learned implicitlym, shown in Figure 1. Another line introduces physical structure into learned dynamics. For example, Symplectic ODE-Nets show that structure-preserving formulations can improve stability and extrapolation in dynamical systems (Greydanus et al., 2019; Toth et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2020). Yet the embodied video world-model setting requires a different design target: a transition mechanism that can be inserted into modern latent visual world models while preserving their existing observation, tokenization, and rendering interfaces. Our key conceptual claim is that physical consistency should not be treated as an auxiliary property imposed after a generic transition has been learned. Instead, the transition law itself should be physically structured. In other words, the central modeling object should shift from “a neural sequence model that predicts the next latent” to “a learned physical evolution rule in latent phase space.” This view is natural for embodied manipulation: a robot does not merely observe image sequences, but interacts with a dynamical system through forces, contacts, inertia, and dissipation. A world model that aims to support action should represent not only appearance evolution, but also the latent physical state whose evolution determines whether an imagined future remains executable. Motivated by this view, we propose Hamiltonian World Model ( HWM ), a physically structured latent transition framework for embodied world modeling. HWM maps backbone latents into learned generalized coordinates and momenta, which evolve under a dissipative Hamiltonian system. The Hamiltonian models the energy landscape, while damping accounts for non-conservative effects such as friction. A semi-implicit Euler update reduces numerical drift, and the predicted phase states are mapped back to backbone latents for decoding. This design structures long-horizon dynamics while preserving the backbone’s perceptual and rendering capabilities. The framework offers three conceptual advantages. First, coordinates and momenta separate configuration-like information from motion-related dynamics. Second, an energy-based transi-tion combines Hamiltonian evolution with learned dissipation. Third, multi-step latent consistency aligns the structured rollout with backbone trajectories over horizons where physical drift emerges. Together, these components form a plug-and-play transition module compatible with existing obser-vation interfaces. We evaluate HWM in simulation and three embodied world-model settings. In simulation, it improves speed, acceleration, and jerk prediction while better preserving energy relative to an unstructured transition. On Ctrl-World for single-arm multi-view manipulation and EVAC for dual-arm single-view manipulation, it improves generation and logic metrics, supporting better visual-temporal and physical consistency. On Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B for single-arm single-view task execution, it increases success rates on LIBERO and Robocasa, demonstrating benefits for both prediction quality and downstream execution. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 21. We propose a transition-centric view of world modeling: physical consistency should be built into the latent transition law, rather than added through auxiliary losses or post-hoc evaluation. 2. We introduce HWM , a plug-and-play Hamiltonian latent transition framework that maps backbone latents into phase-space variables and evolves them with dissipative Hamiltonian dynamics for improved long-horizon stability. 3. We demonstrate consistent benefits across simulation and three embodied world-model settings, including physical-state prediction, better visual and logic metrics on Ctrl-World and EVAC, and higher downstream task success on LIBERO and Robocasa with Cosmos. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Generative and embodied video world models. Recent work has scaled world models into high-capacity video generators and interactive simulators. GAIA-1 and Genie demonstrated that generative models can support controllable, action-conditioned future prediction at scale (Hu et al., 2023; Bruce et al., 2024). In robotics, manipulation-oriented models such as Multi-View Masked World Models, RoboDreamer, IRASim, Ctrl-World, and Cosmos further improved view consistency, controllability, and open-loop rollout quality for embodied interaction (Seo et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2025). Broader embodied-data and system efforts, such as AgiBot World, likewise reflect the field’s shift toward scalable, manipulation-centered world modeling (AgiBot-World-Contributors et al., 2025). Our work is complementary to this line: rather than proposing a new rendering or tokenization backbone, HWM replaces the generic latent transition with a physically structured alternative. This distinction matters because current systems can achieve strong perceptual realism while still learning dynamics mostly implicitly, which leaves room for physical drift over long rollouts. 

Physics-structured neural dynamics and world models. Another line of work introduces explicit physical structure into learned dynamics. Hamiltonian Neural Networks, Hamiltonian Generative Networks, and Symplectic ODE-Nets showed that structure-preserving parameterizations can improve stability and extrapolation in dynamical systems (Greydanus et al., 2019; Toth et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2020). More recent world-model variants, including HaM-World, PH-Dreamer, and LaWM, inject Hamiltonian, port-Hamiltonian, or least-action principles into latent transitions (Tang et al., 2026; Luan & Shi, 2026; Xiao & Ghaffari, 2026). HWM is most closely related to this family, but differs in emphasis: we aim for a drop-in transition design for embodied video world models that jointly captures conservative evolution, control, dissipation, and residual contact-rich effects, instead of focusing only on planner-oriented control benchmarks or on a single physical formalism.

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate HWM from three complementary perspectives: physical-state prediction and energy consistency in simulation, visual generation quality, and long-horizon physical consistency and downstream task success in real-world rollouts. In simulation, we test whether HWM captures underlying physical regularities beyond short-range correlations. For real-world evaluation, we consider three world-model families: COSMOS for single-arm single-view prediction (NVIDIA et al., 2025), Ctrl-World for single-arm multi-view manipulation (Guo et al., 2025), and EVAC (Jiang et al., 2025) for dual-arm single-view manipulation, using each backbone’s standard visual generation metrics. We emphasize Ctrl-World for long-horizon analysis, as multi-view observations reveal temporal and geometric inconsistencies. Across experiments, we preserve the original observation interface and other backbone components, replacing only the latent transition with HWM where applicable and comparing primarily against the corresponding vanilla backbone. 5.1 SIMULATION EXPERIMENTS

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We presented HWM , a Hamiltonian transition framework that combines controlled dissipative dynamics with a residual branch to improve long-horizon physical consistency in embodied world models. Experiments across Ctrl-World, COSMOS, and EVAC demonstrate benefits in prediction quality, physical consistency, and downstream task execution. Several limitations remain. Due to limited 9computational resources, we could not evaluate more backbones or conduct broader experiments across tasks, training scales, and random seeds. Moreover, learned phase variables need not corre-spond to identifiable physical quantities, so Hamiltonian structure alone does not guarantee physically correct predictions. Dissipation, external control, and the residual branch also limit the conservation properties of the complete dynamics, particularly during contact-rich interactions. Finally, the current evaluations do not establish robustness to substantially different environments or rollout horizons beyond those tested. Broader evaluation and stronger characterization of the learned dynamics are therefore needed.

## A RELATED WORK 

Latent world models for planning and control. World models have long served as learned simulators for sequential decision making, from Dyna and World Models to PlaNet, Dreamer, and DreamerV3 (Sutton, 1991; Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2019; 2020; 2023). These works established that compact latent dynamics can support imagination-based planning and control, but they were primarily developed for reinforcement learning settings with comparatively simple observation and interaction structure. HWM builds on this planner-facing latent-transition perspective, but targets embodied manipulation scenarios in which viewpoint changes, contact events, and action-conditioned rollouts place much stronger demands on long-horizon physical consistency. 

Embodiment-grounded evaluation of world models. As world models are increasingly used for robot learning, evaluation has moved beyond appearance metrics toward action-conditioned usefulness and executability. Recent studies argue that generated futures should support policy ranking or downstream control rather than merely look plausible (Guo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). RoboWM-Bench makes this embodiment-grounded perspective explicit by evaluating whether generated manipulation videos can be translated into successful robot actions (Jiang et al., 2026). Our evaluation protocol follows this shift: we assess HWM not only by predictive quality, but by whether its structured transitions improve long-horizon physical consistency and action usefulness across distinct embodied world-model families. 

## B SIMULATION EXPERIMENTS 

To separate transition dynamics modeling from high-dimensional rendering complexity, we first conduct controlled experiments in a simulated environment. Because the simulator provides clean action-conditioned trajectories together with accurate physical annotations, it enables a more direct test of whether the learned latent dynamics preserve the underlying physical structure. Under this setting, we adopt SimVP as the baseline and investigate the effectiveness of incorporating the proposed Hamiltonian World Model ( HWM ) . We evaluate HWM from three complementary perspectives: long-horizon prediction quality, instantaneous physical-state prediction, and energy conservation. 

Long-Horizon Prediction Quality. We first investigate whether incorporating Hamiltonian struc-ture improves the overall quality of predicted trajectories. Specifically, we train the models with prediction horizons of 10 and 30 frames, and roll them out to different test horizons to evaluate both in-distribution prediction and long-horizon extrapolation. The quantitative results are reported in Table 5. Across all combinations of training and test horizons, HWM consistently achieves lower MSE and MAE than the vanilla baseline, showing that imposing Hamiltonian structure also improves visual prediction quality. More importantly, the advantage becomes more pronounced under long-horizon rollout. For example, when trained with a 10 -frame prediction horizon and extrapolated to 60 frames, 

HWM reduces the MSE from 62 .32 to 53 .03 and the MAE from 95 .32 to 82 .58 , corresponding to rela-tive reductions of 14 .9% and 13 .4% , respectively. These results suggest that Hamiltonian modeling mitigates the accumulation of dynamics errors during rollout, leading to more stable and accurate long-term predictions. 13 Instantaneous Physical State Prediction. Beyond overall prediction quality, we further evaluate the accuracy of instantaneous physical-state prediction, including object position, speed, acceleration, and jerk. For each generated rollout, we first estimate the object center in every frame and compute velocity using finite differences between consecutive positions. Higher-order physical quantities, including acceleration and jerk, are then derived accordingly. Our evaluation includes the full 

HWM , an ablated variant without the Hamiltonian constraint, and the ground-truth trajectory. We fix the training prediction horizon to 10 frames and roll out the models to 30 and 50 frames. The results are summarized in Table 1. HWM consistently yields lower physical-state prediction errors under both rollout horizons. Notably, the improvements are substantially larger for higher-order motion quantities. Under the 30 -frame rollout, HWM reduces the acceleration RMSE from 10 .966 to 

9.235 , corresponding to a 15 .8% reduction, and decreases the jerk RMSE from 14 .135 to 11 .401 ,yielding a 19 .3% improvement. The same trend persists under the longer 50 -frame rollout, where the acceleration and jerk errors are reduced by 10 .2% and 12 .8% , respectively. These results demonstrate that Hamiltonian modeling improves the ability to capture instantaneous system dynamics and recover accurate motion states. We further visualize the predicted velocity trajectories in Fig. 3. The velocity curves produced by HWM exhibit substantially better agreement with the ground truth, whereas the non-Hamiltonian variant shows larger deviations and stronger fluctuations, further validating the effectiveness of the Hamiltonian constraint for instantaneous physical-state prediction. 

Energy Conservation. Finally, we evaluate the physical consistency of generated trajectories from the perspective of energy conservation. For each rollout, we estimate the underlying physical states from the generated video and compute the corresponding Hamiltonian(total system energy). We then compare the temporal evolution of the Hamiltonian produced by HWM , the non-Hamiltonian variant, and the ground truth. For conservative systems, such as projectile motion under uniform gravity, the total mechanical energy should remain approximately constant. Therefore, Hamiltonian drift provides a principled measure of long-horizon physical consistency. We further visualize both the Hamiltonian trajectories and the corresponding energy drift in Fig. 4. As shown in the figure, the Hamiltonian predicted by HWM remains significantly closer to the ground-truth energy throughout the rollout, whereas the non-Hamiltonian variant exhibits substantially larger drift and oscillations. These results demonstrate that the proposed Hamiltonian formulation effectively preserves global physical invariants, leading to long-horizon dynamics that are more faithful to the underlying physical system.

LIBERO. We follow the standard LIBERO simulation benchmark protocol (Liu et al., 2023). LIBERO consists of a single Franka Emika Panda robot arm interacting with a variety of objects and environments, and provides four primary task suites: LIBERO-Spatial, LIBERO-Object, LIBERO-Goal, and LIBERO-Long. These suites evaluate different aspects of manipulation generalization, including spatial layout variation, object variation, language-specified goals, and long-horizon task composition. Each suite contains 10 tasks with 50 demonstrations per task, resulting in 500 demonstrations per task suite. For our experiments, Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B is used as the base world model, and we compare the original transition model against HWM while keeping the remaining policy and planning components fixed. Following the standard data processing protocol (Kim et al., 2026), unsuccessful demonstrations are filtered from the policy-training data, while the complete unfiltered demonstration set is retained for world-model and value-function training. We report task success rate across the four LIBERO suites, with particular emphasis on LIBERO-Long, where accumulated transition errors over extended action sequences provide a more demanding test of the learned dynamics. The results show that incorporating Hamiltonian latent dynamics consistently improves task execution success over the original Cosmos transition. The gains are especially pronounced on long-horizon tasks, where small one-step prediction errors can otherwise compound into increasingly inaccurate imagined trajectories. By enforcing a structured evolution in latent phase space, HWM produces more stable intermediate states and more reliable action-conditioned predictions, allowing the downstream policy to maintain a coherent sequence of actions for a longer duration. These results indicate that improvements in latent transition quality translate directly into better downstream manipulation performance rather than merely improving reconstruction fidelity. 

Robocasa. We further evaluate HWM on the Robocasa simulation benchmark (Nasiriany et al., 2024), which contains 24 static kitchen manipulation tasks executed by a single Franka Emika Panda robot arm. Following the evaluation protocol of prior works (Kim et al., 2026), we evaluate each task over 50 trials distributed across five evaluation scenes with different floor plans and visual styles, with 10 trials per scene. The final success rate is averaged across all 24 tasks and three random seeds, corresponding to 3600 evaluation trials in total. 16 Unlike LIBERO, the Robocasa evaluation protocol emphasizes generalization to unseen object instances, and two of the five evaluation scenes for each task contain styles that are not observed during training. This setting therefore provides a stronger test of whether the learned world model captures transferable interaction dynamics rather than memorizing appearance-specific correlations. To isolate the data efficiency of the proposed dynamics model, we train on the 50 human-teleoperated demonstrations available for each task and do not use the additional MimicGen demonstrations. We then evaluate the task execution success rate under the standard Robocasa protocol. Compared with the original Cosmos transition model, HWM achieves higher average task success across the 24 Robocasa tasks. The improvement is particularly meaningful in scenes containing unseen styles and object instances, where purely appearance-driven prediction can be insufficient to maintain consistent object interactions over long action sequences. The structured Hamiltonian transition reduces accumulated latent drift and produces more coherent action-conditioned futures, which in turn improves the reliability of downstream planning and policy execution. Overall, the LIBERO and Robocasa results demonstrate that the benefit of HWM extends beyond improved latent or visual prediction metrics. When integrated into Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B, the proposed Hamiltonian transition consistently improves downstream task execution success, with the largest gains appearing in settings that require long-horizon reasoning, sustained object interaction, and generalization beyond the visual configurations encountered during training.
