# Data Center Load Accommodation under QoS and Checkpoint-Induced Frequency Security Constraints 

Ruolan Cui, Student Member, IEEE, Ziang Liu, Student Member, IEEE, Chenxi Hu, Member, IEEE, 

Francis Yunhe Hou, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —The rapid growth of artificial intelligence workloads is accelerating the expansion and grid interconnection of data centers (DCs). Existing data center load accommodation (DCLA) assessments primarily focus on grid and resource adequacy and often imply that greater workload flexibility always increases accommodable capacity. However, workload flexibility has a dual effect. Workload deferral and temporal shifting can alleviate net-work congestion, whereas synchronized checkpointing of training workloads can cause large and rapid load reductions, threatening frequency stability. Therefore, DCLA cannot be determined solely by grid adequacy. This paper develops an optimal DCLA method that jointly accounts for workload quality of service (QoS), checkpoint-induced frequency security (FS), and grid adequacy. First, a QoS-aware model characterizes workload-dependent flexibility while ensuring service feasibility. Second, checkpoint-induced FS constraints are developed for periodic and event-triggered power variations, with key frequency-security limits reformulated in algebraic form. Third, a two-stage lexicographic model is developed to maximize DCLA and minimizes the required energy storage (ES) deployment, and then optimizes system operation under the given DC and ES siting and sizing decisions. Case studies show that the proposed method avoids a 40.3% overestimation of DCLA compared with the grid-adequacy benchmark. QoS is the primary limiting factor, while checkpoint-induced frequency-security constraints become more restrictive as training workloads increase. These findings highlight that reliable DCLA should account for both its grid-support value and the associated QoS and FS risks. 

Index Terms —Data center, load accommodation, quality of service, frequency security, checkpoint

Large-scale DC intercon-nection places increasing pressure on transmission corridors, substation capacity, resource adequacy, and interconnection processes [3], while grid expansion often lags behind load growth because of siting, permitting, investment approval, and long construction lead times [4]. Unlike conventional inflexible loads, DC demand is computing-driven and exhibits workload-dependent temporal flexibility. Inference, fine-tuning, and training workloads have different degrees of deferrability and interruptibility, allowing some workloads to be shifted or temporarily suspended to relieve network congestion [5], [6]. However, such flexibility is bounded by quality-of-service (QoS) requirements, including service continuity and workload completion. Moreover, long-duration training workloads rely on checkpointing to save training states for recovery, and synchronized checkpoint op-erations across large clusters can cause rapid and substantial load releases that threaten frequency security (FS) [2], [7]. First, existing load-accommodation studies consider net-work constraints and operational uncertainty [8], as well as dynamic operating limits [9]. Flexible grid resources, such as energy storage (ES) and soft open points, have also been used to improve load accommodation capability [10]. These studies identify grid bottlenecks and quantify grid-side accommoda-tion limits. However, they generally model additional loads as inflexible demand or exogenously specified flexibility, and therefore do not capture the service feasibility or checkpoint-induced frequency impacts of flexible DC workloads. Second, DC flexibility studies have developed workload-to-power models and demand-response formulations [5], [11]. Recent studies further couple computing workloads with power-system regulation and operation [12], [13], while market-oriented frameworks optimize flexible DC operation under electricity-market incentives [14]. These studies demon-strate the operational value of workload flexibility, but most assume predetermined DC capacity rather than determining the maximum accommodable capacity. Moreover, representing flexibility using a fixed shiftable fraction or only a daily energy constraint may allow excessive workload reshaping or implicit curtailment, thereby obscuring the effect of QoS requirements on DCLA. Third, frequency-security studies have highlighted the risks posed by fast variations in emerging large loads [1], [4]. Recent measurements further show that AI training workloads can exhibit synchronized power variations and checkpoint-related transients [2], [7]. This section quantifies service feasibility for flexible DC workloads. Workloads are first classified according to their service flexibility, and the corresponding QoS constraints are then formulated for the DCLA problem. 

A. Workload Flexibility Classification 

From the grid perspective, a DC is a large nodal load subject to connection capacity and network security limits. However, its accommodable capacity depends not only on grid adequacy, but also on whether its computing demand can be deferred or interrupted. Workload flexibility must therefore be explicitly represented in the DCLA formulation. We adopt a four-level workload classification based on latency sensitivity, deferrability, and interruptibility [3], [6]. Flex-0 represents latency-critical and non-interruptible ser-vices, such as real-time inference and online search. These workloads require immediate execution and have negligi-ble temporal elasticity, and are therefore modeled as rigid loads [16]. Flex-1 to Flex-3 exhibit progressively greater deferrability and interruptibility. Flex-1 represents batch infer-ence that can be deferred over a short time window. Flex-2 represents fine-tuning workloads that can be temporarily suspended, whereas Flex-3 represents long-duration training workloads with the greatest temporal shifting capability [17]. The resulting flexibility hierarchy is illustrated in Fig. 2. To embed DC service requirements into system-level DCLA optimization, this qualitative hierarchy is translated into class-dependent rolling-window service envelopes that provide an aggregate representation of fine-grained SLA characteristics and bound short-term deviations from the reference execution 3

profile. As summarized in Table I, the rolling-window lengths and allowable service shortfalls are parameterized based on the workload-flexibility characteristics and demonstrated service levels reported in [3], [6], with more flexible workloads allowing a larger temporary service shortfall over a longer time window. The shifted workload volume is preserved through the daily workload completion constraint.

III. C HECKPOINT -I NDUCED FREQUENCY SECURITY 

BOUNDARY 

This section establishes a checkpoint-induced FS bound-ary for DCLA optimization. The checkpoint-induced power release is first quantified, and the RoCoF, frequency-peak, and QSS limits are then derived to constrain the resulting overfrequency risk. 

A. Checkpoint-Induced Power Release 

Checkpointing introduces synchronized transient phases in large-scale training and fine-tuning workloads. When many graphics processing units (GPUs) or training jobs enter the checkpoint phase simultaneously, the DC load may change rapidly, producing short-term power ramps and frequency security concerns [1], [7]. As illustrated in Fig. 3, synchronized checkpoint operations can cause repeated and rapid reductions in GPU power. This paper considers two checkpointing modes. Periodic checkpointing saves training states at predefined intervals to support fault tolerance and recovery after unexpected interruptions. Event-triggered checkpointing saves states in response to operational actions, such as task suspension, power curtailment, or GPU reallocation, so that the workload can later resume from the saved state. The recovery process after task reloading may cause a reverse load ramp, which can be mitigated by smooth recovery or snapback prevention [6]. This reverse ramp is not treated as the main constraint in this paper.

D. Sensitivity Analysis on Workload Characteristics 

Table V also compares the inference-heavy, balanced, and training-heavy mixes under identical system settings. 

1) QoS-Limited DCLA Across Workload Mixes: Without FS constraints, DCLA increases with the training-workload share. Greater deferrability improves service-feasible accom-modation because training workloads can be shifted over longer windows and can better absorb network and resource constraints. Inference-dominated systems remain more con-strained because a larger share of their demand is rigid or limited to short deferral windows. 

2) Interaction Between Workload Mix and FS: The com-plete formulation changes this ranking. Training-heavy work-loads provide the greatest temporal flexibility, but they also create the largest checkpoint-induced disturbance. As a result, the frequency-security penalty grows faster than the accommo-dation benefit from deferrability. Inference-heavy workloads cause weaker checkpoint disturbances, but their rigid service requirements limit the amount of usable flexibility. Under the studied settings, the balanced mix achieves the highest QoS- and FS-aware DCLA because it best balances service flexibility against frequency security risk. 

E. Complementary Effects of Workload Flexibility and Instal-lable ES 

To evaluate the individual contributions of these two strate-gies, we conducted an ablation study by removing workload flexibility and installable ES independently while retaining all other settings. 

1) Contribution of Workload Flexibility: When both work-load flexibility and ES are disabled, the DCLA is limited to 330 MW. Introducing installable ES under rigid workloads yields no improvement, as the DCLA remains at 330 MW (with zero ES units being deployed). In that case, the active limitation comes from service inflexibility rather than from transferable grid or energy margins. Workload flexibility is therefore the primary enabler of additional accommodation, increasing the DCLA to 400 MW even without the use of 10 

ES. By allowing throughput to move within service windows, flexible scheduling creates feasible operating space that cannot be obtained by storage alone. 

2) Additional Contribution of Installable ES: With flexible scheduling enabled, installable ES provides an additional ac-commodation gain, bringing the maximum DCLA to 430 MW through the deployment of 18 ES units. In the studied system, storage complements workload flexibility rather than replacing it. Flexible scheduling first creates temporal accommodation headroom, after which ES exploits the remaining system-wide network and intertemporal margins. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This paper developed an optimization-based DCLA method that jointly accounts for grid adequacy, workload QoS, and checkpoint-induced FS. A QoS-aware workload model was developed to characterize heterogeneous workload flexibility while ensuring service continuity and workload completion. Checkpoint-induced power releases were linked to accom-modated training workloads, and the corresponding RoCoF, frequency-peak, and QSS limits were reformulated as al-gebraic constraints. These components were then integrated into a two-stage lexicographic formulation, in which Stage I maximizes DCLA and minimizes the required ES deployment, while Stage II optimizes system operation with the DC and ES siting and sizing decisions fixed. Case studies on the IEEE 39-bus system show that conven-tional grid-adequacy-based DCLA methods overestimate QoS-and FS-aware DCLA by 40.3% for the balanced workload mix. QoS accounts for the largest capacity reduction by limiting excessive workload reshaping and preventing implicit work-load curtailment, whereas checkpoint-induced FS constraints become binding for training-heavy workloads. In the baseline case, the QSS limit is binding and the frequency-peak limit is nearly active, while RoCoF retains substantial margin. The results reveal the dual effect of workload flexibility on DCLA. Greater deferrability can alleviate network and in-tertemporal constraints, but the associated checkpoint-induced power releases may offset these benefits and make frequency security the binding constraint. Therefore, greater workload flexibility does not necessarily increase secure DCLA. Reliable DC interconnection decisions should jointly account for the grid-support benefits of workload flexibility, its QoS limita-tions, and the associated frequency-security risks. Future work will incorporate more detailed workload and checkpoint dy-namics, operational uncertainty, and higher-fidelity frequency-response models.
