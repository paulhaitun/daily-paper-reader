# Mobile Embodied Agent Network (MEAN): Integrated Framework, Enabling Technologies, and Task-Driven Deployment 

Zhaoyang Zhang, Lingxiao Sun, Weijie Zhou, Chunhui Feng, Zhaohui Yang, Xuan Cheng, Tianyi Qin, Zhixian Kong, Baoping Cheng, Zijian Wu, and Jingze Che 

Abstract —Mobile networks are evolving from human-centered, data-oriented communication infrastructures toward machine-and/or agent-dominant, task-oriented platforms that are to serve the rapidly increasing networked machines and embodied agents. In order to fulfill the newly arose complex task objectives, the framework of future mobile network is to be innovated to reflect and facilitate the intrinsic interplay and integration of the under-lying processes of perception, communication, computation, deci-sion, action, and feedback, etc. This survey establishes the mobile embodied agent network (MEAN) as a task-oriented networking paradigm in which networking and distributed intelligence are jointly organized around closed-loop embodied task execution. We first establish a unified MEAN framework that explains how network resources and intelligent functions jointly support embodied task execution, and extend its performance indicators from conventional communication metrics to embodied task outcomes and cross-layer system effects. With this framework, we analyze three complementary enabling mechanisms that form a cognition-interaction-adaption loop. Large artificial intelligence (AI) models provide high-level cognition, token communication connects this cognition with distributed agents through shared semantic representations, and reinforcement learning closes the loop by adapting system operation to network, task, and physical feedback. We further investigate the bidirectional interplay between MEAN and emerging technologies, showing how they mutually extend system capabilities and reshape task-oriented network design. Finally, we discuss open challenges and future research directions toward the practical realization of MEAN over real joint communication and computation platform. 

Index Terms —Mobile embodied agent network, token communi-cation, wireless large AI model, multi-agent collaboration, edge intelligence, closed-loop control.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The evolution of wireless networks has long been driven by demands for higher data rates, broader connectivity, and more reliable low-latency services. Fifth-generation (5G) and 5G-A systems have advanced these capabilities to support enhanced mobile broadband, massive machine-type communications, and ultra-reliable low-latency communications [1]. However, the emerging vision of sixth-generation (6G) networks indi-cates a more fundamental shift: they are expected to move beyond connecting people, devices, and data flows toward supporting connected intelligence in dynamic physical envi-ronments [2]–[4]. In such a vision, wireless networks will not merely deliver information from one endpoint to another, but will increasingly support integrated sensing, computation, and intelligence to enable closed-loop perception, decision, coordi-nation, and action in applications such as autonomous vehicles, uncrewed aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms, smart manufacturing, and human-robot collaboration [5]–[8]. Unlike passive termi-nals, the autonomous and mobile agents in these applications continuously interact with the physical environment, making their task execution directly dependent on timely and reliable information exchange, intelligent decision, and environmental feedback. Embodied artificial intelligence serves as the key driving force in this transition. Unlike conventional AI systems that mainly operate in digital spaces with static datasets or one-shot inference pipelines, embodied agents perceive the physical world through sensors, reason over environmental states and task objectives, and execute actions through physical bodies or actuators [9]–[12]. Recent advances in embodied multimodal models, vision-language-action models, and robotic founda-tion models have further enhanced their capabilities for se-mantic understanding, task planning, and adaptive physical in-teraction [13]–[15]. Nevertheless, future embodied intelligence cannot rely solely on isolated single-agent systems. In many practical scenarios, embodied agents are spatially distributed, resource-constrained, and required to cooperate under chang-ing network and physical conditions. Their task performance is therefore determined not only by the intelligence of individual agents, but also by the quality of networked collaboration among agents, embodied platforms, edge infrastructures, and cloud intelligence. Advances in integrated wireless networking and edge intelli-gence provide connectivity and distributed computing support for embodied agents. Embodied AI complements these capa-bilities with physical perception and task reasoning. However, these capabilities still need to be integrated within a uni-fied, task-oriented networking framework. A. Motivation 

Existing wireless-AI research provides complementary foundations for intelligent mobile systems. Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) coordinates environmental sens-ing with information transmission, while integrated sensing, communication, and computation (ISCC) extends this joint design to information processing [16], [17]. Mobile edge computing and edge intelligence bring computing resources and AI inference closer to mobile agents [18]–[20]. At the network level, AI-radio access network (AI-RAN) introduces AI-driven optimization, while open-radio access network (O-RAN) provides programmable interfaces for network control [21]. Together, these capabilities allow agents to draw on networked resources for perception and reasoning beyond what individual devices can support. However, these capabilities alone do not ensure coherent support for closed-loop embodied tasks. Network decisions determine whether agents receive the information and com-puting support needed to act in time, while their actions change the physical environment and subsequent network demands. Supporting embodied intelligence therefore requires network operation and physical task execution to adapt to each other through continuous feedback. As summarized in Fig. 2, three gaps separate existing wireless-AI designs from this re-quirement, concerning the organizing paradigm, optimization objectives, and cross-layer integration. 

Paradigm gap. Existing wireless systems are largely or-ganized around delivering communication and computing ser-vices, with service completion often treated as the endpoint of network operation. For embodied agents, however, delivering data or an inference result is only an intermediate step toward completing a physical task. Its value depends on whether it enables the agent to take an appropriate action at the required time. Delayed information, for example, may describe a state that has already changed and lead to an unsuitable action. The resulting physical outcome must therefore inform subsequent network decisions. Objective gap. Conventional evaluation emphasizes subsystem-level metrics such as communication latency, inference accuracy, and energy consumption, whereas embodied task success depends on how these factors jointly affect physical execution. Optimizing these metrics independently may overlook their interactions, so improvements in individual metrics do not necessarily lead to more timely, reliable, or successful task completion. For example, a more accurate model may require additional processing time, making its output less useful for an action that must be taken promptly. MEAN therefore requires an evaluation framework that relates basic system performance to embodied task outcomes and captures cross-layer intelligence and resource tradeoffs. Representative measures include task success rate, mission completion time, and safe success rate for task outcomes, together with planning validity and taskresource efficiency for cross-layer evaluation [22]–[24]. 

Integration gap. The technical foundations required by MEAN remain fragmented across system layers. Communica-tion, sensing, computation, intelligence, and control are often designed with different interfaces, timescales, and optimiza-tion objectives, although decisions in one layer can directly affect the operating conditions of others. Agent mobility and environmental changes alter communication and sensing conditions, while resource allocation and computation place-ment affect inference latency, coordination, and subsequent physical actions. Addressing these dependencies requires a shared representation of task state and cross-layer coordination mechanisms that align resource decisions with the needs of physical execution. Addressing these gaps requires complementary capabilities for cognition, interaction, and adaptation. Large AI models provide the cognitive foundation by interpreting multimodal observations and task instructions to support reasoning and planning [25], [26]. Realizing these capabilities across dis-tributed agents requires task-relevant information to be ex-changed and aligned. Token communication supports this interaction through shared semantic representations, enabling agents to exchange context and coordinate their contributions to a common task [27]–[29]. The resulting plans must also adapt to changing physical environments. RL and multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) support this adaptation by using network states and execution feedback to adjust task orchestration [30]–[34]. Together, these technologies form a cognition-interaction-adaptation loop. Model-based reasoning guides what agents need to communicate and accomplish, token exchanges pro-vide shared context for collaborative execution, and feedback-driven adaptation adjusts how network resources support reasoning and communication. These adjustments shape the information and resources available for subsequent reasoning and interaction. MEAN provides a unified networking frame-work for integrating this loop into distributed embodied task execution. The following subsection formalizes MEAN and clarifies its core capabilities and positioning. 

B. Definition of MEAN 

In this survey, MEAN refers to a task-oriented wireless net-working paradigm in which mobile embodied agents, wireless infrastructures, cloud-edge-device intelligence, and physical environments are jointly organized to support networked em-bodied task execution. Unlike conventional wireless systems centered on data delivery or isolated intelligent services, MEAN coordinates networking and distributed intelligence according to task requirements and physical-world feedback. MEAN is characterized by four key features:

Physical closed-loop operation. Network operation is coupled with perception, decision, action, and physical feedback during task execution.

C. Contributions

We develop a system architecture and evaluation frame-work for MEAN. The architecture organizes mobile em-bodied agents, wireless networks, edge–cloud resources, closed-loop functional capabilities, and cross-layer intel-ligence within a common system view. The evaluation framework further categorizes performance measures into basic system, embodied task, and cross-layer intelligence and tradeoff indicators.  

> •

We provide a systematic review of three enabling tech-nologies for MEAN, including reinforcement learning for feedback-driven adaptation, token communication for distributed semantic interaction, and large AI models for cognition and task-driven deployment. A. Framework of MEAN 

As shown in Fig. 4, MEAN can be organized into three tightly coupled layers: the mobile embodied agent and wire-less network foundation, the closed-loop functional capabil-ity pipeline, and the high-level cross-layer intelligence and coordination layer. These layers are connected through atoken-oriented semantic interface and an RL-based adaptive loop, enabling task-oriented closed-loop intelligence across agents, wireless networks, computing resources, and intelligent coordination modules. The mobile embodied agent and wireless network foun-dation provides the physical and infrastructural substrate of MEAN. It consists of mobile embodied agents, mobile wire-less networks, and edge–cloud computing resources. Mobile agents, such as UAVs, robot dogs, autonomous vehicles, and mobile robots, integrate sensors, actuators, on-device comput-ing, and mobility to perceive and interact with the physical environment [9], [11], [12], [36]. Meanwhile, 5G/6G wireless networks connect mobile agents with BSs, RSUs, APs, and edge–cloud infrastructure, while facing mobility, handover, time-varying channels, interference, spectrum constraints, and latency and reliability requirements. Edge servers and cloud data centers further provide heterogeneous computation and storage resources [18], [19], [37], [38]. This layer therefore jointly supports physical interaction, wireless connectivity, and distributed computation. The closed-loop functional pipeline transforms task intel-ligence into concrete embodied behavior through five core capabilities: perception, decision, coordination, control, and deployment and validation. Through continuous interac-tion with the environment, RL dynamically adjusts commu-nication, computation, coordination, and control policies ac-cording to task requirements and network conditions, thereby connecting system-level resource adaptation with embodied task execution.

C. Key Design Challenges 

Realizing MEAN requires addressing three fundamental system-level challenges arising from dynamic environments, constrained wireless communication, and limited computa-tional resources. 

1) Task and Resource Coordination in Dynamic Environ-ments: Mobile agents, wireless conditions, and computing resources evolve dynamically, making it challenging to co-ordinate task execution and resource allocation across het-erogeneous components. MEAN requires adaptive cross-layer mechanisms to jointly optimize task decisions, communica-tion, computation, and control while maintaining closed-loop stability. Section III explores RL-based adaptation to address this challenge [51], [52]. 

2) Information Effectiveness and Consistency under Con-strained Communication: Limited bandwidth, variable latency, and unreliable wireless links constrain information exchange among agents and distributed intelligence modules. MEAN must preserve task-relevant information and semantic consis-tency while minimizing communication overhead, particularly 8

under compression and transmission errors. Section IV inves-tigates token-oriented communication to support efficient and reliable semantic interaction [41], [42]. 

3) Real-Time and Reliable Intelligence under Resource Con-straints: Deploying advanced AI models on mobile and edge platforms is constrained by limited computation, memory, and energy resources. MEAN must balance reasoning and planning capabilities with inference latency, resource efficiency, and execution reliability, while mitigating uncertain or unsafe model outputs. A. Motivation and RL Formulation 

MEAN integrates mobile embodied agents, wireless net-works, and distributed computing infrastructures to support closed-loop perception, reasoning, communication, and phys-ical interaction. Unlike conventional mobile networks that primarily transport data between endpoints, MEAN directly supports task execution in dynamic physical environments. Mobile robots, UAVs, autonomous vehicles, and other embod-ied agents continuously perceive their surroundings, exchange task-relevant information, invoke local or edge intelligence, coordinate with other agents, and adjust their physical actions according to environmental feedback. Consequently, the ef-fectiveness of MEAN depends not only on communication efficiency, but also on how communication, computing, intel-ligence, and agent capabilities are dynamically orchestrated according to evolving task requirements. Such orchestration is particularly challenging because the operating conditions of MEAN vary across both the physical and cyber domains. Agent mobility changes network topology and channel quality; sensing uncertainty alters the value of newly collected observations; task phases introduce different latency, reliability, and computation requirements; and lim-ited spectrum, energy, and computing resources constrain the set of feasible decisions. Moreover, these factors are tightly coupled. For example, transmitting high-resolution sensory information may improve perception quality but consume substantial wireless resources, whereas feature compression can reduce communication overhead at the cost of information loss. Offloading an inference task to an edge server may improve computation capability but introduce transmission and queueing delays. Similarly, prioritizing one safety-critical agent may temporarily reduce the resources available to other agents while improving overall mission reliability. Therefore, MEAN requires an adaptive mechanism that can continuously balance task utility against communication, computation, en-ergy, and safety costs. This problem is inherently sequential. A decision made at the current time may affect not only instantaneous network performance but also future task progress and system states [53], [54]. Allocating additional bandwidth to a perception stream may improve subsequent localization and planning; postponing a non-critical model update may save resources in the current stage but reduce model freshness later; selecting edge-assisted inference may improve decision quality while changing future communication load; and modifying the co-operation pattern among agents may reshape both informa-tion availability and resource competition. These delayed and coupled effects make static rules and one-shot optimization insufficient for highly dynamic MEAN deployments. Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a natural framework for addressing such task-driven adaptation and orchestration. By continuously interacting with the environment, an RL policy can learn how current decisions influence long-term task outcomes under uncertain and time-varying conditions. Rather than optimizing a single instantaneous metric, such as throughput, latency, or inference accuracy, RL can jointly ac-count for task completion, information value, communication efficiency, computation cost, energy consumption, reliability, and physical safety [30], [31]. More importantly, RL allows orchestration policies to adapt according to task phase, agent state, mobility, network conditions, and accumulated execution feedback, making it well suited for the closed-loop operation of MEAN. To provide a unified view, Fig. 5 illustrates an RL-enabled adaptation and orchestration framework for MEAN. At each decision epoch, the MEAN environment produces a multidi-mensional system state reflecting ongoing embodied tasks and available system resources. The task and embodied-agent state may include task progress, task priority, agent role, mobility, sensing quality, inference confidence, model freshness, and safety relevance. The network state characterizes channel qual-ity, bandwidth availability, interference, queue status, latency, reliability, and network topology. The computing and resource state further captures processor utilization, edge-server avail-ability, memory, energy budget, and other resource constraints. By jointly observing these states, the RL decision engine learns an orchestration policy that dynamically maps system conditions to task- and resource-level actions. The resulting RL actions span several tightly coupled control dimensions. First, task orchestration determines how embodied tasks are decomposed, prioritized, assigned, and executed locally, at edge or cloud nodes, or collaboratively across multiple agents. Second, task-aware information ex-change determines what information should be transmitted, when communication should occur, and at what represen-tation or compression level. Third, network resource man-agement adapts spectrum, bandwidth, power, access oppor-tunities, scheduling priorities, routing, and reliability levels according to task requirements rather than link-level metrics alone. Finally, multi-agent adaptation and coordination enables distributed embodied agents to coordinate their task roles, communication behaviors, and resource usage under heteroge-neous and time-varying conditions. These control dimensions are coupled through the common objective of maximizing task-level utility under limited system resources. Formally, at each decision epoch t, the system observes a state st that captures task progress, agent conditions, network states, computing availability, and resource budgets. C. RL for Information Exchange 

Information exchange connects distributed embodied task execution with wireless communication in MEAN. Embodied agents may share raw observations, learned features, model updates, inference results, and coordination-related informa-tion. However, the usefulness of these transmissions is highly context-dependent: information that is valuable at one stage of a task may become outdated at another. Its value may also depend on what the receiving agent already knows and what information is needed for its current decision. Since different information types vary in freshness, task relevance, and communication cost, transmitting all available information is often inefficient. RL-based information exchange therefore focuses on deciding what information to exchange, when to transmit it, and how it should be represented or compressed according to task and system states [65]. Unlike conventional traffic scheduling, the value of a trans-mission in MEAN depends on its contribution to task execu-tion rather than data volume alone. For example, a compact but urgent control message may be more valuable than a large sensory stream, while intermediate features may replace raw observations when comparable inference performance can be maintained with lower communication cost [66], [67]. This makes information selection closely related to the downstream use of the transmitted content, rather than only to its size or channel requirement. Two representative information-exchange patterns are par-ticularly relevant. In model-distribution architectures, includ-ing split learning, collaborative inference, and model parti-tioning, the policy can adapt where computation is divided and what intermediate information is exchanged according to communication and computing conditions [68]–[70]. In distributed learning, such as federated and edge learning, RL can select participating agents and useful local updates based on their expected contribution and the resources required for communication [71], [72]. This is especially relevant in MEAN, where observations collected by embodied agents are often heterogeneous and non-IID. As a result, communication decisions should account not only for whether information is available, but also for whether it provides new or complemen-tary knowledge to the receiver. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the RL policy can jointly observe the progress of the task or learning process and the current com-munication and resource conditions. Based on the observed state, the policy decides whether communication is necessary and, if so, how it should be carried out, such as by adjusting update timing, compression, participation, peer selection, or model placement. The reward reflects the resulting task utility relative to the communication and execution cost. Feedback from downstream learning or inference can further indicate whether the transmitted information was useful, providing a basis for subsequent exchange decisions. Different RL formulations can be adopted according to the information-exchange setting. Adaptive update scheduling is a useful application of RL in distributed learning. Rather than transmitting model updates at fixed intervals, the policy can evaluate their current usefulness and decide whether communication is necessary or should be deferred [73]. D. RL for Wireless Resource Management 

Wireless resource management in MEAN extends conven-tional radio optimization by incorporating task-level infor-mation into resource-control decisions. Rather than relying only on channel and traffic conditions, the controller also considers how communication affects task progress and the timeliness of learning or inference. The same amount of wireless resource may therefore have very different task value depending on which information is being served and when it is needed. Radio resources can thus be adapted according to their contribution to task execution instead of link-level performance alone. This distinction becomes especially important when multi-ple embodied agents compete for limited wireless resources. A scheduler may need to balance the communication needs of agents that differ not only in channel quality, but also in their current role in the task. Consequently, resource allocation is no longer a simple matter of serving the user with the best instantaneous link condition. The controller must instead determine how limited radio resources should be shared so that communication supports the overall task process without unnecessarily degrading network efficiency. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the RL controller jointly observes the current radio environment and task-related learning state. Based on these observations, it determines how communi-cation resources should be allocated and configured across users and links. Such decisions affect not only transmission performance but also whether task-relevant information can reach the required agent or computing node in time. The reward therefore reflects the resulting task benefit together with communication efficiency and operational cost, while practical requirements on reliability, latency, interference, and resource availability can be incorporated as constraints. A key issue is how to relate radio-level outcomes to task-level performance. The effect of a resource decision may not be immediately vis-ible at the communication layer, but may appear later through improved inference, fresher task information, or more timely control feedback. Designing suitable intermediate feedback is therefore important for allowing the RL controller to identify which radio decisions actually contribute to task progress. In practice, these decisions may involve several radio-control variables that interact with one another. For example, changing the scheduling priority of a task flow may alter the bandwidth available to other users, while power or MCS adjustment can affect both transmission reliability and interfer-ence. RL is therefore useful for learning coordinated control policies over such coupled decisions instead of tuning each parameter independently. The control timescale can also vary: fast radio adaptation may respond to channel fluctuations, 13 

whereas task-aware priorities may change more slowly as the embodied task progresses. Different RL methods can be selected according to the control space. Value-based methods such as DQN and DDQN are suitable for discrete decisions including scheduling, access, channel, or MCS selection [75], [76], whereas actor–critic methods such as DDPG and SAC are more appropriate for continuous variables such as power and bandwidth alloca-tion [77]. Constrained or safe RL can be used when strict latency, reliability, or safety requirements must be satisfied, while MARL supports distributed resource allocation among multiple embodied agents [78]. In practice, the choice of RL method depends not only on whether the control variable is discrete or continuous, but also on whether resource decisions must be coordinated across multiple users and task flows. The deployment architecture also influences how resource-control policies are implemented. A centralized controller can exploit broader network and task information, while distributed policies may be more suitable when embodied agents must react locally or when global state exchange is costly. This trade-off makes MARL particularly relevant to large-scale MEAN deployments. MARL-based joint adaptive modulation and coding (AMC) and resource allocation pro-vides a useful example of how distributed learning can support wireless control, where users learn resource-allocation policies from local observations and system feedback [79]. While the original formulation mainly targets throughput and fairness, its objective can be extended in MEAN to account for the contribution of radio-resource decisions to task execution. In this case, users carrying more time-sensitive or task-critical information can be treated differently even under similar radio conditions, providing a natural transition from link-centric to task-aware control. Overall, RL-based wireless resource management provides the network-level control layer of MEAN, allowing conven-tional radio resources to be dynamically allocated according to both communication conditions and task requirements. 

E. Advanced RL for Dynamic and Distributed MEAN 

Beyond task orchestration, information exchange, and wire-less resource management, practical MEAN systems must also cope with distributed decision-making, changing operating conditions, and safety-critical execution. These challenges motivate more advanced RL mechanisms that extend the basic orchestration framework. A first challenge arises from the distributed nature of MEAN. Multiple embodied agents often make decisions based on local observations while sharing communication and com-puting resources. MARL is therefore particularly relevant for coordinating their task execution, information exchange, and resource use [80]. Centralized training with decentralized exe-cution (CTDE) offers a practical implementation paradigm, al-though communication overhead, non-stationarity, and credit-assignment issues may still limit scalability. Distributed coordination alone is insufficient when the op-erating environment changes over time. Variations in tasks, mobility, channel conditions, or resource availability can re-duce the effectiveness of policies trained under fixed settings. Meta-RL can improve adaptation to new conditions with limited interaction data [81], while digital twins provide virtual environments for policy training, evaluation, and refinement before or during deployment [82]. Together, they can reduce the cost and risk associated with online adaptation. As policies adapt to changing conditions, safety becomes another critical requirement. Safe and constrained RL is im-portant when decisions must satisfy strict requirements on reliability, latency, energy, or physical safety. It helps the policy avoid unsafe actions and keep system operation within acceptable limits. This is especially important when RL is used for physical control or other safety-critical tasks. Beyond coordination, adaptation, and safety, MEAN must also determine which information is worth communicating. Semantic-aware RL introduces task relevance and informa-tion value into communication decisions, allowing agents to adapt what information is exchanged and at what level of representation. This provides a natural bridge from RL-based communication control to the token-oriented communication framework introduced in the next section. Together, these advanced RL mechanisms extend the basic MEAN orchestration framework from single-policy adaptation toward distributed, adaptive, safe, and semantically informed operation. IV. 1) Definition and Connotation of Tokens: Within the MEAN framework, a token is no longer merely a basic text unit processed by large language models (LLMs), but a semantic abstraction of the physical world perceived by embodied agents as shwon in Fig. 10. This redefinition enables tokens to transcend simple symbolic attributes, becoming a core nexus connecting the physical perception space with the cognitive planning space. Essentially, a token serves as a universal cognitive carrier for embodied agents within the perception-reasoning-action loop. It encompasses compressed representations of multi-modal features mapped through a unified latent space, while carrying specific intent instructions and task constraints. Through the transition from primitive bitstream transmissions to structured semantic tokenization, the representation and flow of information within embodied networks are fundamentally reshaped, imbuing the communi-cation process with a high cognitive dimension. This supports a transition in end-to-end collaborative logic from physical-layer signal replication to application-layer intent alignment. 

2) Multi-modal Feature Compression: Multi-modal feature compression is essential for exchanging task-relevant infor-mation under limited bandwidth. Rather than transmitting full sensory streams, MEAN maps high-dimensional observations into compact representations in a shared latent space. Three mechanisms support this task-oriented compression: 

• Semantic Bottleneck Constraints : Heavily inspired by the Information Bottleneck (IB) theory, the core logic of this mechanism lies in trading off irrelevant raw details for high compression ratios and generalization capabilities. Unlike traditional source coding governed by the Mean Squared Error (MSE) criterion, the tok-enization process in MEAN follows a task-driven in-formational utility objective. The Perception Module acts as a non-linear encoder, mapping high-dimensional data —such as LiDAR point clouds, visual imagery, and RF signals —onto a low-dimensional task-optimal mani-fold. Under these constraints, the agent extracts only the specific semantic features essential for decision-making, such as object categories, relative poses, and dynamic trajectories, while suppressing task-irrelevant background artifacts and stochastic environmental noise. This reduces bandwidth requirements by orders of magnitude and enhances system robustness, ensuring the precise delivery of primary intents under extreme resource constraints [83], [84]. 

• Cross-modal Alignment and Representation : To fa-cilitate inter-agent semantic coherence among agents equipped with heterogeneous sensors, MEAN utilizes multi-modal large language models (VLMs) as the un-derlying architecture to construct a Unified Latent Space. This mechanism employs techniques such as contrastive learning to encode information from diverse modalities, including vision, haptics, and array signals, into a consis-tent token representation system. This aligned representa-tion achieves latent-space semantic invariance: regardless of whether the input originates from optical sensors or radar depth maps, the generated tokens maintain topo-logical proximity within the shared latent manifold. Such high semantic faithfulness is critical for handling Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDP). > •

Tool Invocation and External API Triggering : When local cognitive capabilities are insufficient for specialized tasks, MEAN introduces an automated tool invocation mechanism. The Toolformer mechanism, through self-supervised learning, enables the model to autonomously insert specific API call tokens into the generated token stream, triggering on-demand calls to external utilities such as calculators, search engines, or specialized sen-sors [88]. Furthermore, when integrated with the Gorilla framework, MEAN nodes can autonomously retrieve and invoke an extensive array of external RESTful APIs, effectively mitigating the inherent constraints of LLMs regarding domain-specific knowledge and numerical pre-cision [89]. This augmented reasoning paradigm en-ables embodied agents to leverage the Reasoning and Acting (ReAct) framework to facilitate iterative action-observation cycles and real-time decision refinement based on the functional tokens retrieved from external computational interfaces [90]. 

4) Transformation of Communication Paradigms: The core logic of the MEAN communication protocol is undergoing a fundamental transformation from transmitting how to execute (bits) to what to execute (intent/tokens). This shift marks a paradigm leap from the traditional internet of things (IoT), where devices are merely responsible for perception and transmission, to the internet of agents (IoA), where edge nodes possess capabilities for perception, reasoning, planning, and independent action [91].  

> •

Intent-driven Interaction : In contrast to the bit-oriented paradigm focused on precise replication of waveforms or bit sequences, MEAN prioritizes intent understanding. Embodied agents exchange high-level semantic tokens, allowing the receiver to autonomously infer subsequent actions based on local semantic cues. This significantly reduces the dependency on continuous, high-intensity raw data exchange, thereby drastically decreasing communi-cation overhead [84], [92].  

> •

Cognitive Consistency under Resource Constraints :In 6G edge environments, achieving full physical-layer synchronization and global state visibility is difficult due to channel jitter, limited bandwidth, and dynamic network topologies. To address this, MEAN introduces the model context protocol (MCP) [93]. Distributed em-bodied agents share key task tokens and context rep-resentations via MCP to ensure consistent contextual understanding across different nodes. This ensures that group collaboration, such as coordinated coverage by UAV swarms or dynamic scheduling in vehicular net-works, maintains high goal-driven coherence and task ex-ecution robustness under low-bandwidth, non-stationary conditions [94], [95]. 

B. Intent Grounding and High-Level Task Decomposition 

Intent grounding connects language-level task goals with physical entities, spatial context, and agent capabilities. In MEAN, this requires aligning instructions with multi-modal observations and execution constraints so that the resulting 16 

task tokens can support feasible decomposition and planning in dynamic, partially observable environments. 

1) Multi-modal Intent Grounding: Within the MEAN inter-action ecosystem, intent grounding transcends unimodal lin-guistic extraction; instead, it manifests as a deep tensor fusion and feature alignment between multi-modal perceptual streams and the cognitive core. The system synchronizes captured visual scenes, acoustic commands, and RF perceptual data in the spatio-temporal domain for integration into the embodied cognitive core. This fusion mechanism endows the system with context-aware situational comprehension, enabling the disambiguation of underspecified commands based on the real-time physical environment.  

> •

Context-aware Intent Instantiation : The Perception Module provides the requisite physical references for semantic instructions through the cross-verification of real-time visual features, acoustic signals, and RF fin-gerprints. For instance, upon receiving an abstract com-mand regarding the traversal of a designated region, the system utilizes spatial tokens to anchor the intent to a specific local coordinate frame and its associated physical parameters. Global correlation between linguistic query vectors and visual feature maps is executed via cross-attention mechanisms. By constructing a multi-modal alignment score matrix, the system identifies the physical properties and spatial poses of specific objects refer-enced in the command within the current local coordi-nate system. This process typically leverages frameworks such as Grounded Language-Image Pre-training (GLIP) or Grounding DINO, unifying visual object detection and semantic comprehension under a singular objective function to facilitate the real-time anchoring of intent within the physical domain [96], [97].  

> •

Task Tokenization : The cognitive core maps environment-augmented instructions into a sequence of structured task tokens. Each token represents a logical atom and implicitly includes the boundary conditions and physical constraints of the action. MEAN transforms intent into executable code blocks or policy tokens, allowing agents to leverage the reasoning power of pre-trained models to generate logically rigorous low-level instructions. 

2) Task Decomposition and Chain-of-Thought Planning: 

Faced with long-horizon tasks characterized by high non-stationarity and complex physical constraints, MEAN must possess the ability to deconstruct macro-intents into micro-action primitives. This process primarily relies on hierarchical task topology construction and a token-sequence-based CoT reasoning mechanism.  

> •

Hierarchical Task Topology Construction : MEAN uti-lizes large-scale models as centralized decision-making engines to recursively decompose high-level abstract goals into a series of sub-goal tokens characterized by rigorous causal dependencies. Diverging from unimodal linguistic planning, MEAN incorporates a physical af-fordance assessment mechanism during the decomposi-tion phase. When generating individual task tokens, the cognitive core evaluates not only the semantic coherence of the plan but also the operational feasibility of the action within the current physical state, integrating en-vironmental tokens facilitated by the perception module [98]. Consequently, the system constructs a dynamic task topology, ensuring that complex long-horizon planning is consistently grounded within the feasible operational envelope. This approach effectively mitigates cognitive hallucinations and semantic-physical inconsistencies that are prevalent when traditional large-scale models are deployed in embodied scenarios.  

> •

Dual-constrained CoT Reasoning : To facilitate the seamless mapping from discrete symbolic logic to con-tinuous actuation, MEAN employs an enhanced CoT reasoning protocol. By integrating formal logical pred-icates and spatio-temporal constraint parameters into the token stream, the architecture implements an internalized self-reflective reasoning process for task execution [99]. Prior to the dispatch of each task token to the actuation layer, the cognitive core performs closed-loop verification to ensure the task sequence preserves causal coherence within the temporal domain and adheres to kinematic and collision-avoidance constraints in the spatial dimen-sion. This modular planning mechanism enables agents to adapt to stochastic environmental perturbations by selectively updating affected token nodes rather than ne-cessitating global plan re-computation, thereby enhancing computational efficiency and operational robustness.  

> •

Multi-agent Token Chaining : In scenarios requiring multi-agent swarm intelligence, such as coordinated re-connaissance by UAV swarms, MEAN facilitates orches-trated task distribution and cross-node synchronization. The leader node defines the operational constraints and boundary conditions for the motion primitives of collab-orative nodes through the generation of structured path-planning token clusters. By leveraging the end-to-end mapping capabilities of Robotic Transformer 2 (RT-2), these high-level semantic tokens are translated into low-level actuation tokens. This architecture enables hetero-geneous agents to function as an integrated decentralized execution system, completing complex spatio-temporal collaborative tasks via a deterministic and sequential propagation of token-mediated control signals [84], [100].

D. Optimization and Performance Metrics for Token Networks 

Token-network optimization must balance semantic fidelity, task performance, communication overhead, and heteroge-neous edge-computing resources. This section considers com-pact model support for token processing and task-aware met-rics for evaluating communication efficiency and semantic reliability. 

1) Compact Model Support and Edge Deployment: Given the power consumption and memory bandwidth limitations of mobile embodied devices, deploying large-scale models with full parameters is often impractical. Therefore, utilizing compaction techniques to build small language models (SLMs) or compact embodied models is a critical technical path for ensuring that edge nodes can generate high-quality reasoning tokens in real-time.  

> •

Multi-dimensional Model Compression : MEAN adopts a combined strategy of quantization and structural prun-ing. By introducing techniques such as AWQ, models 18 

can run at low bit-width precision, significantly reduc-ing memory footprint while maintaining the faithfulness of semantic reasoning [87]. Additionally, by removing redundant attention heads or neuron layers, pruning techniques further reduce the Floating Point Operations (FLOPs) per token inference, ensuring that embodied nodes maintain millisecond-level intent response speeds even with low computational power [101].  

> •

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) and LoRA Adaptation : To enable compact models to handle specific embodied tasks, such as navigation in specific terrains or industrial gripping, MEAN extensively applies parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) technology. By training only a tiny fraction of low-rank decomposition matrices, LoRA allows nodes to quickly adapt to new physical constraints and task intents without altering the pre-trained model backbone. This approach reduces training energy consumption and allows agents to achieve rapid knowledge transfer by exchanging small adapter weights [102]. 

2) Key Performance Metrics: Conventional throughput and packet-loss metrics do not capture whether exchanged tokens preserve task-relevant meaning. Concurrently, complex em-bodied manipulation scenarios require LAM to perform joint reasoning over multi-modal data (visual, linguistic, tactile, etc.), imposing computational loads that frequently exceed the capacity of a single chip on a mobile terminal. Consequently, MEAN must seek a novel deployment paradigm that balances model capability against device constraints, which avoids sacrificing real-time performance by placing LAM entirely in the cloud, while also circumventing the computational and power limitations of deploying them wholly on the edge [104], [105]. However, deploying LAM in MEAN scenarios requires careful consideration of the following issues: the design of the deployment architecture, token-based communication mecha-nisms between nodes, the capability for real-time inference execution, and continuous deployment optimization in dy-namic environments. Specifically, the deployment architecture determines the specific LAM deployment pattern in the cloud-edge-device system. Token-based communication mechanisms enable efficient information flow of tokens as the fundamental unit between different nodes to meet the requirements of real-time tasks. A. Cloud-Edge-Device and Multi-Agent Deployment Architec-tures 

Deploying LAM in MEAN scenarios requires careful con-sideration of the deployment architecture. Currently, there are two mainstream cloud-edge-device collaborative deployment architectures. The first is the large-small model collaboration architecture, which deploys lightweight small models on the edge device to handle routine tasks, while the cloud-based large model serves as an enhanced processor for complex samples. The second is the model partitioning collaboration architecture, which partitions LAM hierarchically, deploying shallow layers on the edge/device and deep layers on the cloud, transmitting intermediate activations rather than raw data between nodes. As shown in Fig. 12. However, the above architectures are primarily designed for general edge computing scenarios and face new challenges when directly migrated to MEAN. The core characteristics of MEAN lie in the continuous movement of agents leading to dynamic network topology changes, rigid constraints on infer-ence latency imposed by physical closed-loop control, and the deep integration of communication, perception, computation, and control. These characteristics require specially designed deployment architectures for LAM in MEAN. 

1) Large-Small Model Collaboration Architecture: Cloud-based large models are mainly responsible for complex reasoning, planning, and knowledge-intensive tasks, where broad generalization, deep semantic understanding, and cross-domain knowledge are essential. Edge-based small models, by contrast, handle rapid processing of routine requests, precise computation, and pre-processing near the data source, offering low-latency responses, personalization, and local data process-ing. This complementary division of labor is the foundation of large-small model collaboration: rather than forcing a single model to satisfy all requirements, the system assigns each part of the workload to the tier best suited for it. Through collaborative inference and training, the two tiers achieve dynamic task allocation, task partitioning, and result fusion under the premise of preserving data locality. A typical allocation mechanism uses the small model as the primary processor for routine requests and triggers a cloud-based large model call when the small model’s output confidence falls be-low a preset threshold. For task partitioning, a single task can be decomposed into subtasks, with the small model handling precise computation or pre-processing and the large model handling complex reasoning and planning. Hybrid mechanisms further integrate task-level and token-level collaboration —for example, speculative decoding in which the small model rapidly generates drafts and the large model verifies them in parallel, optionally combined with retrieval-augmented gener-ation to enhance the small model’s domain knowledge cover-age. These mechanisms enable efficient, scalable, and reliable intelligent services in heterogeneous, resource-constrained en-vironments while preserving data locality [106], [107]. 

2) Model Partitioning and Distributed Inference: The core idea of the model partitioning collaboration architecture is to partition LAM hierarchically or by components, deploying shallow layers on the edge or device tier and deep layers on the cloud tier, transmitting intermediate activations rather than raw data between nodes. Its design motivation lies in the fact that the parameter count of a complete LAM far exceeds the capacity of a single device, yet its computational 20 

graph contains natural partition points —shallow layers are responsible for general feature extraction, while deep layers handle semantic understanding and action generation. Under this architecture, the most critical problem to be solved in the deployment architecture is the selection of the partition point. The partition point determines the trade off between computational load on edge nodes and the volume of data to be transmitted, which can be dynamically adjusted by sensing wireless channel conditions in real time by offloading more computation to the cloud when channel quality is good, and migrating computation to the edge when channel quality deteriorates [108]. Furthermore, the partition granularity can be refined from the layer level to the attention head level or even the token level to accommodate the heterogeneous computational requirements of different tasks [109]. 

3) MEAN-specific Deployment Requirements: The MEAN scenario possesses unique characteristics that distinguish it from general edge computing, imposing additional constraints and design considerations on deployment architectures. In MEAN, agents continuously move within physical space, causing the connectivity topology between agents and edge nodes to change dynamically over time. An agent may move from the coverage area of one edge node to another, or even enter regions with poor network coverage. This re-quires the deployment architecture to possess service instance following migration capability. When an agent’s attachment node changes, its ongoing LAM inference tasks need to be smoothly migrated to the new node. Unlike traditional session migration, the state of LAM inference tasks is more complex and information such as the historical tokens cached in the KV cache profoundly affects inference continuity. How to achieve low-overhead following migration while ensuring inference continuity is a core problem that MEAN deployment architectures must address. Meanwhile, the inference results of LAM directly drive physical actuators, and the control latency requirements are typically rigid and non-violable. In general edge comput-ing, latency exceeding expectations merely affects quality of service; in MEAN, however, latency violations may lead to missing control commands, thereby causing physical safety incidents such as collisions. Therefore, the deployment archi-tecture must guarantee worst-case time predictability, rather than merely pursuing minimization of average latency. This means that the selection of partition points and resource allocation strategies must be optimized based on latency upper bounds rather than average values. 

B. Cross-Node Communication Mechanisms for Distributed LAM Inference 

In MEAN, the deployment of LAM does not place the entire model on a single node, but rather distributes its computational and inference tasks across cloud, edge, and device tiers for collaborative execution. This distributed deployment paradigm makes communication an inherent part of the inference process itself, where the data flow between nodes directly determines the efficiency and quality of LAM inference, which demand efficient and reliable communication mechanisms. The deployment environment of MEAN endows its com-munication process with several distinctive characteristics: communication resources exhibit significant heterogeneity and dynamic variation across different nodes [110], the inference-control closed loop imposes rigid constraints on communi-cation latency, and meanwhile, transmitted content naturally contains differences in importance and semantic redundancy, enabling selective transmission [111]. These characteristics collectively shape the design principles of cross-node com-munication for distributed inference. The left half of Fig. 13 illustrates selected communication mechanisms for LAM de-ployment in MEAN. 

1) Node-aware Adaptive Transmission: Nodes in MEAN that ranging from lightweight mobile terminals to high-performance edge servers —exhibit significant differences in communication capabilities, and the mobility of agents further exacerbates the dynamic fluctuations of wireless link quality. When distributed inference tasks need to be collaboratively completed across such heterogeneous and dynamic networks, fixed communication protocols cannot adapt. Addressing this problem requires establishing a node-aware adaptive transmission protocol. When the deployment architec-ture supports both independent and collaborative execution of computational tasks, the system can continuously monitor real-time channel quality, available bandwidth, and other statuses between nodes, and dynamically adjust cross-node commu-nication strategies accordingly. When channel conditions are favorable, the system can offload more computation to the cloud to reduce the load on local computing resources [112]. When channel quality degrades, agents can adjust their com-putation nodes in real time, migrating computational tasks that would otherwise require transmission over low-quality links to closer nodes, or switch to local computation to execute tasks independently and reduce reliance on remote communication. 

2) Deployment-Mode-Oriented Data Flows and Communi-cation Requirements: In mobile network environments, the deterioration of wireless link quality is hardly avoidable, while bandwidth fluctuation, packet loss, and delay jitter directly cause increased inference latency or loss of critical data. More importantly, the data flows generated under different deployment modes differ significantly in type and role, and treating them uniformly would waste scarce bandwidth and potentially undermine real-time performance. Therefore, these data flows must first be explicitly distinguished. In large-small model collaboration, nodes mainly exchange task requests, context, and inference results, while in model partition deploy-ment, intermediate activations at the partition point must be transmitted across nodes, and in service migration scenarios, model parameters, KV caches, and request execution states may need to be transferred. These data flows differ fundamentally in their commu-nication requirements: task requests and context determine whether inference can be correctly initiated, inference results and intermediate activations affect inference continuity and the stability of the control loop, while model parameters and execution states place greater emphasis on consistency and integrity during migration. C. Real-Time Inference and Resource-Constrained Execution of LAM 

After deploying LAM across the cloud-edge-device nodes of MEAN, the execution efficiency during the inference phase directly determines whether the system can meet the real-time constraints of the physical world. More critically, LAM infer-ence is not an isolated computational process but is embedded within a physical closed loop, which means that the action output by inference must reach the actuator within a strict time budget, otherwise the agent’s state will have already changed, rendering the inference result invalid. Therefore, real-time inference mechanisms for deployment scenarios need to maxi-mize the role of key information and reduce redundancy during reasoning, while fully considering the computing constraints of edge devices and adopting timeout handling strategies to sustain system operation when latency requirements cannot be met. The right half of Fig. 13 presents selected inference mechanisms for LAM deployment in MEAN. 

1) Early Exit Mechanisms and Adaptive Depth Pruning: 

Early exit mechanisms place lightweight classifiers between LAM layers, each producing an action prediction and a confi-dence level from the current hidden state. When the confidence at an intermediate layer exceeds a preset threshold, inference terminates early at that layer and outputs the action directly, avoiding full forward propagation for every input. This mechanism is suitable when the required inference depth varies with input complexity: simple commands in open environments may need only the first few layers, whereas precise navigation through dense obstacles still requires full forward propagation. Adaptive thresholds can further adjust exit aggressiveness according to task criticality and available time budget, lowering confidence requirements under high load or tight real-time constraints in exchange for faster response. The key risk arises from the joint design of thresholds and inter-layer classifiers: if the threshold is set too low or confi-dence estimates are unreliable, complex or critical inputs may exit early before sufficient information is available, leading to incorrect actions. The resulting speedup must therefore be balanced against inference correctness. 

2) Dynamic Token Pruning and Adaptive Precision Scaling: 

Different MEAN nodes —ranging from lightweight mobile robots to high-performance edge servers —exhibit orders of magnitude differences in computational capability. Deploying the same LAM across heterogeneous nodes requires a trade-off between inference accuracy and execution efficiency. The 22 

strategy of deploying multiple model variants of different sizes directly lacks flexibility and cannot adapt to dynamic changes in runtime resource states. The core approach is to introduce runtime dynamic adjust-ment mechanisms that enable LAM to adapt to the capability constraints of different nodes and the demand of different tasks. And during inference, tokens that contribute little to the final output are dynamically discarded based on their attention scores or information entropy. Discarded tokens no longer participate in the computation of subsequent layers, thereby reducing sequence length and computational load [114], [115]. The pruning ratio can be dynamically adjusted according to the current load state of the node —the higher the load, the more aggressive the pruning. Complementing this reduction in token count, adaptive precision scaling assigns different numerical precision lev-els to different layers or tokens. Critical tokens or shallow embeddings maintain high precision (e.g., FP16), while non-critical tokens or deep features can be downgraded to low precision (e.g., INT4). The precision assignment scheme can be dynamically generated by a lightweight runtime analysis module that monitors the activation value distribution and numerical sensitivity of each layer, recommending the optimal precision configuration for each layer [116]. 

3) Inference Timeout Handling: The uncertainty of LAM inference, including varying sequence length and fluctuating network conditions leads to significant jitter in inference latency for each execution. However, the physical control loop typically requires stability in the output action frequency, which can be disrupted by latency fluctuations. More critically, when a particular inference instance severely exceeds its time budget, the agent may completely lose control input [117], directly threatening operational safety. Therefore, a timeout handling mechanism must be established: when LAM inference cannot complete within the current control cycle, the system should not wait but should immediately switch to an alternative action source, ensuring that the actuator always receives valid control signals. The core of the timeout handling mechanism lies in fully utilizing local computational resources and available data to achieve effective control relaying, rather than simply stopping actions or pausing the task. The fallback strategy for timeout handling can adopt dif-ferent sources depending on the underlying deployment ar-chitecture. Under model partitioning or full deployment ar-chitectures, the intermediate layers of LAM already contain coarse-grained action predictions, when remaining time is insufficient, the best available intermediate layer output can be extracted as an emergency action, offering the advantages of strong semantic consistency and no need for additional backup model maintenance. Under the large-small model collaboration architecture, where the intermediate layer states of the cloud-based LAM are unreachable, a lightweight policy network pre-deployed on the agent’s local device is required, which gener-ates emergency actions with extremely low inference latency. The two strategies can be flexibly combined or independently selected based on the actual deployment architecture. 

D. Adaptive Coordination and Continuous Evolution of LAM 

After the initial deployment of LAM in MEAN, the system does not enter a static and unchanging operational state. The environment in which mobile embodied agents operate evolves continuously —surrounding objects keep changing, task objectives are constantly updated —while the system’s own resource status also fluctuates dynamically, including variations in wireless channel quality and fluctuations in edge node loads. A static deployment scheme and fixed-parameter LAM model cannot adapt to such dynamic changes. Therefore, an adaptive and continuous evolution mechanism must be established, enabling deployment strategies, model parameters, and interaction semantics to co-evolve with changes in both the environment and system state [118]. 

1) Dynamic Optimization of Deployment Strategy and Ar-chitecture: The initial deployment scheme is configured based on the system state at task startup, but this state changes continuously over long-term operation. Individual edge nodes may become gradually overloaded due to an increasing number of agents being served, or an agent may enter an area with poor network coverage, degrading the connection quality to its edge node. If the deployment scheme remains unchanged, the originally optimal node allocation will evolve into aperformance bottleneck. The core approach to addressing this problem lies in estab-lishing an online reconfiguration mechanism for deployment strategies [119], [120]. This mechanism continuously monitors the resource status of each node, including computational load, available memory, network round-trip latency, and battery level —along with task-level performance metrics such as end-to-end inference latency and token throughput. According to the intensity of environmental changes, when performance deviates from expectations, the system initiates dynamic ad-justments of the deployment scheme, such as reallocating execution nodes for certain LAM layers or redistributing token transmission paths based on current network conditions. This ensures that the adjustment process can track the dynamic changes of both the environment and system state in real time without excessively impacting overall performance. 

2) Online Self-Adaptation of Deployed Models: Beyond deployment strategies, pre-trained model parameters struggle to adapt to continuously changing environments and require dynamic evolution with environmental variations. Mobile em-bodied agents continuously accumulate new experiential data containing critical information about the current environment during task execution. The core of online adaptation for deployed models lies in balancing adaptability and stability: the model must learn from new experiences to improve environmental adaptability while retaining its ability to handle original scenarios, preventing service interruptions or performance fluctuations caused by frequent updates. Mechanisms such as continuous learning [121], [122] or reinforcement learning [32] can achieve rapid environmental adaptation and long-term stable operation of deployed models through lightweight parameter updates and low-latency inference scheduling, thereby ensuring real-time responsiveness while providing sustained performance assur-ance for the real-world deployment of LAM. 23 c

Context-aware memory sup-ports prediction of environmental changes and future contact opportunities while accounting for uncertainty in stored infor-mation. These predictions guide adaptive agent collaboration by identifying agents whose capabilities meet task require-ments and whose communication opportunities permit the necessary exchanges within task deadlines. Task-driven cross-layer intelligence jointly optimizes agent selection, routing, and computing allocation to improve task performance under communication delay and interruption constraints. Execution feedback guides subsequent adjustments based on observed task outcomes.

D. ISAC Enabled MEAN 

Unlike conventional communication systems, where sensing and communication are designed independently, Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) allows embodied agents to simultaneously acquire environmental information and ex-change task-related data through shared wireless resources. This capability is particularly important for MEAN, where perception continuously interacts with communication, reason-ing, and control during closed-loop task execution. However, realizing ISAC in MEAN remains challenging due to dynamic environments, heterogeneous sensing modalities, limited wire-less resources, and the need for real-time coordination among distributed embodied agents. Therefore, ISAC should be inte-grated with task-oriented perception, semantic interaction, and adaptive decision-making within a unified MEAN architecture. Fig. 17 illustrates the unified framework of multi-agent context fusion in ISAC-enabled MEAN. Rather than treating sensing observations from different agents independently, the proposed framework enables distributed embodied agents to collaboratively construct a unified contextual understanding of dynamic environments through collaborative information exchange, multi-modal feature fusion, and spatiotemporal alignment. First, distributed embodied agents continuously exchange task-relevant information through heterogeneous communica-tion links, including airground, agentagent, and edgecloud interactions. Instead of transmitting all raw sensing data, only task-oriented observations and contextual information are shared among collaborative agents, significantly reducing communication overhead while extending the perception range beyond individual sensing capabilities. Second, heterogeneous observations collected from multi-ple sensing modalities are integrated through a lightweight multi-modal feature fusion module. Features extracted from different modalities are projected into a common latent space and aggregated through attention-based fusion mechanisms, allowing complementary information from visual, radio, and onboard sensing to be effectively combined into a unified feature representation. Finally, since observations originate from distributed mo-bile agents operating under different viewpoints and sam-pling times, spatiotemporal alignment is performed before they are used for environment understanding. Through the interaction of these three components, the proposed context fusion framework enables MEAN to transform observations into a consistent context-aware environment representation, supporting coordinated decisions.

VII. O PEN PROBLEMS AND FUTURE DIRECTION 

Despite recent advances, existing technologies still lack a unified design and systematic validation framework for end-to-end embodied task execution in MEAN. This section discusses the key open problems and future research directions toward scalable, reliable, and task-oriented closed-loop embodied intelligence.  

> •

Unified theoretical foundations for MEAN: Existing studies often analyze communication, computation, learn-ing, and control separately, lacking a unified theoretical framework for task-oriented embodied intelligence [38], [41], [123]. The key challenge is to establish explicit relationships among network dynamics, semantic infor-mation, physical interaction, and task outcomes, since improvements in individual components do not necessar-ily translate into better closed-loop performance. Future research should develop unified mathematical models and analytical methods that jointly characterize task suc-cess, resource efficiency, stability, and reliability. Such foundations could clarify fundamental performance limits and provide theoretical guidance for cross-layer system optimization.  

> •

Cross-layer co-design under dynamic environments: 

Existing resource management methods often optimize communication, computation, and control independently, making them difficult to adapt to dynamic embodied envi-ronments [18], [38], [124], [125]. The key challenge lies in coordinating heterogeneous resources and task require-ments that evolve across different time scales, where local optimization may compromise overall task performance. Future research should investigate task-aware cross-layer orchestration and adaptive optimization mechanisms that jointly adjust communication, computation, perception, and control policies. Particular attention should be given to maintaining closed-loop responsiveness and stability under mobility, resource fluctuations, and environmental uncertainty.  

> •

Reliable and efficient token-mediated interaction: 

Existing token communication methods lack systematic mechanisms for preserving task-relevant semantics across heterogeneous embodied agents and dynamic wireless links [41]–[43], [46]. The fundamental tradeoff is be-tween communication efficiency and semantic fidelity, since aggressive token compression may discard infor-mation essential for coordination and reliable action ex-ecution. Future research should explore task-aware token generation, adaptive compression, semantic alignment, and context synchronization under bandwidth and reli-ability constraints. Developing interoperable token rep-resentations and error-resilient exchange mechanisms is also essential for maintaining consistent task understand-ing across agents with different sensing modalities and intelligence models.  

> •

Scalable multi-agent coordination and cooperation: 

Existing multi-agent coordination approaches face scal-ability limitations as agent populations, task dependen-cies, and network interactions increase [9], [49], [51], [52]. The central challenge is balancing coordination effectiveness against growing communication overhead, synchronization requirements, and distributed decision-making complexity, particularly under partial observ-ability and dynamic network topology. Future research should investigate hierarchical coordination, distributed task allocation, and communication-efficient collaborative learning to support large-scale embodied systems. Robust mechanisms for role reassignment, local autonomy, and failure recovery are also needed to maintain cooperative task execution when connectivity or agent availability changes.  

> •

Real-time deployment of large AI models in MEAN: 

Existing large-model deployment approaches remain con-strained by substantial computational, memory, and en-ergy requirements, limiting their applicability to real-time embodied systems [40], [45], [46]. The key challenge is balancing reasoning capability against inference latency, communication overhead, and control reliability, since delayed or incorrect model outputs may directly affect physical execution. Future research should investigate lightweight architectures, adaptive inference, device–edge collaboration, and uncertainty-aware execution mecha-nisms to achieve efficient and reliable closed-loop intel-ligence. These approaches must jointly consider model accuracy, resource consumption, and physical task re-quirements rather than optimizing inference performance alone. Safety, robustness, and trustworthiness: Existing ap-proaches provide limited system-level safety guarantees when learning-based decisions, wireless communication, and physical control are tightly coupled [39], [42], [50]. The main challenge is that errors in perception, reason-ing, or communication can propagate through the closed loop, potentially causing unsafe actions or task fail-ures. Moreover, adaptive policies and large-model outputs may introduce additional uncertainty and unpredictable behavior. Future research should develop uncertainty-aware decision-making, runtime safety monitoring, fault-tolerant control, and fallback mechanisms. Integrating formal safety constraints with adaptive intelligence is es-sential for reliable operation under dynamic and uncertain conditions.  

> •

Benchmarking and evaluation methodology: Ex-isting benchmarks primarily evaluate communication, robotics, or AI capabilities separately, lacking standard-ized methodologies for assessing complete MEAN sys-tems [9], [11], [12], [36], [49]. The key challenge is establishing reproducible evaluations that capture inter-actions among network conditions, computational re-sources, intelligent decisions, and physical task outcomes. Improvements in isolated metrics may not reflect actual gains in closed-loop execution. Future research should develop integrated benchmarks and experimental testbeds incorporating realistic wireless dynamics, heterogeneous agents, and multi-agent tasks. Unified evaluation proto-cols should jointly measure task success, latency, resource efficiency, reliability, and safety to enable meaningful system-level comparisons.  

> •

Interoperability and system standardization: Exist-ing embodied intelligence systems employ heteroge-neous hardware, sensing modalities, control interfaces, and communication protocols, limiting interoperability and system integration [37], [38], [82], [127]. The key challenge is enabling consistent task coordination and information exchange across diverse agents, intelligence models, and edge–cloud infrastructures without sacrific-ing platform-specific capabilities. Future research should establish common abstractions for agent capabilities, task descriptions, resource states, and semantic tokens, to-gether with standardized interfaces for cross-layer inter-action. Modular architectures and interoperable protocols will be essential for transforming isolated MEAN proto-types into scalable, reusable, and extensible systems. VIII. C ONCLUSIONS 

This survey shows that supporting mobile embodied agents requires more than adding isolated AI functions to wireless networks. The central challenge is to coordinate communica-tion, intelligence, and physical action around evolving task objectives and execution feedback. Accordingly, RL, token communication, and large AI models should not be viewed as independent technical modules, but as complementary mechanisms that jointly enable adaptive, semantically aligned, and cognitively capable operation. The future development of MEAN therefore calls for system design and evaluation to move beyond individual network or model metrics and focus on the effectiveness of the entire embodied task loop. This task-centered perspective provides a basis for integrat-ing emerging technologies into practical networked embodied intelligence.
