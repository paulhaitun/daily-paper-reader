# Optimizing Airport Electric Ground Support Fleets with Digital-Twin-Guided Predictive Control 

Juan Pablo Bertucci 1, Mauro Salazar 1, and Theo Hofman 1

Abstract —Electrifying airport ground operations is a key step toward reducing aviation-related emissions, but it makes task assignment and charging coordination strongly coupled in real time. This paper develops a digital twin of airport ground handling operations for electric ground-support vehicles (EGSVs), combining an agent-based model of airport operations with live flight-schedule updates and vehicle-energy dynamics. On this platform, we compare three control strategies for EGSV dispatching and charging: a rule-based heuristic, a rolling-horizon model predictive controller (MPC), and a Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) controller. The controllers are evaluated on three representative Schiphol Airport operating days and for multiple fleet sizes. The results show a clear trade-off between delays and energy use. Across all tested scenarios, MCTS yields the lowest average flight delay, with a pooled delay of 4.09 min, improving on the heuristic and MPC controllers by 64.7% and 31.5%, but at an increased energy consumption of 1% and 0.3%, respectively. The MPC and MCTS controllers coordinate charging more effectively throughout the day and reduce the peak charging infrastructure required by 6.0 and 4.7 charger units, respectively. Additionally, we find more vehicles can result in lower overall energy consumption and lower charging infras-tructure requirements for all controllers. These findings highlight the value of digital twins not only as offline design evaluation tools, but also as practical environments for real-time control policies in electrified airport operations. 

Index Terms —Airport Ground Operations, Digital Twins, Elec-tric Ground Support Vehicles, Predictive Control 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Aviation emissions pose a significant environmental chal-lenge, with ground support operation (taxiing, push-back, passenger and baggage transport, etc.) emissions constituting up to 10% of an airport’s total carbon footprint [1]. At Copenhagen Airport, these emissions represent between 5-9% [2] while at Bologna Airport they account for 9.3% of total emissions [3]. To address this issue, airports are transitioning to carbon-neutral vehicle fleets. For example, Schiphol Airport aims to achieve an emission-free ground fleet by 2030 [4]. These fleets comprise heterogeneous Ground Support Vehicles (GSVs), including passenger buses, baggage tractors, and fuel trucks, whose coordinated operation is essential for efficient and punctual Ground Handling Operations (GHO) As these fleets become electric, the joint scheduling of tasks and charg-ing activities for Electric GSVs (EGSVs) becomes critical to avoid operational delays. Literature review: Several studies have focused on the opti-mization of airport GHOs using mathematical optimization, heuristics and simulation-based approaches. Airport-specific models have addressed apron vehicle assignment, dispatching and scheduling under operational constraints using mathemati-cal optimization, showing travel distance reductions, improved resource usage and increased schedule robustness [5], [6], [7]. GHOs have also been modeled as a large-scale vehicle-routing and scheduling problem across multiple service types, where realistic instances are tackled using gradient-free optimization (large neighborhood search and genetic algorithms) [8], [9]. These studies show the value of optimization for airport operations, but they typically focus on vehicle scheduling and resource allocation without explicitly considering the additional constraints introduced by fleet electrification. A second stream of literature focuses explicitly EGSVs. In the broader EV-routing literature, several models extend routing problems to include charging stops, time-windows and electric vehicle-specific constraints [10], [11]. Charger availability and partial recharging decisions have also been modeled explicitly, showing that such features can substantially affect feasibility and cost [12], [13]. Furthermore, offline optimization studies on electric vehicles have shown that fleet sizing, charging-task scheduling and infrastructure design are tightly coupled in electric fleet operations [14], [15], [16]. Within airport-specific applications, recent studies formulate the scheduling of electric arXiv:2610.26270v1 [eess.SY] 19 Oct 2026 2

or mixed ground-handling fleets with charging constraints as bi-objective, decomposition-based or cooperative scheduling problems [17], [18], [19], [20], while stochastic dynamic plan-ning models have been proposed for electric towing vehicles under uncertain aircraft arrival and departure times [21]. Be-yond ground handling, the electrification of aviation networks more broadly has motivated optimization-based approaches for flight scheduling and energy management [22], [23], [24], as well as co-design frameworks that jointly optimize fleet size, battery capacity and charging infrastructure [25], [26]. These studies demonstrate the growing relevance of EGSV optimization in airports, but they mainly focus on offline optimization or a single scheduling formulation rather than on online control within a broader airport operational execu-tion environment. A third stream of literature addresses real-time decision-making for transportation and fleet systems. In broader mobility contexts, Model Predictive Control (MPC) has been applied to reschedule operational tasks and han-dle disruptions in an online fashion [27], [28], [29], [30]. Heuristic and search-based methods such as Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) are viable alternatives for high-dimensional, stochastic fleets because they strike a balance between solution quality and computational time [31]. Deep learning methods have also shown promising results for large-scale airport ground-handling scheduling [8], [32]. However, few studies apply and compare such controller families specifically for heterogeneous airport EGSV fleets while jointly accounting for charging, infrastructure limitations and stochastic execution. Digital twins have also been explored in airport manage-ment for multiple purposes. Existing studies have used DT concepts for airport information management and operational synchronization [33], while related airport applications have considered simulation-supported operational control and DT-based scheduling for airport vehicles and airside systems [34], [35], [36]. These studies confirm the value of DTs for airport operations, but they do not focus on the real-time control of heterogeneous EGSV fleets with explicit charging and energy constraints. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prior work integrates an airport ground-operations digital twin, ex-plicit EGSV energy and charging modeling, and a comparative evaluation of multiple lookahead-based controller approaches for real-time EGSV fleet management under realistic operating conditions, including infrastructure design impacts. 

Statement of Contributions: This paper makes three main contributions. First, we develop a digital twin of airport ground handling operations that integrates real-time flight-schedule data via the airport’s API, and explicitly models charging, energy consumption and operational constraints of EGSVs. Second, we implement and compare three distinct online controller approaches for managing an EGSV fleet, enabling comparison under stochastic scheduling and charging conditions. Third, we use the DT platform as an experimental environment to per-form a comparative evaluation of these controller approaches and their operational and infrastructure implications under realistic conditions. With this, we advance the state-of-the-art in airport ground handling operations for electrified fleets, pro-viding insights into controller performance and infrastructure

We conclude the paper in Section V with the key take-aways from our findings and suggestions for future work. II. D IGITAL TWIN FOR SCHIPHOL AIRPORT 

We consider the general problem of scheduling a fleet of electric ground support vehicles (EGSVs) to perform a set of turnaround tasks for a given flight schedule at an airport. The EGSVs are responsible for performing various services such as baggage handling, catering, and passenger transport, which are essential for the timely departure of flights. The scheduling problem involves assigning EGSVs to tasks across the airport’s spatial layout while considering constraints such as task precedence, vehicle availability, battery capacities, and charging infrastructure requirements. Additionally, the scheduling must account for uncertainties in flight operations, such as delays and cancellations, which can impact the demand for ground support services. The objective is to minimize flight delays while optimizing energy consumption and charging infrastructure usage. In the following we outline the main agents used to develop a DT of Schiphol Airport. The DT models (i) the airport environment and layout, (ii) EGSV movement and energy use, (iii) aircraft turnaround processes, and (iv) charging infrastructure. It includes four primary agent types: Airport Agents, ApronVehicle Agents, Aircraft Agents, and ChargingStation Agents, each described in the subsequent sections. The main agents and their interactions in the simulation are shown in Fig. 3. An in-depth discussion of each agent and the simulation calculations can be found in [37]. 3

A. Airport Agent 

The Airport agent defines the simulation environment in which all other agents are initialised and interact. It stores the network of transitable service roads, and on startup, flight-schedule data from the Schiphol API is used to generate Air-craft agent arrivals. Fig. 2 depicts the simplified layout of the primary service roads at Schiphol Airport, constructed from OpenStreetMap data [38] and official documentation [39]. The layout consists of eight piers, two apron areas, and 142 aircraft stands. The DT relies on flight-schedule data obtained through the Schiphol Flight API, including scheduled and actual ar-rival/departure times and apron stand locations, to generate a complete daily schedule [40]. Aircraft models are obtained by matching their registration code to open databases [41], and this classification, together with operational manuals, provides typical turnaround-task durations. (A table detailing the turnaround, uplift, and other tasks durations is given in Annex 1). The DT incorporates two types of disruptions: temporal and spatial. Temporal disruptions correspond to dis-crepancies between scheduled and actual arrival or departure times, while spatial disruptions reflect changes in aircraft apron-stand assignment. Each Aircraft agent generates service tasks that must be completed before pushback, and these tasks form the demand that ApronVehicle agents respond to.

D. ChargingStation Agent 

Charging Station agents represent individual chargers with fixed locations and power capacities. A charger begins in the 

Available state. When an ApronVehicle receives a charging task, it requests a charger. If available, the charger transitions to Reserved and the vehicle moves toward its location. Upon arrival, the state changes to Charging and remains so until the vehicle is fully charged or until the controller stops charging. The charger then returns to Available . If no charger is available, the ApronVehicle queues or reroutes according to the controller policy.

In this section, we develop the three control strategies for the EGSVs in the DT: First, a reactive dispatching heuristic assigns tasks based solely on each agent’s current state, providing a computationally efficient operational baseline. Second, a Model Predictive Control (MPC) approach solves a rolling-horizon Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP), offer-ing optimization-based decisions consistent with established industrial practice in other disciplines. Third, a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm performs a lookahead search over feasible actions using the updated DT states, acting as a middle ground between heuristics and optimization-based controllers. All three strategies are designed for deployable, closed-loop operation under continuously evolving airport states, rather than on the solving of static combinatorial problems. The interaction between the DT components and the control methods is illustrated in Fig. 1.

V. C ONCLUSION 

This paper compared three control strategies for a fleet of electric ground-support vehicles at Schiphol Airport, evaluated in a digital twin: (i) a nearest-vehicle heuristic, (ii) an MPC-based controller, and (iii) an MCTS-based controller. We find MCTS achieves the lowest overall delays with low energy consumption, making it a promising approach for real-world deployment in EGSV fleets. The heuristic controller yields rea-sonable solutions with minimal computational requirements, making it suitable for real-time applications with limited resources. Operations are also simplified, but at the expense of higher delays and infrastructure and fleet oversizing. MPC offers the most balanced delay-energy trade-off for mid-sized fleets, but does not make substantial gains for the largest fleet sizes. Larger fleets generally reduce charging infrastructure needs and energy consumption through more efficient vehicle assignments, a key upfront cost driver, but introduce greater operational complexity. Intelligent controllers are essential to capturing these efficiency gains and avoiding oversizing; smaller fleets benefit most, as they have less slack and are more vulnerable to inefficient heuristic use. Ensuring adequate KPI levels during this transition is critical, and is enabled by the use of DTs. A key shortcoming of the present work is the lack of validation of the DT with recorded operational data; future work will address this and test the controllers across a wider variety of scenarios.
