# BOLT: Multi-Bottleneck Bound-Guided Learning for Flexible Job-Shop Scheduling with Time Lags and Resource-Coupled Transport 

Ziheng Zhang, Wei Zhang, Yunyi Zhao, Zuming Liu, Wei Zhou, and Boon Ping Gan 

Abstract —Flexible job-shop scheduling becomes resource-coupled when a scarce vehicle fleet moves jobs between stations, because the schedule sets the transport waits. BOLT (bound-oriented learning for transport-coupled scheduling) builds alower bound from chain, machine-capacity, and fleet-capacity relaxations. The bound equals the makespan at termination, so its per-step changes form a reward that sums to a constant minus the makespan. Because it never exceeds the attainable makespan, each candidate’s bound increase is a proven rise of the makespan floor, which the policy reads as a price. The root bound certifies a completed schedule’s suboptimality gap without a solver. On industry-motivated 10-module prefabricated-construction instances, explicit transport coupling outperforms a transport-penalty policy in every tested cell where loaded moves occupy at least 66% of fleet time and in none at or below 56% .Across five zero-shot 50- and 80-module production batches, BOLT outperforms the best dispatching-rule pair on four and the penalty policy on the three with long moves. In the three-vehicle 80-module batch, its mean makespan is 13 .0% below the best pair and 4.2% below an hour-budget constraint-programming solver whose proven gap averages 34% . BOLT decides in 2.3–5.9 ms per event on one processor core and re-plans a disruption in seconds. 

Note to Practitioners —Many plants move heavy modules be-tween stations on a few transporters or automated guided vehicles, and the wait for a vehicle depends on the schedule. This paper gives such a plant a dispatcher guided by a guaranteed lower bound on completion time. The dispatcher decides each machine and vehicle assignment within milliseconds on one processor core and re-plans within seconds after a breakdown. Its station and vehicle decisions have separate output layers, so a plant can retune or roll back one class while the other stays fixed. Without a solver, the dispatcher reports a guaranteed bound on each schedule’s distance from optimal, which is loose on large batches (38–47% on average). The cases studied use published process data for prefabri-cated prefinished volumetric construction. Only tested cases with loaded moves of 2.8 to 4.7 h, as between separate production areas or with a slow fleet, showed gains over a fixed transport allowance. I. Introduction Automated production systems often move partly finished products between stations on a shared transporter or automated guided vehicle fleet. Their machine and vehicle schedules jointly set throughput. Prefabricated prefinished volumetric construction (PPVC) is one heavy-load case, in which factories build whole rooms before shipment to site. Singapore man-dates PPVC on selected government land-sale sites, and one documented project moved 1,866 modules that each weighed 26 to 31 tons [1], [2]. Another documented development erected two 56-story towers from 3,034 such modules, about 27 modules per floor [3]. Each module rides a heavy trans-porter between stations, and every move occupies one vehicle for its full duration, so the plant must assign stations and vehicles together whenever a module becomes ready. This joint decision is difficult because the schedule deter-mines each transport wait. A job moves through a sequence of stations, with a vehicle carrying it between different locations. Some waits have durations that the instance fixes. A cast module must cure, which means harden, for a known time before the next operation can begin, and a machine setup takes a known time once the job order is chosen. Transport waits differ, because a finished module remains at its station until a vehicle becomes free, and how long that takes depends on which other jobs are using the fleet at that moment. Machine decisions set when transport requests arise, while fleet availability sets when the next operation can start. A fixed travel allowance therefore misjudges the delay when vehicles are scarce, and the scheduler must represent the load that each decision places on the fleet. arXiv:2610.22433v1 [cs.LG] 15 Oct 2026 PREPRINT 2

Existing scheduling methods do not provide that represen-tation as a certified learning signal. Prefabrication scheduling studies omit transport or treat it as a fixed time surcharge [4], and learning studies of dynamic flexible job shops model it as a fixed transfer delay [5]. Exact and metaheuristic models sched-ule a finite fleet [6]–[8]. Because they search each instance separately, they do not amortize scheduling decisions into a learned millisecond-scale dispatcher. Learning-to-dispatch policies make online decisions in milliseconds [9]. Several such methods derive each reward from changes in a lower bound on the makespan attainable from the partial sched-ule [10]–[13]. If the bound equals the makespan at termination, these rewards sum to a constant minus the makespan, so maxi-mizing their return minimizes makespan exactly. These bound-guided methods [10]–[13] do not model an explicit vehicle resource. Most vehicle-aware learners [14]–[16] use designed rewards or learned estimates. Moon et al. [17] combine vehicle decisions with a chain-bound reward, but their bound omits aggregate fleet capacity. A chain bound follows each job’s own remaining operations with one fixed travel time per move, so it treats every job as if a vehicle were always free. It stays flat as the makespan grows with the number of jobs waiting for one vehicle. What remains missing is an admissible bound, tight at termination, that counts aggregate fleet workload, so that an objective-faithful reward also responds to fleet contention. The principle adopted here is to represent every shared bottleneck in one admissible bound. For each partial schedule, chain, machine-capacity, and fleet-capacity relaxations bound job progression, machine workload, and mandatory loaded moves. Their maximum remains admissible because none can exceed the makespan attainable from that state, and it selects the active bottleneck with no tuned weight. The fleet-capacity term aggregates transport work across jobs, so it rises under contention where a chain bound stays flat. The same quantity can then connect the scheduling objective to learning decisions, certified gap bounds, and solver search. BOLT (bound-oriented learning for transport-coupled scheduling) implements this principle with a certified multi-bottleneck lower bound. The bound is admissible at every reachable partial schedule (Section IV). It equals the makespan at termination, so its one-step change supplies an objective-faithful reward. For each candidate, the successor bound gives a certified action price, which is a proven minimum increase in the makespan lower bound (Section V). At the root, the same quantity gives a solver-free certified upper bound on the relative suboptimality gap of any completed schedule. As a redundant constraint, its fleet-capacity term also raises the lower bound that warm-started CP-SAT proves in the tested one-vehicle cells (Section VI-D3). The merged-head and factorized realizations read the same bound signals, so controller ownership does not change the learning objective. Whether that objective’s fleet term changes the schedules a plant runs depends on how heavily the fleet is loaded. On the prefabricated-construction instances tested, the bene-fit of explicit coupling depends on fleet load. At the 10-module training size, explicit coupling shortens makespan in every cell at or above 66% loaded utilization, and a transport-penalty policy is adequate at or below 56%. Production batches of 50 and 80 modules match the output of two to three tower floors and lie outside the trained sizes. On the three batches with long moves, BOLT outperforms this penalty policy on every instance. In the 80-module batch with three vehicles, its mean makespan is 13 .0% below the best dispatching-rule pair and 4 .2% below warm-started CP-SAT given an hour, whose proven gap averages 34%. Ablations on four pre-specified cells attribute the training improvement to the action-price feature. Removing the fleet-capacity term lengthens the schedules by 4.8% and 6 .2% in the two 10-module cells where that term sets the bound, and by 12 .9–23 .0% on three production batches. BOLT takes 2 .3–5 .9 ms per event on one processor core, or 0.8–15.9 s for a whole schedule, from 10 to 80 modules. After a machine breakdown, it re-plans in 7–14 s, while CP-SAT at budgets of 1 and 10 s returns the right-shifted plan unchanged. A genetic algorithm needs 33–120 s to find a schedule shorter than BOLT’s. Plants can use BOLT to learn and deploy resource-coupled transport scheduling, bound a completed schedule’s relative suboptimality gap without a solver, and decide when explicit coupling is useful. The scheduling model is deterministic, with processing, waiting, and travel times known before scheduling begins, and machine choice lies within a station cell, as in the PPVC layouts studied. Similar conditions arise when heavy partly finished products move on a small automated guided vehicle fleet [7], [14], [15]. The studied PPVC instances come from an industry-motivated generator whose routing classes and operation structure follow published PPVC process documen-tation [2]. They contain no plant’s production records, and the study reports no plant deployment. Within this scope, to our knowledge, no learned flexible job-shop scheduler combines this bound with certified action pricing, a solver-free certified gap bound, and solver strengthening. Splitting control between machine and vehicle controllers is not part of this claim, because it is established practice [5], [14]–[16]. None of these systems trains on a proven bound (Section II). BOLT makes online dispatch decisions, while exact and metaheuristic methods with per-instance budgets provide solution-quality references. The paper makes four contributions.

Several learning stud-ies below address dynamic or stochastic flexible job-shop scheduling problems (FJSP), whereas ours is deterministic. 

Bound-guided learning for job shops. Learning-to-dispatch methods for FJSP commonly derive a reward at each decision from an optimistic completion-time estimate. Zhang et al. [10] use changes in a chain-based lower bound as the reward, Song et al. [11] extend this to FJSP, and DANIEL [12] pairs the same principle with a dual-attention encoder. With undiscounted returns ( 𝛾 = 1), the cumulative reward is a constant minus the realized makespan, and the time-lag work [13] proves the same property for machine-free lags. That work schedules machines alone, with no fleet and with every wait fixed by the instance. This paper reuses its reward design, instance generator, and lag features. It adds the resource-coupled transport delay, the fleet-capacity term, the candidate prices, the root certificate, the solver constraint, and the regime study. The bound changes because a scarce fleet can become the makespan bottleneck that a chain relaxation alone does not represent. Moon et al. [17] extend this line to FJSP with transport, but their bound omits the fleet. Deep reinforcement learning schedulers that select dispatching rules [9] react to disturbances without a bound. BOLT adds machine- and fleet-capacity relaxations and proves the composite bound for the coupled states defined here. 

FJSP with transport and coupled resources. Bilge and Ulu-soy [6] introduced simultaneous machine and fleet scheduling, Berterottière et al. [7] give a recent model and instance family, and Yao et al. [8] give a mixed-integer model for a limited automated guided vehicle (AGV) fleet. Hurink and Knust [18] apply tabu search to a job shop with one transport robot, and Xin et al. [19] schedule machines and AGVs with a hybrid algorithm. All five schedule vehicles by per-instance search, and none provides an analytic bound at each state that a dispatcher could reuse for reward, action guidance, and solver propagation. Learned transport-aware schedulers avoid this per-instance search. They select dispatching rules for an AGV fleet [20], add transfer-time constraints to dynamic FJSP [21], or pair learned sequencing with a rule for AGV assignment [22]. B. A Taxonomy of Scheduling Delays 

Definition 2 (Delay-semantics taxonomy) . Let a delay be a mandatory wait attached to a scheduled entity. We distinguish three semantics according to what the wait blocks and what de-termines its duration. A machine-free delay blocks the job only, and its duration is fixed by the instance. The post-operation lag ℓ𝑖 𝑗 is of this type [13]. A machine-side delay blocks the machine and consumes no third resource. Its duration is fixed by the instance, possibly as a function of committed choices such as the preceding job [26]. A resource-coupled delay blocks the job and seizes a third resource whose availability the schedule itself determines. Its effective duration, the time from the job becoming ready to its arrival, is therefore set by the fleet’s assignment and sequencing across all jobs. Transport in FJSP-TL-T is of this type. 

Why resource coupling requires a new bound. In the first two classes, every wait has a minimum duration fixed by the instance [13], and a chain recursion can use that minimum for each uncommitted delay while remaining admissible and tight. The same approach fails for a resource-coupled delay. A per-transition minimum remains admissible but can be arbitrarily loose, because it ignores competition among jobs for the fleet. This motivates the fleet-capacity component (Remark 1). 

C. Scope and Modeling Assumptions 

Sequence-dependent setups and machine-availability con-straints are excluded to isolate the effect of the shared fleet. The model treats transport as occupancy of a scarce resource with a tabulated travel time between cells, and it models no road network, congestion, or route optimization. Vehicle charging, collisions, heterogeneous fleets, station-buffer limits, and factory-to-site delivery are outside the model. Station buffers are assumed unbounded. A finished module therefore waits for its vehicle in the cell of its last machine, which stays free for other operations. Blocking machines, which hold a finished module until a vehicle collects it, and curing stages that seize a capacity-limited resource create resource-coupled delays but lie outside the model. Each lag ℓ𝑖 𝑗 sets only a minimum wait, so maximum time lags, which cap that wait, are outside both the taxonomy and the model.

A. Framework Overview 

BOLT uses the certified bound of Section IV through-out training and deployment (Fig. 2). At each event, the environment exposes the partial schedule and the feasible commitments, and the bound supplies the reward and one price per candidate. These signals require no particular policy architecture, so the merged-head and factorized realizations of Section V-E consume the same signals through one dual-attention encoder. 

B. Objective-Faithful Bound-Difference Reward 

Following bound-difference rewards for job shops [10], [11], [13], BOLT emits at each decision the difference 𝑟 𝑡 =

𝜅 (𝐵 (𝑠 𝑡 ) − 𝐵 (𝑠 𝑡 +1)) , with 𝜅 the positive instance-fixed scale of Theorem 1. This construction is standard, and the contribution is the certified composite bound under resource coupling. The undiscounted return is a scaled and shifted −𝐶 max , so maximizing it minimizes makespan exactly (Theorem 1(c)). 

C. Certified Action Prices 

The central learning mechanism in BOLT is candidate-specific bound guidance. For a feasible commitment 𝑎 at state 𝑠 with successor 𝑠 · 𝑎 , BOLT defines the action price 

Δ(𝑠, 𝑎 ) = max (𝐵 (𝑠 · 𝑎 ) − 𝐵 (𝑠 ), 0). The price is a one-step lookahead of the bound, equal to the positive part of the reward cost −𝑟 𝑡 /𝜅 that the commitment would incur. It differs from a heuristic penalty because the successor bound is admissible. By Corollary 1, 𝐵 (𝑠 ) + Δ(𝑠, 𝑎 ) is a valid lower bound on any schedule beginning with that commitment. All candidate prices are computed in one pass, and the policy reads them as one input channel, never as a mask or a reward term. The policy may still select an apparently expensive action when other learned features justify it. Admissibility does not itself certify that the ranking improves schedules, which Section VI-C3 tests separately.

E. Policy Realization and Deployment 

BOLT has two actor heads over one shared encoder, 𝜋 𝑀 

for machine-class events and 𝜋 𝑉 for vehicle-class events. The encoder follows DANIEL’s dual-attention design [12] over operation and machine nodes. Vehicles enter the machine-side attention stream as a third node set, projected to machine-feature width with a learned type embedding. Operation nodes retain the lag channels of [13], and both node types gain anticipatory transport channels. Exactly one head acts at each event, so training is proximal policy optimization (PPO) over two event-class heads with one critic that reads the pooled embedding. We use the MAPPO implementation [29], which reduces to this case when one head acts per step. The two heads mirror how plants assign these decisions. A manufacturing execution system typically dispatches stations, and a vehicle or crane controller dispatches the fleet. Separate output heads give each decision class its own decision-layer parameters, so one class can be fine-tuned, validated, or rolled back while the other class’s head stays fixed. The encoder is shared, so an encoder change affects both classes. Each controller runs a replica of the network, receives a broadcast of the shop state, and acts only on its own event class. A controller whose learned decisions are withdrawn can therefore fall back to a dispatching rule while the other keeps its learned decisions (Section VI-D1). The merged head, drawn on one controller in Fig. 5, can be replicated in the same way, and the factorized form differs only in that the two classes share no output layer. No experiment here measures the value of this structural property. VI. Experiments

What the travel setting means, and what binds. A processing step averages 4.4 h. Actual moves between different cells run slightly longer than the shortest travel, about 30 min at ¯ 𝜏 / ¯𝑝 =

0.1, 2.8 h at 0 .6, and 4.7 h at 1 .0. The two longer settings exceed moves inside one hall and represent moves between separate production areas or a slow fleet, and none is calibrated to a plant’s travel times. In the tested cells, BOLT outperforms the penalty anchor of Section VI-C1 only with these longer moves. With short moves (0 .1), both the anchor and the GA are at least as good as BOLT (Table S-II, Fig. 3(a)). We describe fleet load by the minimum loaded utilization, the share of fleet time spent carrying loads by any schedule as short as the best one found. It runs from 8% to 82% over the grid and from 74% to 80% on the five production batches (Tables III and S-V). 

Baselines. The first family contains nine priority dispatching rule (PDR) pairs, three machine rules crossed with three vehicle rules, named in the supplement. The second is a genetic algorithm (GA) whose random-key chromosome holds operation priorities, machine preferences, and the weights of a state-dependent vehicle rule. It starts from the nine PDR pairs and uses population 100, elitism 5, tournaments of two, uniform crossover, and Gaussian mutation (rate and scale 0.1). Its search stops after 60 CPU-s beyond seeding unless stated otherwise. Every chromosome is decoded through the same simulator, so the GA searches the same constructive class as BOLT (specification in the supplement, measured cost in Fig. S-1). The third is a merged-head control. It makes the same decisions through one decision head, with the same encoder, signals, and budget [11], [17]. The fourth is a warm-started CP-SAT reference (OR-Tools 9.14, default parameters, 300 s per instance, four workers, one run per instance). It is seeded from the best-of-nine PDR solution and has the fleet term of Section VI-D3 as a redundant constraint (model in the supplement). This time-limited reference weakens at production scale. Without the warm start, it found no feasible schedule within an hour on 3 of 3 probed 50-module instances, and at 80 modules its own proven gap after an hour averages 34%. Existing vehicle-aware learned schedulers do not sup-port machine-free lags with these transport and reservation semantics, so reproducing them on PPVC-T would change their problem. BOLT is compared with published learned policies on the independently released benchmark of [23] (Section VI-B3). 

Protocol. Every arm reported here is trained on 3 seeds 

{301 , 302 , 303 } and its makespans averaged per instance. Single-run arms (seed 301) are marked where they appear. Comparisons are paired at the instance level, with each in-stance’s outcome the mean over the training seeds, so the tests quantify instance variation. A two-level bootstrap over training seeds and instances excludes zero in 18 of the 23 headline comparison cells, and the other cells are not distinguishable at the seed level (Table S-XI). The best dispatching-rule pair is selected per cell on the same instances, which favors the rule and makes that comparison conservative for BOLT. We use Wilcoxon signed-rank tests at 𝛼 = 0.05, Holm-corrected within each pre-specified cell family, and report per-instance win/tie/loss counts (W/T/L). Parity claims use two one-sided tests (TOST) with a pre-specified ±2% margin, and all mechanism and equivalence claims follow pre-specified decision rules (rules and dates in the supplement). Training is undiscounted ( 𝛾 = 1, as Theorem 1(c) requires) and runs 2,000 PPO updates (3.4 GPU-hours) per arm. A new station or operation type has no learned type embedding, so it needs retraining at this cost (Table S-XIII). Within the tested ranges of Sections VI-B2 and VI-C2, fleet size, travel intensity, station count, and, after size-mixture training, instance size can change without retraining. The best dispatching-rule pair still outperforms the policy in three tested settings (the 10-module policy at 15 modules with moderate travel, one vehicle for 80 modules, and wide processing-time dispersion at moder-ate travel). An independent validator checks every schedule (protocol and configuration in the supplement).

C. Mechanism and Ablation Analysis 1) Fleet Load Determines When Explicit Transport Cou-pling Matters: Whether explicit transport modeling improves makespan depends on fleet load. Following the regime map-ping of [23], we re-evaluate a transport-penalty policy, the penalty anchor, against BOLT inside the explicit environment (Fig. 3(a)). The anchor is trained with full travel-time knowl-edge but no vehicle contention, and it was fixed before any premise test (specification in the supplement). BOLT outper-forms the anchor in the three cells whose loaded utilization is at least 66%, and the anchor matches or outperforms BOLT in the nine cells at or below 56%. The three winning cells are one in-distribution cell ( |𝑉 | = 1, ¯ 𝜏 / ¯𝑝 = 0.6, 74.3% loaded) and two zero-shot cells at ¯ 𝜏 / ¯𝑝 = 1.0. No grid cell lies between 56% and 66%, so the crossover is located only within this interval. The anchor in Fig. 3(a) is one seed at half budget, and its vehicle decisions follow the nearest-vehicle-first rule while BOLT’s vehicle head is learned. Fig. 3(a) therefore compares the two complete policies and is descriptive. A pre-specified premise test probes the same claim. It required the explicit-coupling policy to outperform the anchor on both scarce-fleet cells ( |𝑉 | ∈ {1, 2}; paired one-sided Wilcoxon, Holm-corrected). The first attempt, at moderate travel ( ¯ 𝜏 / ¯𝑝 = 0.6) with single runs, passed on the one-vehicle cell (74 .3% loaded, +1.32%) but not on the two-vehicle cell (53 .1% loaded), so the test failed. Its one permitted retry, at heavy travel ( ¯ 𝜏 / ¯𝑝 = 1.0), was fixed after that result and before any retry data, so it is an exploratory second test and not confirmatory evidence. With both arms retrained on travel intensities 0.6 and 1.0 over 3 seeds, it passed on both cells, with improvements of +14 .50% (W/T/L 100 /0/0) and +1.96% (82 /0/18). Both attempts agree with the utilization boundary, and the failed attempt restricts every claim that follows to high fleet load (protocol and 𝑝 values in the supplement). At production scale, BOLT is shorter than the anchor on every instance of the three batches of Table III, by 8.0–16.8% (W/T/L 20/0/0 in each cell, 𝑝 = 1.9 × 10 −6, all arms on one processor thread). Every training seed alone is also shorter (Table S-XIX). In the two one-vehicle batches with short moves, the factorized policy does not shorten the anchor’s makespan, while the merged-head policy does (Table S-V).

4) Price Guidance under Processing-Time Heterogeneity: 

The chain recursion substitutes the eligible minimum for every uncommitted operation, so strongly machine-dependent processing times could loosen the pre-commitment bound and weaken price guidance. We test this effect zero-shot by redrawing processing times around each operation’s mean while the allowed spread across machines (the dispersion level 𝑅 ) rises to 100. This level gives a realized within-operation ratio of 7.6 on average (protocol and tables in the supplement). The certified floor stays below the best-known makespan on all 1,500 transformed instances. On the two training cells, the price channel’s advantage over shared-reward training rises from 1 .96% to 3 .54% on the scarce-fleet cell (2 .70% at the stress level) and from 0 .55% to 2 .40% on the moderate cell. Every level is significant without multiplicity adjustment (largest 𝑝 = 2.3 × 10 −3). On the held-out heavy-travel cell, the advantage is negative at the baseline dispersion, not distinguishable from zero at 𝑅 = 2, and positive from 

𝑅 = 5 upward. Commitment replaces the optimistic minimum with the machine-specific time in the successor bound, so the share of candidates with a positive price rises with dispersion (Corollary 1). Against fixed rules, the policy’s zero-shot mar-gin over the best pair persists at heavy travel through 𝑅 = 20 and is overtaken at moderate travel from realized ratios near 2.5. 

D. Deployment and Industrial Decision Support 1) Factorized Control and Its Makespan Cost: Factoriza-tion costs makespan against the merged-head control on the production batches but not at the training sizes. Across the 16 training-size and transfer cells, the factorized policy is cell-wise TOST-equivalent to the merged-head control within ±2% in 15 cells, with largest absolute difference 0 .37% (Table S-XXVII). It is better in the 15 × 25 zero-shot cell at ¯ 𝜏 / ¯𝑝 = 1.0(−2.84%). On the production batches, both arms are retrained on the same size mixture, and the merged head is shorter in all five greedy-decoded cells (Table S-XVIII). Its lead lies above the margin in the two one-vehicle cells ( +4.33% and +4.44%). Equivalence is not established at 50 modules in Table III ( +1.89% and +2.54% at 𝑛 = 20), and the arms are equivalent within the margin at 80 modules under both decodings. Because both realizations consume the same bound signals and backbone capacity, the comparison isolates the cost of factorization, which is therefore not the source of the scheduling gain. When the nearest-vehicle-first rule replaces every learned vehicle decision, both realizations still outperform the best fixed dispatching-rule pair on all five production batches, by 3.8–13.1% (Table S-XX). With the shortest-travel-time rule instead, both lose 11–20% against their learned decisions, so the fallback rule matters. BOLT keeps the factorized form for the separate decision layers of Section V-E. 

2) Millisecond Dispatch and Second-Scale Disruption Re-covery: The deployed factorized policy (Fig. 5) runs as a 34,892-parameter replica on each controller, with no central solver. On one processor core, the deployed size-mixture policy takes 2.3 ms per event at 10 modules and 5.9 ms at 80 modules, including the shop-state update, and it builds a whole schedule in 0.8 s at 10 modules and 15.9 s at 80 modules. A workstation GPU takes 4.3 ms per event at 80 modules, and the process uses at most 519 MiB (Table S-XXXIII). Deciding per event also lets the policy re-plan after a breakdown. When the machine with the most remaining work fails at 30% of the plan on the two heavy production batches, the policy re-plans the rest in 7–14 s and outperforms right-shift repair and re-dispatching (Table S-XXI). Warm-started CP-SAT returns the right-shifted plan unchanged at search budgets of 1 and 10 s, which take 9–41 s of wall time. At 60 s it shortens that plan by at most 0.8%, so the policy’s schedule stays 11.0–19.0% shorter (Table S-XXII). The GA overtakes the policy after 33–120 s of wall time, and the policy’s schedule is then 4.8– 9.5% longer. A plant that can wait about two minutes therefore gets a shorter repaired schedule from the GA, while the policy provides a repaired plan within seconds and a decision at every event. Under duration noise of up to ±30% (one training seed, two cells), re-planning did not significantly shorten right-shift execution (largest difference 0 .7%), and the re-plan stays 4 .1– 6.4% shorter than rule re-dispatch (Table S-XXIII).

of optimal. This certificate is valid on every instance, and its tightness depends on which term sets the bound. The solver closes 267 of the 1,600 grid and transfer instances, all of them chain-bound, and on these the root bound averages 99.7% of the proven optimum (worst 97.1%). Over all 1,600 instances it averages 87.6% of the best-known makespan, and 76.0% PREPRINT 12 

on the 304 instances where the fleet term sets it (both lower limits on tightness; Table S-XVI, Fig. S-6). The measured certificate is therefore loosest where the fleet binds. On the production batches of Table III, the certificate places the policy’s schedules within 38–47% of optimal on average per cell. This guarantee is loose, but it stays valid where the time-limited solver no longer closes the problem. Added to CP-SAT as a redundant cumulative constraint, the fleet term raises the solver’s proven lower bound above the better of its own bound and 𝐵 (𝑠 0). The excess is 3.6–4.7% at ¯ 𝜏 / ¯𝑝 = 0.6 and 2.2–2.8% at 1 .0 ( |𝑉 | = 1, 12 instances per cell, Table S-X). With the constraint, the incumbent is 4.8% shorter at 0 .6 (W/T/L 12/0/0) and unchanged at 1 .0 (6/0/6), and no variant closes an instance in 300 s. VII. Conclusion When a scarce fleet moves jobs between stations, the transfer delay depends on the schedule. BOLT uses one certified multi-bottleneck bound whose chain, machine-capacity, and fleet-capacity components never exceed the makespan still attainable. The bound equals the makespan at termination, so its temporal difference is an objective-faithful reward, and its candidate-action increases are proven rises of the makespan floor that the policy reads as prices. Ablations on four pre-specified cells attribute the training gain to these prices, and re-moving the fleet-capacity term lengthens the learned schedules by 12 .9–23 .0% on three production batches. On the industry-motivated PPVC benchmark, explicit transport coupling re-duces makespan in every tested 10-module cell where loaded moves occupy at least 66% of fleet time, and a transport-penalty policy is adequate at or below 56%. Across five production batches, BOLT outperforms the best dispatching-rule pair in four and the penalty policy in the three with long moves. With three vehicles at 80 modules, it also produces shorter schedules than an hour-budget solver that ends far from its own bound. In the two one-vehicle batches, the factorized policy does not shorten the penalty policy’s makespan, and a merged-head policy does. The root bound gives a valid but loose certificate of a completed schedule’s suboptimality gap without a solver, and as a redundant constraint it raises the lower bound that CP-SAT proves. Stochastic arrivals, vehicle routing and congestion, heterogeneous fleets, finite buffers, and online dispatch without destination-machine reservations remain for future work, and other objectives would need their own bound. Within the tested scope, BOLT dispatches in milliseconds per event, re-plans a disruption in seconds, and gives station and vehicle controllers separate decision layers. References

A redundant fleet-capacity cumulative constraint and an auxiliary linear energy cut carry the 𝐵 veh relaxation of the main text. The model also tightens the horizon to the warm-start makespan and breaks vehicle symmetry. The solver is seeded with the best-of-nine priority-dispatching-rule (PDR) solution. The nine PDR pairs cross the machine rules shortest processing time (SPT), most work remaining (MWKR), and first in first out (FIFO) with the vehicle rules shortest travel time (STT), nearest vehicle first (NVF), and FIFO. The genetic algorithm (GA) uses a random-key chromosome with one priority key, one machine-preference key, and one vehicle priority offset per operation, plus four weights of a state-dependent vehicle rule. At a vehicle event, the rule scores each pending task by a weighted sum of its min-max normalized STT, NVF, and release-order features and its priority offset. The weights (1, 0, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0, 0), and 

(0, 0, 1, 0) therefore reproduce the STT, NVF, and FIFO rules exactly. Every chromosome is decoded through the same event-driven simulator as BOLT, so the GA searches the same constructive class and needs no repair, and the independent validator rechecks its best schedule. The population of 100 holds the nine PDR seeds, each decoding to its own PDR makespan, and random chromosomes. Each generation keeps the five best chromosomes, selects parents by tournaments of two, and applies uniform crossover per vector and Gaussian mutation per gene with rate 0.1 and standard deviation 0.1. The search stops when the budget of process CPU time expires (60 CPU-s on one thread unless stated otherwise), and the seeding before the search is not charged to this budget (Fig. S-1). A. Timing Rules of the Solver Space 

Table S-XII keeps every machine assignment and every machine and vehicle sequence of BOLT’s schedules and re-computes only the start times under the timing rules of the CP-SAT model. That model differs from the environment in two rules. It holds no destination machine during a move, and PREPRINT (SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL) 7

it lets a vehicle run its empty leg before the task is released and wait at the pickup cell. The start times are recomputed in topological order of the fixed precedence graph, and an independent checker of the CP-SAT semantics accepts every re-timed schedule. Pooled over the 10-module cells, re-timing shortens the schedules by 3.2% and closes 33% of the gap to the CP-SAT incumbent. A Shapley split assigns 0.85% of makespan to the reservation and 2.4% to the early empty legs, so the reservation accounts for 8.9% of the gap. At production scale, re-timing shortens the schedules by 1.9– 3.5%. The decomposition keeps BOLT’s own sequences, and a policy acting in the larger space could choose other sequences. Its timing share is therefore a lower bound on the part of the gap that the restrictions of the constructive class cause.

Execution Under Duration Noise Under duration noise of up to ±30%, the policy stays ahead of rule re-dispatch and behind the search methods at every noise level, and it keeps this position without re-planning (Table S-XXIII). The study multiplies every processing time, machine-free lag, and loaded travel time by 1 + 𝜀 , with 𝜀 

uniform on [− 𝜎, 𝜎 ] and 𝜎 ∈ { 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}. It uses the 20 instances of each of the two heavy production-batch cells of the breakdown study, with three noise replicates per instance and one set of draws shared by all arms. The policy is the size-mixture policy of training seed 301 only, so the study does not cover the spread across training seeds. Each arm executes its nominal schedule under the realized durations, either by right shift or with re-planning at fixed checkpoints. Right-shift execution of a nominal schedule loses little makespan to this noise. Right shift keeps every assignment and sequence and starts each operation once its predecessors have finished under the realized durations. At 𝜎 = 0.3, it raises the policy’s realized makespan by 1 .7% at 50 modules and 2 .5% at 80 modules over the noise-free execution. The 60-CPU-s GA schedule rises by 2 .3% and 3 .1%. Re-planning adds no measurable gain to right shift. The re-planning arm re-plans the remaining work from the realized state at nine checkpoints, one after each further 10% of completed operations. Its realized makespan differs from right shift by at most 0 .7% in either direction at every 𝜎 > 0, and no difference is significant (Holm-adjusted 𝑝 ≥ 0.21). Without noise, re-planning reproduces the nominal makespan on all 20 instances at 50 modules and shortens it by 1 .3% at 80 modules (Holm-adjusted 𝑝 = 0.04). A threshold arm re-plans only when a finished operation is late by more than 5% of the nominal makespan. It never triggered in 480 runs, because the largest lateness reached 83% of that limit. The policy’s place among the re-planning methods holds at every noise level. Its re-plan is 4 .1–6 .4% shorter than best-of-nine rule re-dispatch at 𝜎 > 0, and shorter on all 20 instances of both cells at 𝜎 ≥ 0.2. The GA re-plan, with 10 CPU-s of search per re-plan, is shorter than the policy’s re-plan on every instance, by 5 .8–7 .2% at 𝜎 > 0. The CP-SAT re-plan, run on one replicate at 𝜎 = 0 and 0 .2, is shorter by 3 .3–6 .6%. Both search arms start from the 60-CPU-s GA schedule and keep the current plan when it is shorter than their result. They also spend more planning time. One re-plan takes 4 .17 and 9.14 CPU-s for the policy at 50 and 80 modules, against 21 .6and 37 .7 CPU-s for the GA and 18 .0 and 28 .7 CPU-s for CP-SAT. These times include building the residual problem and the warm start, so the GA’s time exceeds its search budget. Duration noise of this size therefore requires no re-planning, and re-planning serves discrete disruptions such as the machine breakdown of Tables S-XXI and S-XXII. XX. External Benchmark: Port Validation and Timing Semantics The released repository of Link et al. [8] does not preserve the seed mapping of its test set, so we validate the port by comparing distributions. We regenerate 100 instances from their generator at its released head and schedule them with their own solver and heuristic classes: all 24 deterministic released combinations (four heuristic pairs at each of six fleet sizes) are compared against the released per-instance makespans by two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, and none rejects at 𝛼 = 0.01. Accordingly, all external comparisons in the main text are distribution-level (unpaired, 𝑛 = 100 per side) against their released model rows. The two environments time the same decisions differently. Their simulator dispatches anticipatorily: a vehicle may travel empty to the pickup before the job is ready and wait there, departing loaded at the later of its arrival and the job’s readiness. Our environment releases a task only when its job is ready, so the empty and loaded legs run back to back. The anticipatory rule never lengthens a schedule. Our policy’s schedules are computed under the stricter conservative rule and then scored by replaying the decision sequences in their simu-lator, so a win carries over. Their executor times our decision sequences and does not hold machines, so our destination-machine reservation does not apply there. Timing semantics explain part of any loss. Re-timing identical schedules under their rule changes the makespan by −1.0% at |𝑉 | = 3, −11 .0% at |𝑉 | = 6, and up to −15 .3% at |𝑉 | ≥ 9 (3 seeds, 𝑛 = 100 per fleet size). The difference is negligible where the fleet is scarce and largest where it is not. The scarce-fleet result also holds for a single credit-only run (seed 301, no price channel), which scores 4213 ±286 at |𝑉 | = 3, within 0.6% of the bound-guided arm, and outperforms the same three released anchors (-15.3%, -16.9%, and -8.9% against their joint model, best modular pair, and best heuristic). XXIV. Deployment Cost by Instance Size Table S-XXXIII gives the cost of running the deployed size-mixture policy at the training size and at the production batch sizes. On one processor core, the time per event grows from 2.3 ms at 10 modules to 5.9 ms at 80 modules. The forward pass and action selection take 1.5 ms and 4.2 ms of this time, and the environment step takes the rest. A GPU keeps the forward pass at 2.5–2.6 ms at every size, so it shortens the time per event only at 50 and 80 modules. Memory changes little with size, because the PyTorch runtime takes most of the 506–519 MiB peak resident memory on the processor. Training the size-mixture policy took 7.3–11.6 GPU-hours per seed as wall-clock time on a GPU shared with other jobs, so these hours are an upper bound on the exclusive-device cost, against 3.4 GPU-hours per arm at the training size (Table S-XIII).
