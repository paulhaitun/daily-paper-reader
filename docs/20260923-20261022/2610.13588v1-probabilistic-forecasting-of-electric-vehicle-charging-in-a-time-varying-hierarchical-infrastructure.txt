Uncertainty quantification for electric vehicle (EV) charging demand is essential to turn the increasing pressure of EV adoption on power sys-tems into a source of flexibility. We study a large-scale dataset of public charging stations in Scotland, where demand exhibits a hierarchical struc-ture, organized across multiple aggregation levels, from individual charging points to regional and national scales. The charging infrastructure is contin-uously evolving, with new stations deployed and existing ones occasionally removed, which calls for forecasting and uncertainty quantification methods tailored to this evolving structure. To address these challenges, we combine hierarchical forecasting with recent advances in conformal prediction with missing data. The conformal procedures considered produce valid prediction intervals at every node of the hierarchy and the algorithm CP-MNR-Nested ⋆

successfully accounts for the time-varying hierarchical structure of the data. 

1. Introduction. The introduction begins by motivating the need for uncertainty quan-tification in electric vehicle (EV) charging demand forecasting and then turns to the specific challenges posed by EV charging infrastructures, with data collected from ChargePlace Scot-land, the EV charging public network in Scotland, serving as a study case. It concludes with a brief overview of related work and the organization of the paper. The large-scale deployment of electric vehicles is reshaping charging infrastructures and electricity consumption patterns, both in magnitude and in temporal structure, and is expected to accelerate further in the coming years (IEA, 2022). This directly concerns distribution system operators and charging network operators, who must plan for and operate a load that keeps growing in scale, evolves as new charging points are continuously deployed, and must be tracked across multiple levels of aggregation, from individual charge points to entire networks (Muratori, 2018; Powell et al., 2022). Beyond these challenges, it also creates new opportunities to leverage EV charging as a flexible resource for power system operations. In particular, smart charging has been identi-fied as a key mechanism to help balance supply and demand in the presence of intermittent renewable energy sources (Štogl et al., 2024; Bogdanov and Breyer, 2024). Realizing this po-tential, however, requires reliable forecasts of EV charging demand (Alaraj et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025), which are subsequently used as inputs to optimization and control algorithms (Fescioglu-Unver and Akta¸ s, 2023; Diaz-Londono et al., 2024). In this context, point forecasts alone are not sufficient, as they only provide a partial de-scription of the energy demand, and fail to capture its variability. Probabilistic forecasting has therefore become a standard tool for EV charging demand forecasting (Khan et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023; Ostermann and Haug, 2024; Amara-Ouali et al., 2025), with the goal of producing prediction intervals around point forecasts that cover the true values with a specified probability. EV charging demand forecasting is inherently a multi-resolution problem, as operational decisions are made across a range of spatial and organizational levels, each tied to a distinct market or grid function. For instance, charge point operators need station-level forecasts to schedule maintenance and manage local queuing and on-site pricing; retailers and balance responsible parties need regional aggregates to size day-ahead and intraday market positions; and transmission system operators need national aggregates to dimension reserve require-ments and maintain system balance. This range, from individual charging stations to regional aggregates and national systems, has been studied at various granularities (Kim and Kim, 2021; Yi et al., 2022; Ostermann and Haug, 2024). The forecasting task across these granularities is structurally interdependent, as the energy demand at a given level of aggregation is the sum of the energy demand at the lower levels. This construction induces a hierarchical structure, which can be exploited to share informa-tion across the different levels of aggregation to improve the accuracy of forecasts at each level of the hierarchy. For instance, Khan et al. (2023) compare forecasting accuracy across different spatial aggregation levels, from individual charging piles to their sum, and Zhang et al. (2023) study a similar hierarchy for the city of Beijing using large-scale real-world driv-ing and charging data, both for point forecasting; van Etten, Degeler and Luo (2024) propose a global model for point forecasting across multiple stations in four cities, while Zheng et al. (2023) directly exploit the hierarchical structure by enforcing consistency across a network of charging stations in the case of probabilistic forecasting. In this article, we explore a hierarchical structure within the public charging network in Scotland through data from ChargePlace Scotland (see Figure 1). The data, first studied by Davies, Hart and Galloway (2024), exhibit a rich administrative hierarchical structure, with 1398 charging stations distributed across 32 local authorities, and a national aggregate at the country level. It embodies a central modeling challenge in EV demand forecasting: charging infrastructure evolves over time. The adoption of electric vehicles drives the deployment of new charging stations, while existing stations may be temporarily or permanently removed from the network, e.g., due to maintenance or changes in ownership. Since only active sta-tions contribute to aggregate demand, it induces a so-called time-varying hierarchical struc-ture. These features raise two modeling challenges. The first is to forecast demand for new stations, with limited or no historical data. The second is to account for the current state of the infrastructure, both for point forecasting and for uncertainty quantification. The latter being an important source of variability for EV charging demand. In addition to these modeling considerations, data from ChargePlace Scotland exhibit sev-eral challenging features documented by Davies, Hart and Galloway (2024). Despite a rela-tively high penetration of electric vehicles in Scotland, total energy demand at the national level shows a decreasing trend over the period of interest (see the right hand side of Figure 2). Operational forecasting of EV charging demand requires models that not only achieve high predictive accuracy but also satisfy practical constraints such as fast and scalable inference. For tabular data and multivariate time series, it is well known that tree ensembles are highly competitive and easy-to-calibrate forecasting models. For instance, frameworks such as Ran-dom Forests (Breiman, 2001), and XGBoost (Chen et al., 2015), are widely used in machine learning competitions (Makridakis, Petropoulos and Spiliotis, 2022; Bojer and Meldgaard, 2021) and industrial applications (Goude, Nedellec and Kong, 2013). Recently, these models have been challenged by deep learning-based approaches. In the context of EV charging, van Etten, Degeler and Luo (2024) consider a global model to forecast energy demand across 55 charging stations in Boulder, Palo Alto, London, and Perth, demonstrating the effectiveness of deep learning architectures like Transformers and N-HiTS (Challu et al., 2023). Alongside deep learning, pre-trained foundation models have driven significant progress in predictive modeling for both tabular and time series data. Models such as TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2025) and TabICL (Qu et al., 2025) achieve state-of-the-art performance on benchmark tasks like FevBench (Shchur et al., 2025) and TabArena (Erickson et al., 2026). Similar to gradient 4 PRINCIPATO ET AL. 

boosting machines, these foundation models can be readily adapted to time series forecasting by incorporating time-dependent features or lagged variables. In this work, we utilize TabICL due to its open-source availability and high inference efficiency. However, while these auto-mated machine learning frameworks are highly efficient, many applied settings require do-main experts to design interpretable models by integrating physical or systemic knowledge directly into the model equations. This ensures a necessary trade-off between predictive per-formance and interpretability. Generalized Additive Models (Wood, 2017, GAMs) naturally facilitate this integration, making them highly popular in power system management (Pierrot and Goude, 2011; de Vilmarest et al., 2023; Antoniadis et al., 2024). To achieve a complete and up-to-date overview, we propose considering these three types of forecasting models, along with a simple combination of them, at all three levels of the hierarchy (see Section 5.2). The aforementioned forecasting methods are tailored for the large number of charging stations in the dataset, but they do not account for the hierarchical structure of the data. This is a common limitation in the context of hierarchical forecasting, which is commonly addressed by producing forecasts that are agnostic to the hierarchical structure, and to subse-quently adjust them to ensure that they follow the same aggregation constraints of the data. A well-established framework for this adjustment is forecast reconciliation, introduced by Athanasopoulos, Ahmed and Hyndman (2009); Hyndman et al. (2011). The idea is to com-bine forecasts from different levels of aggregation through linear combinations to produce coherent forecasts with respect to the hierarchical structure. A natural way to achieve this is through a linear projection of the base forecasts onto a so-called coherent subspace (Pana-giotelis et al., 2021; Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos and Hyndman, 2019). Techniques of forecast reconciliation have been applied in the context of EV charging demand forecasting by Zheng et al. (2023) to produce probabilistic forecasts based on reconciled scenarios. Recently, conformal prediction has emerged as a particularly attractive framework to quan-tify uncertainty around point forecasts, as it produces prediction sets with finite-sample and distribution-free coverage guarantees under mild assumptions (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer, 2005; Lei et al., 2018; Barber et al., 2021). Its appeal in the context of EV charg-ing demand forecasting (Amara-Ouali et al., 2025) lies in its flexibility: similar to forecast reconciliation, conformal prediction can be applied post-hoc to any base predictor, including those obtained from complex machine learning models, without the need for retraining (An-gelopoulos and Bates, 2023). This conceptual similarity suggests combining them to produce hierarchy-aware prediction intervals, as proposed by Principato et al. (2026). In particular, the authors show that forecast reconciliation preserves the validity of conformal prediction inter-vals, while potentially yielding tighter prediction sets, thereby reducing uncertainty around the forecasts. A recent line of work consists in extending conformal prediction to accom-modate non-stationarity in time series settings. Gibbs and Candès (2021) introduce adaptive conformal inference, that updates the conformal sets to account for temporal distribution shifts. Adaptive conformal inference has been applied in a variety of energy forecasting set-tings, including electricity price forecasting by Zaffran et al. (2022), and EV charging demand forecasting by Amara-Ouali et al. (2025). The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides further details about the dataset and formalizes the forecasting problem. The modeling emphasizes the hierarchical structure, its temporal evolution, and the specific objectives it induces. Section 3 is devoted to a pedagogical exposition of the main ingredients of the methodology, namely forecast reconciliation and conformal prediction in the context of hierarchical forecasting. Section 4 presents the proposed methodology. It describes how to combine forecast reconciliation and conformal prediction in order to account for the time-varying hierarchical structure when quantifying uncertainty. Finally, Section 5 describes the experimental setup and summarizes the performance of the different methods on the ChargePlace Scotland data. 2. EV charging energy demand in Scotland. Data is collected from ChargePlace Scotland, a publicly available record of charging sessions from the largest public EV charging network in Scotland. The resulting dataset is referred to as the ChargePlace Scotland dataset. The code used for the experiments is available on the following GitHub repository. 

2.1. The ChargePlace Scotland dataset. The period considered goes from October 2022 to October 2025, extracting session-level information and merging it with spatial and technical characteristics of the charging infrastructure, following Batic (2025). The spatial attributes include the geographical coordinates of each charging point, the address of the charging station, and the local authority to which it belongs. Sessions for which these spatial attributes are missing are excluded from the analysis. Energy consumption at the session level is then aggregated to obtain daily energy demand at each location with available geographic information, thereby constructing a panel of daily demand series at the charging-station level. The charging stations are indexed by i = 1 , . . . , n , where n = 1398 is the total number of stations. For each day t, let y(t)

We consider the natural geographical/administrative partition of the n stations into two levels of aggregation, namely the local authority and the country levels. For instance, the local authority of Glasgow City contains 96 charging stations, and its daily energy demand is thus the sum of the daily energy demand of these 96 stations. This forms a hierarchical structure, which yields a total of m = 1431 nodes (1398 stations, 32 local authorities, and 1 national node). The hierarchical structure is illustrated in Figure 1, with the example of the local authority of Glasgow City. This grouping is motivated by the fact that local authorities are the level at which charging infrastructure is planned, funded, and reported by Scottish public bodies (e.g., Transport Scotland, ChargePlace Scotland), making them a natural intermediate level between individual stations and the national system for both operational and policy purposes. This level also coincides with existing administrative and demographic statistics (population, population density, car ownership), which can be used as exogenous covariates. Nevertheless, the proposed approach can be adapted to other contexts (e.g., by consider-ing finer territorial partitions such as districts or quarters). 2.3. Coverage objectives. Infrastructure activity is a key driver of EV charging demand and must therefore be accounted for when assessing forecast uncertainty. This is addressed by extending the usual marginal coverage requirement to a conditional setting. The goal is to produce reliable forecasts of EV charging demand at each node of the hierar-chy, i.e., for each station, local authority, and the national aggregate, on a time period indexed by t ∈ Test , and to quantify the uncertainty around these forecasts. Given a nominal coverage level 1 − α = 90% , a classic requirement is to produce prediction intervals at node i contain-ing the true demand for at least 90% of the days in Test . For instance, if infrastructure activity is properly accounted for (e.g., through forecast reconciliation), there is no uncertainty about the prediction of energy demand at an inactive station. Hence, marginal coverage may appear satisfactory simply because inactive periods are trivially covered, while coverage on active periods remains poor. Similarly, the uncertainty quantification at an active station may de-pend on the activity of other stations in the hierarchy. For example, a temporary closure of neighboring stations may lead to a surge in demand at the station under consideration, result-ing in a higher variability of the energy demand. As a consequence, assessing coverage by averaging over all days in Test may not be representative of the underlying uncertainty. A natural solution is to extend the coverage requirement to a conditional one, where cover-age is assessed by averaging over subsets of Test corresponding to each specific configuration 8 PRINCIPATO ET AL. 

of the infrastructure. This is infeasible in practice, as the number of possible configurations is large and only few observations are available for each. Therefore, we consider two weaker conditional coverage requirements, where coverage is assessed by averaging over subsets of Test corresponding to ranges of similar infrastructure activity patterns. The first is a lo-cal conditioning, where coverage is assessed by averaging over the days for which the node under consideration is active. This is the minimal requirement for a meaningful coverage assessment, as we are only interested in forecasting the energy demand at active nodes. In the rest of the article, this condition is referred to as validity. The second is a stronger con-ditioning, where coverage is assessed by averaging over the days for which the node under consideration is active, and the number of active nodes in its neighborhood–defined by the local authority–partitions the time indices into two subsets: low and high activity regimes. The rationale for this additional conditioning is that the activity of the nodes within the same local authority is an important source of variability for the accuracy of the forecasts. Note that other conditioning could be considered, for instance by changing the definition of the neighborhood, or the number of activity regimes. This second requirement is referred to as conditional coverage or, equivalently, conditional validity. 

2.4. Efficiency objectives. Beyond validity, the intervals should also be efficient, in the sense that they should be as narrow as possible. Indeed, intervals that are too wide are valid as they always contain the true value, but will not be representative of the underlying un-certainty and will not be useful for decision-making. For instance, the prediction interval 

[0 kWh , 10000 kWh] for the energy demand at a station will always contain the true value, but the station operator should not prepare for a demand of 10000 kWh that is not realistic. Therefore, the efficiency of the prediction intervals is measured by their average length. As for validity, the average length is computed only over the days for which the node under consideration is active.

4. Conformal prediction for time-varying hierarchical structures. The conformal procedure described in Section 3.2 encompasses forecast reconciliation methods that account for the infrastructure activity, but the construction of the prediction intervals is not adapted to the time-varying nature of the hierarchy. In particular, the method is designed to produce prediction intervals that are–only–valid marginally over all possible configurations of the in-frastructure activity for the test day t. This is because the non-conformity scores are computed over the entire calibration set, which includes both active and inactive nodes. It is problematic in the case of EV charging demand, as the non-conformity scores for inactive nodes stations trivially zero for a reconciled predictor based rOLS, leading to prediction intervals that are too small for active nodes that have been inactive during the calibration period. This issue is addressed in the subsequent sections by proposing three extensions of the CP procedure. The first extension, referred to as CP-MNR (for Conformal Prediction with Missing Nodes Reconciliation), is designed to produce prediction intervals that are valid conditionally on the node being active, a minimal requirement for a meaningful coverage assessment. The second extension, CP-MNR-Nested ⋆, is designed to produce prediction in-tervals that are valid conditionally on the entire configuration of the infrastructure activity, a more ambitious goal. Finally, the third extension, Adaptive CP-MNR, is designed to produce prediction intervals that are valid conditionally on the node being active, even when the data exhibit a distribution shift between the calibration and test points.

Its formal statement and the proof of its theoretical guarantees are deferred to the Supplementary Material B.3. 

5. Forecasting EV charging energy demand with confidence. This section describes the study case of EV charging demand forecasting on the ChargePlace Scotland data and de-pict how the proposed methodology addresses the specific challenges of this application. We first introduce the experimental setting, including the rolling-window protocol and the data preprocessing choices. Then, the most appropriate base predictor and reconciliation strat-egy are selected and fixed for the subsequent experiments. Finally, the performance of the proposed conformal procedures is evaluated and analyzed. 14 PRINCIPATO ET AL. 

5.1. Experimental setting. The proposed methodology is assessed on a six-month test period, denoted by Test , from February 5th to August 18th, 2024. The period exhibits a sig-nificant temporal drift in the data distribution (Figure 2), making it a particularly challenging setting for forecasting. As discussed in Section 4.3, the CP, CP-MNR, and CP-MNR-Nested ⋆

procedures are designed for a single test point. To deploy them sequentially over the entire test period, one could in principle retrain the underlying models at each time step, at a sub-stantial computational cost, or, at the other extreme, keep the models fixed throughout the whole period. We adopt an intermediate strategy and update the models every two weeks to account for the dynamic nature of the data. Accordingly, the training and calibration periods are updated using a rolling-window scheme, illustrated in Figure 5. The variability of the performance metrics is evaluated by running the experiments 10 times with different rolling-window partitions of the historical data. The resulting mean esti-mates and standard errors are reported throughout this section. For each experimental run, the available data before the first test block are split into a training set and a calibration set. This partition is then shifted forward by two weeks at each subsequent test block. The calibration set represents 20% of the historical data and is constructed as follows: it includes the week preceding the test block together with one-week blocks sampled uniformly at random from the remaining time indices. This partitioning affects the prediction intervals through a change in the predictors fitted on the training set and in the calibration points used to compute the non-conformity scores. Before fitting the predictors, the data is preprocessed to remove sessions with missing geographical information and, across all nodes, dates with anomalous data reporting as iden-tified by Davies, Hart and Galloway (2024). Subsequently, features are engineered for the regression tasks across the three hierarchical levels. Calendar features encode weekly and yearly cycles using day-of-week and day-of-year variables (scaled between 0 and 1). Public holidays are recoded and treated as Sundays. Weather features are collected by requesting data with the riem package (Salmon and Elchison, 2025). Daily maximum temperature and daily average humidity are calculated at each node. At the station level, these meteo-rological features are derived using a distance-weighted average from weather stations. For regional and national levels, the station level features are aggregated using a weighted average based on the number of charging points per station. With the same weighted average scheme, charger locations (longitude and latitude) are considered both at the station and regional lev-els. Equipment features are also included, such as the proportion of slow, accelerated, and fast chargers at each node as well as a dimensioning factor for the number of charging points and paid charging points, estimated dynamically using the prior day’s number of used chargers and the maximum number of used chargers over the previous 30 days. Charging cost features account for the transition of chargers from free to paid described in Section 1. Historical energy consumption incorporates the prior day’s energy, the energy consumed on the same day of the previous week, and a 1-to-7-day rolling mean. Days with zero demand are set to NA (i.e., treated as missing values) so that predictors are fitted exclusively on active nodes. To prevent the propagation of these NAs into the lags and thus avoid reducing the number of available observations, any NA in a lagged variable is imputed using the last available observation of the same day type. Finally, depending on the model, a shifted logarithmic transformation is applied to the target and the lags at the regional and station levels. This prevents the model from favoring larger nodes while ensuring numerical stability for nodes with near-zero demand.

6. Discussion and future work. We successfully combined forecast reconciliation and conformal prediction in the time-varying hierarchical structure of the EV charging network from the ChargePlace Scotland data. The resulting forecasts and prediction intervals are valu-able assets (e.g., as entries of an optimization algorithm) that accurately reflect the uncertainty of the forecasts for the current state of the charging infrastructure. The numerical experiment demonstrates that CP-MNR-Nested ⋆ produces valid prediction intervals for different con-figurations of the charging infrastructure, whereas CP-MNR and Adaptive CP-MNR, yield prediction intervals that are valid conditionally on the activity of the node under considera-tion, but that are less sensitive to the activity of the whole charging infrastructure. A natural direction for future work is to increase the adaptivity of the methodology through online extensions of the learning and reconciliation procedures. In this direction, one can build on the methodology of Brégère and Huard (2022), that combines aggregation of experts with OLS reconciliation, and of Di Modica, Pinson and Taieb (2021); Hihat et al. (2024) that propose online multivariate reconciliation procedures based respectively on a least-squares and a regularized least-squares online estimation.

A.1. Connection with the conformal prediction with missing data framework. The incentive for the CP-MNR and CP-MNR-Nested ⋆ procedures is to construct prediction inter-vals that are valid conditionally on the state of the infrastructure, represented by a binary ac-tivity vector. This is closely related to the objective of the missing data augmentation (MDA) framework of Zaffran et al. (2023, 2024), where validity is conditioned on a binary mask indicating which features are missing. Nevertheless, the binary vector of infrastructure activ-ity does not encode missingness but instead identifies inactive nodes in the hierarchy, whose zero-valued constraints directly affect the forecast of every node of the hierarchy, through the reconciliation procedure. The two settings are thus conceptually different, but the following observation establishes a connection between them: “Base forecasts are covariates in the reconciliation step, and enforcing the infrastructure activity amounts to setting some base forecasts to zero, which corresponds to a specific im-putation strategy in the framework of conformal prediction with missing data.” This observation explicit is made explicit by building on the interpretation from Hollyman, Petropoulos and Tipping (2021) of forecast reconciliation as forecast combination. C.1. Comparison of the reconciliation procedures in terms of efficiency. The per-formance of the different reconciliation procedures in terms of prediction interval length are reported in Table 4, and confirm the choice of rOLS as the reconciliation procedure. The av-erage length of the prediction intervals is computed conditionally on the node being active for each nodes, and the win rate of each reconciliation procedure is computed with respect to the Direct baseline. Hence, a win rate significantly above 50% indicates that the reconciliation procedure yields shorter prediction intervals than the Direct baseline, advocating for its use in practice if the validity rates are similar. The results are gathered by node type to exhibit differences in performance across the hierarchy. OLS yields, at best, a slight improvement over the Direct baseline: it shortens intervals at the national and regional levels, but it is not consistently beneficial at the station level. By contrast, rOLS is uniformly better, with a clear efficiency gain across the hierarchy; for in-stance with a win rate of 60% , 61 .6% , and 59 .4% at the national, regional, and station levels, respectively, for CP-MNR. Note that CP-MNR-Nested ⋆ is not included in the comparison, since its construction is specific to the rOLS setting (the infrastructure activity vector is not taken in consideration in Direct and OLS). At the station level, the highest efficiency gain is obtained for the CP procedure, with a win rate of 81% . This efficiency gain is actually due to the fact that CP does not achieve the nominal coverage level, as discussed next. 

C.2. Comparison of the reconciliation procedures in terms of validity. Any im-provement in terms of efficiency is only meaningful if it does not come at the cost of cover-age. In this regard, Tables 5 and 6 reports the validity rates (as defined in Section 5.3) at the nominal level 1 − α = 90% and at the relaxed level 1 − 2α = 80% , respectively. Both tables demonstrate that the rOLS reconciliation procedure does not compromise the validity of the prediction intervals. It stems from improved point forecast accuracy, both on the calibration and test sets, recovering the theoretical insights from Principato et al. (2026). In essence, they show that, under the assumption of i.i.d. and elliptical non-conformity scores, forecast rec-onciliation improves the efficiency of conformal prediction sets without compromising their validity. The only exception is the CP procedure, which does not subsample the calibration data according to node activity. Therefore, the rOLS forecast errors are set to zero in the cali-bration set for inactive nodes, leading to a lack of coverage conditional on the corresponding node being active in the test set. 34 PRINCIPATO ET AL.
