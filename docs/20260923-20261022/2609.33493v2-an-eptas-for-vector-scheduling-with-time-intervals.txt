# An EPTAS for Vector Scheduling with Time Intervals 

Junho Hwang Independent Researcher 

Abstract 

We study vector scheduling in which each job is active during a fixed time interval. A job uses several resources and stays on one machine for its entire interval; its resource requirements may depend on the machine. The objective is to minimize the largest resource load over all machines and times. For r machines and d resources, we give a deterministic (1+ ε)-approximation in f (r, d, 1/ε )N O(1) time, where N is the binary input length. This gives an efficient polynomial-time approximation scheme for fixed r and d, extending approximation schemes for scalar temporary tasks assignment. The algorithm merges jobs into blocks whose time intervals are fixed before any machine is chosen, and assigns the blocks by dynamic programming over a balanced recursive split of the time line. We also prove strong NP-hardness and an exponential lower bound in 1 /ε under the Exponential Time Hypothesis, already for two identical machines and one resource. 

# 1 Introduction 

Vector scheduling models jobs that use several resources, such as CPU and memory [6, 5]. When each job reserves those resources for a specified time interval, its contribution begins at arrival and ends at departure. We assign every job to one machine for its entire interval and minimize the largest load of any resource at any time. Resource requirements may depend on the machine, and jobs may have specified sets of eligible machines. Figure 1 gives a two-resource example. The single-resource case is offline temporary tasks assignment [3, 4, 2]. Azar, Regev, Sgall, and Woeginger (ARSW) [4, Theorem 4.4] gave a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) on r identical machines, with running time nO(r3 log r/ε 3) for n jobs. Armon et al. [2, Section 4.1] obtained a PTAS on a fixed number of unrelated machines. We give an efficient PTAS (EPTAS) for the multi-resource model: the exponent of the input length is independent of the machine count, the number of resources, and the accuracy. 

Theorem 1.1. For every r, d ≥ 1 and rational 0 < ε ≤ 1, vector scheduling with fixed time intervals on r unrelated machines and d resources admits a deterministic (1 + ε)-approximation in f (r, d, 1/ε )N c0 bit operations. Here N is the binary input length, f is computable, and c0 is an absolute constant. Arbitrary nonnegative rational demands and nonempty eligibility sets are allowed. 

Splitting the time line at all job endpoints turns an instance into ordinary vector scheduling with one coordinate for every resource and every piece of the time line. The number of pieces grows with the input, and Theorem 1.1 keeps it outside the parameter dependence. When all jobs share the same interval, the model is ordinary vector scheduling on unrelated machines. The scalar identical-machine case has a sharper dependence on the accuracy (Theorem 6.2). Even for two identical machines and one resource, we prove strong NP-hardness and an expo-nential lower bound in 1 /ε under the Exponential Time Hypothesis (Proposition C.1). 

Approach. All main ideas already appear for identical machines and one resource; Sections 3–6 treat this case and prove Theorem 6.2. We split the time line recursively and collect at each 1arXiv:2609.33493v2 [cs.DS] 1 Oct 2026 split the jobs that are active there. Those jobs are merged into few blocks, each placed on a single machine. A block acts as one job whose interval is the union of the intervals of its jobs. The blocks are chosen so that this over-estimates the total load only slightly, and their intervals are known before any machine is chosen. Each split cuts the current part of the time line at its middle and at the median endpoint of the earlier blocks inside it, so few block intervals end inside any part. A dynamic program over the splits then assigns the blocks exactly: inside a part, it remembers only the machines of the blocks whose intervals end there and one constant load vector for the others. Section 7 extends the algorithm to several resources, machine-dependent demands, and eligibility sets, and proves Theorem 1.1. Each job gets a scalar weight, and its resource requirements are rounded into a bounded number of types; within a type, all requirements are fixed multiples of the scalar weight. Blocks are formed within each type, and one dynamic program assigns the blocks of all types together. 

Related work. The blocks follow the rounding phase of ARSW [4, Section 3]. Over-estimating loads by step functions with few steps, and the cut at the median endpoint, follow Grandoni, Mömke, and Wiese [12, Sections 2.1–2.2]. Fixing the block intervals before choosing machines permits the stronger running-time bound here. Our rounding of resource requirements into types (Section 7) extends the minimum-weight normalization and grouping of Armon et al. [2, Section 4.1.2] to several resources. For ordinary vector scheduling on identical machines, Chekuri and Khanna [6] gave a PTAS in fixed dimension, and Bansal et al. [5] gave an EPTAS. Their dimension parameter counts all coordinates; here the number of time coordinates grows with the input. Temporal bin packing [9] and Round-UFP [14] minimize the number of capacity-feasible bins or copies. For scalar temporary tasks on identical machines, unit weights admit an optimal polynomial-time assignment [1], while variable weights rule out approximation ratios below 3 /2in polynomial time when r is part of the input [4, Theorem 5.1].

5.1 The separator tree 

A region P = [ l, u ] is a consecutive range of slab indices; its internal jobs are the jobs whose entire interval lies in these slabs. The separator tree has a region at each node, and its root contains all slabs and all jobs. Every node removes one or two of its slabs, forms groups from the internal jobs that meet them, and passes the other internal jobs to at most three children. The merged jobs of the groups at the ancestors of P are the ancestor merged jobs of P . Each of them contains the common slab of its group, which lies outside P . So its interval meets P in a proper nonempty prefix of P , a proper nonempty suffix of P , all of P , or nothing. In the first two cases it ends inside P , at a boundary ζ between slabs ζ − 1 and ζ of P , where l < ζ ≤ u. In the third case it covers P , and in the fourth it misses P .The node removes the middle slab ⌊(l + u)/2⌋ and the median slab ζ, where ζ is the lower median of the boundaries at which ancestor merged jobs end inside P , counted with multiplicity. If none ends inside P , the middle slab also serves as the median slab. The remaining slabs form at most three children. One group consists of the internal jobs that meet the median slab, and a second group of those that meet the middle slab but not the median slab. Discard empty groups, and let G(P ) be the at most two remaining ones. Every other internal job lies in one child, since an interval that meets two children crosses a removed slab. Compute the merged jobs of the 6new groups by Lemma 4.1, and recurse. The whole tree, with all merged jobs, is fixed before any machine is chosen. Figure 3 shows an example.

All input rationals are written in binary, and the product of their denominators has O(N )bits. Every sum of input demands or scalar weights is therefore a polynomial-bit numerator over a shared denominator, and computing qij , wi, W , and LB takes polynomially many exact comparisons and additions. The integer s = ⌈rd/ε ⌉ has O(N ) bits. A retained coefficient is stored as the integer ⌈sp ijk /w i⌉ ∈ { 0, . . . , s 2}; comparing these integers and the sets E′ 

> i

identifies the types in polynomial time, and there are at most n of them. Every target Tν , in (17) and in the proof of Theorem 1.1, has polynomial bit length, and the definitions of η, τ , Q, λ, and the tolerances of Lemma 3.2 add products and quotients of polynomial-bit numbers. For a group, the values mi are slab values of WG, a class index follows from comparisons with powers of two, and the partitions of Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 come from scans of exact prefix sums. C.4 Three-coloring and the lower bounds 

Three-coloring as a monotone 1-in-3 formula. For a graph H with nH vertices and mH edges, take variables Xu,q for every vertex u and color q ∈ { 0, 1, 2} and Zuv,q for every edge uv and color q, with the clauses

Proof of Proposition C.1. Hardness. Three-coloring is NP-complete already for graphs of maxi-mum degree four [11]. For such a graph and fixed r ≥ 2, Lemmas C.2 and C.3 applied to its formula give, in polynomial time, an r-machine instance with integer endpoints in {0, . . . , L } and weights in {1, 2} whose optimum is θ if the graph is three-colorable and at least θ + 1 otherwise. An assignment is verified by an endpoint sweep, and all numbers are polynomially bounded, so the decision problem is strongly NP-complete [10]. 

No FPTAS. An FPTAS run with ε = 1 /(2 θ) returns, on a three-colorable input, a peak at most θ + 1 /2, hence at most θ by integrality; on any other input every peak is at least θ + 1. Since 1 /ε = 2 θ is polynomial in the input, this would decide three-colorability in polynomial time, so no FPTAS exists unless P=NP. 

The ETH bounds. Maximum degree four gives mH ≤ 2nH , hence nφ ≤ 9nH , mφ ≤ 7nH ,and θ ≤ 60 nH + 2 by (30). For fixed r the instance has O(nH ) jobs with integer endpoints and weights, so N = O(nH log nH ). With ε = 1 /(2 θ), a deterministic (1 + ε)-approximation in 2 o(1 /ε ) poly( N ) bit operations would decide three-colorability of graphs of maximum degree four in 2 o(nH ) poly( nH ) time. Under the Exponential Time Hypothesis [13] no such algorithm exists [7, Lemma 2.2]. The randomized Exponential Time Hypothesis excludes randomized algorithms that decide 3-SAT in time 2 o(n) with error probability at most 1 /3 [8]. The reductions behind [7, Lemma 2.2] are deterministic, so it excludes such algorithms for three-coloring graphs of maximum degree four as well. A randomized (1 + ε)-approximation that succeeds with probability at least 2 /3 would give one: on a three-colorable input it returns, with probability at least 2 /3, an assignment of peak at most θ + 1 /2 and hence θ, no other input has such an assignment, and a returned assignment is checked by an endpoint sweep. All of this holds for every fixed r ≥ 2. 18
