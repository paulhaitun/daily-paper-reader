ABSTRACT
An embodied brain, or System 2, must turn an abstract goal into actions while dis-
covering relevant scene conditions and checking whether executed skills achieved
their intended effects.
Open-loop evaluations cannot test observations shaped
by an agent’s decisions, while some closed-loop evaluations provide limited
evidence during execution or assume idealized skill outcomes.
We introduce
SCOPER, a suite for studying observation-grounded planning, execution, and re-
covery. SCOPER-bench combines high-level goals, ongoing visual observations,
a shared rule-based System 1 for low-level actions, and controlled execution fail-
ures. Its construction and validation pipeline yields 870 tasks across BEHAV-
IOR and ProcTHOR, complemented by about 3,500 situated questions tied to
the evaluated agent’s execution. Benchmark results reveal difficulties in retaining
scene evidence, monitoring progress, and recovering from unexpected outcomes.
To address these difficulties, SCOPER-harness combines Evidence Memory and
Progress Verification with a dynamic task agenda and bounded exploration, with-
out additional model training. Across two evaluated open-source backbones and
two simulators, the harness improves task success by 11.1–28.6%; a harnessed
9B model outperforms a directly executed 27B model on both simulators. In an
offline next-action evaluation using observations from 200 real-robot demonstra-
tions, the harness also improves full-call accuracy by 14.7% and 15.3%. SCOPER
provides a setting for diagnosing and improving embodied reasoning throughout
a complete task execution cycle.

INTRODUCTION
An embodied agent must do more than turn a goal into a sequence of actions (Huang et al., 2022b;
Singh et al., 2022; Song et al., 2022). Its high-level controller, which we call the embodied brain or
System 2, must decide what information to seek, interpret visual observations (Driess et al., 2023;
Zeng et al., 2022), select skills (Mu et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2024), and determine whether those
skills achieved their intended effects (Guo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b). A low-level System 1
executes the selected skills, following the separation of language-based planning and skill execution
explored in prior embodied systems (Ahn et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2022c; Liang et al., 2022;
Huang et al., 2023). In a partially observed environment, exploration may reveal new requirements,
while an unsuccessful action may invalidate the current plan (Guo et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023b).
These demands are coupled: what the agent observes shapes what it should do (Wu et al., 2023b;a),
and what happens after it acts shapes what it should do next. Studying them together is essential
to understanding whether an embodied brain can guide long-horizon behavior through a complete
closed-loop task (Yenamandra et al., 2023).

loop alone therefore cannot establish conditions for studying embodied System 2.
We introduce the SCOPER suite for studying System 2 in closed-loop, observation-grounded plan-
ning, execution, and recovery, comprising SCOPER-bench and SCOPER-harness. SCOPER-
bench requires agents to infer subtasks from abstract goals and multi-frame visual observations
throughout execution; construction-time decompositions serve validation. A unified rule-based Sys-
tem 1 supplies shared execution infrastructure, and a scalable construction and validation pipeline
yields 870 tasks across two simulators (Section 3). Controlled failure injection tests whether agents
detect unmet expectations and replan to resume progress. Evaluations reveal persistent difficulties in
acquiring and maintaining scene evidence for planning, monitoring outcomes, and recovering from
failures.
SCOPER-harness addresses these difficulties through Evidence Memory, which maintains explicit
language descriptions of observed scene information, and Progress Verification, which checks ex-
pected outcomes against observations after each execution step. A dynamic agenda organizes pend-
ing subtasks and is updated using accumulated evidence and verification results. Because repeated
exploration and checking can delay execution, an anti-deadlock mechanism limits unproductive rep-
etition to balance information gathering with task advancement.
Experiments show that complete execution remains challenging even with shared low-level skills:
the strongest directly evaluated model achieves 50.5% task success on BEHAVIOR and 35.1% on
ProcTHOR. Across evaluated open-source models, SCOPER-harness consistently improves per-
formance, with gains of up to 28.6 percentage points, enabling a smaller model to perform com-
parably to the strongest evaluated model. Evaluated embodied-finetuned models also underper-
form general-purpose models, indicating that embodied specialization does not necessarily yield
stronger closed-loop reasoning in this setting. Tests with real-world observations further show that
benchmark-guided harness refinements improve performance, particularly in memory- dependent
and failure-recovery cases.
Our contributions are threefold: (1) SCOPER-bench: A validated cross-simulator benchmark
jointly testing observation-grounded task decomposition, execution, and failure recovery.

RELATED WORK
Open-loop Evaluation of Embodied Reasoning.
Recent benchmarks evaluate embodied under-
standing and planning from recorded egocentric observations by asking the models to answer some
open-loop questions. (Grauman et al., 2021; 2023; Cheng et al., 2023; Mangalam et al., 2023;
Yang et al., 2025a). EgoPlan-Bench2 (Qiu et al., 2024) tests planning in everyday scenarios, and
ECBench (Dang et al., 2025) examines diverse embodied cognitive abilities. These protocols probe
reasoning over visual evidence, but observations remain independent of the evaluated model’s de-
cisions. They therefore cannot establish whether an agent can acquire missing evidence through
action, revise task decomposition as execution unfolds, or recover from its own execution failures.
SCOPER-bench tests these demands through decision-dependent observations and outcome-guided
planning and recovery.
Closed-loop Embodied Benchmarks.
Interactive benchmarks (Shridhar et al., 2019; 2020; Pad-
makumar et al., 2021; Puig et al., 2018) increasingly evaluate embodied agents through simulated
task execution (Savva et al., 2019; Szot et al., 2021; Puig et al., 2023; Deitke et al., 2020; Mees et al.,
2021; Liu et al., 2023a). EmbodiedBench (Yang et al., 2025b) and EmbodiedEval (Cheng et al.,
2025) extend evaluation to visual navigation, interaction, and planning, but their standard stepwise
interfaces emphasize observations at action boundaries rather than evidence acquired within skill ex-
ecution. Moreover, action-validity feedback does not systematically test whether agents detect and
recover from deviations between intended and actual skill effects. BEHAVIOR (Srivastava et al.,
2021; Li et al., 2024; 2021) evaluates complex household activities through integrated planning and
control, making high-level reasoning difficult to disentangle from low-level execution in end-to-end
performance. SCOPER-bench isolates these System 2 demands using shared, controllable System 1
skills, multi-frame execution observations, explicit failure injection, and high-level goals.
Agentic designs for embodied intelligence.
Embodied frameworks combine planning, memory,
and execution feedback to sustain long-horizon behavior (Rana et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023a;
Yao et al., 2022; Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023b). Cortex (Peng et al., 2026) aligns VLM
planning with VLA execution through standardized subtasks and semantic memory; Thea (Wang
et al., 2026) integrates persistent scene context with action-outcome evaluation; Mimir (Xu et al.,
2026) grounds an evolving task agenda in world memory and explores under insufficient evidence.
SCOPER-harness emphasizes how scene discovery determines an abstract goal’s required work,
coupling exploration, evidence-driven agenda revision, and verified progress (Section 4).

SCOPER-BENCH
We test System 2 information acquisition, goal decomposition, subtask selection, and plan revision
from observations and outcomes. Instructions specify requirements, not action sequences; comple-
tion and situated question answering (QA) assess execution and scene/task understanding.
3.1
TASK FORMULATION AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Task groups.
We organize the benchmark into four groups. Routine execution specifies con-
crete task requirements while leaving action selection and sequencing to the agent. Observation-
dependent covers state-dependent execution, scene inspection, and responses to task updates. Fail-
ure recovery covers initially present anomalies, controlled execution failures, and mid-execution
disturbances that require corrective actions or replanning. Composite combines multiple subtasks,
requiring agents to maintain progress and reason over cross-step dependencies; these subtasks can
draw from the other three groups.

Task representation.
A task instance is represented as T = (x, R, C0, G, E, Q), where x is the
instruction, R specifies object-role constraints, C0 defines initial conditions, G contains goal predi-
cates, E configures optional execution events, and Q contains attached questions. This configuration
supports construction and evaluation. Evaluated System 2 uses instructions, visual observations, and
prescribed public tool information; construction-time role bindings, reference decompositions, and
privileged simulator state provide no planning guidance.
Agent inputs and evaluation protocol.
System 2 receives instructions, tool descriptions, permit-
ted vocabulary, decision history, and tool-returned images. Exploration and navigation return eight
uniformly sampled execution frames; state-changing actions return before-and-after frames. Direct
execution retains up to 32 frames; harness context routing appears in Section 4.1 and Appendix J.
Execution ends on stop or step-budget exhaustion. Success requires both stop and the result-
ing environment state satisfying the template’s goal predicates. Triggered questions use dedicated
answer-only turns with the same context available to policy decisions. Observation settings, budgets,
and scoring rules appear in Appendix A; QA activation and answering details appear in Appendix I.
3.2
ORACLE SYSTEM 1 AND CONTROLLED EXECUTION
Unified skill infrastructure.
Oracle System 1 provides rule-based active observation, navigation,
and manipulation skills shared by evaluated models and the construction-time teacher. Standardiz-
ing execution controls low-level variation without training a separate policy for each model. Com-
plete skill definitions and object binding are given in Appendices E and H. Navigation uses A*
search (Hart et al., 1968) over traversability maps derived from scene collision geometry. Manipula-
tion bypasses low-level physical execution, directly updating object poses, holding or containment
relations, and task-relevant states with synchronized visual representations. Navigation, scanning,
and target search collect multi-frame observations along paths and viewpoints; state-changing ac-
tions expose their visual outcomes for subsequent System 2 decisions.
Controlled failures and perturbations.
Template-defined events alter System 1 behavior or out-
comes at designated stages, requiring inspection of deviations, identification of unmet requirements,
and recovery or replanning. This combines shared execution infrastructure and controllable imper-
fect outcomes; backend implementations and failure mechanisms appear in Appendices E and F.

3.3
TASK CONSTRUCTION AND VALIDATION
Template-to-scene construction.
Figure 2 summarizes the shared construction pipeline in BE-
HAVIOR (Srivastava et al., 2021) and ProcTHOR (Deitke et al., 2022; Kolve et al., 2017), which
diversify scene styles and layouts. Using template constraints, the constructor matches scenes and
binds roles to objects, checking required objects and fixtures against interaction and spatial con-
straints, using scene profiles in ProcTHOR and scene–template matching with asset materialization
in BEHAVIOR. Initialization sets object placements, containment, agent pose, and task-relevant
states with synchronized visual representations. For dishware inspection, a dirty item is placed
where exploration is needed to discover it; the instruction specifies inspection and handling require-
ments without revealing the target. Rule-based subtask decomposition guides execution validation.
Configuration details appear in Appendix G.
Static and execution validation.
Static checks test role and condition consistency, initial-state
reconstruction, nontrivial goals, and reachability. A privileged teacher follows the reference decom-
position through Oracle System 1, checking goal satisfaction and configured events; failure-injection
instances must recover to completion with failures enabled. Traces and videos support inspection.
We construct 870 executable tasks from 35 base templates and their compositions (Appendix G).
Situated QA attachment.
About 3,500 questions from eight QA templates probe object states,
spatial relations, action outcomes, anomalies, new requirements, and task progress. Candidates
derived from task semantics are screened against teacher traces for valid activation and resolvable
answers. Evaluation triggers questions at configured actions, steps, or subtask milestones, with
answers determined from the evaluated episode’s corresponding state (Appendix I).

SCOPER-HARNESS
Prior embodied systems use environmental feedback and explicit memory to support plan-
ning (Huang et al., 2022c; Wang et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2022a; Jatavallabhula
et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2023). Our evaluations expose two difficulties in closed-loop task execution:
maintaining scene evidence to guide task decomposition and subsequent decisions, and detecting
unsuccessful execution to support recovery. We introduce SCOPER-harness, a training-free har-
ness that addresses these difficulties through Evidence Memory and Progress Verification. A
dynamic agenda connects accumulated evidence to task requirements, while anti-deadlock control
balances information gathering with task advancement. All model-based components share the same
fixed-parameter backbone through role-specific prompts and use no additional privileged simulator
information. Figure 3 summarizes the architecture.

4.1
EVIDENCE MEMORY AND ADAPTIVE TASK DECOMPOSITION
Structured evidence memory.
Partial observations require retaining evidence without treating
missing observations as state changes. We maintain Mt = (Ft, It, Ot): facts store object properties,
relations, and scene descriptions with evidence and validity annotations; incidents record execution
discrepancies and handling status; obligations track outstanding observation or action needs linked
to relevant objects and incidents.
Instruction-derived agenda.
The agenda At organizes required outcomes, ongoing constraints,
and conditional requirements, while memory obligations track observation or handling needs. Ini-
tialization uses only the instruction, retaining unresolved conditions as inspection needs without
assuming unseen states or fixing an action sequence. Items track status and supporting evidence.
Observation-driven memory updates.
Before decisions, discovery extracts task-relevant objects
and states from current and available execution-process images; reconciliation checks facts against
new evidence. Confirmation refreshes evidence, contradiction invalidates facts, and newer scene
descriptions can supersede older ones; invisibility alone does not negate a fact. A deterministic
commit layer checks references, evidence links, and consistency before committing model updates.
Evidence-grounded agenda revision.
Committed memory drives agenda revision: the model
combines the instruction, existing items, and new evidence to activate conditional requirements, add
necessary work within task scope, merge duplicates, and update or reopen affected items. Inspection
items become supported when the required information is obtained; execution items require verified
effects, while ongoing constraints remain monitored. Unresolved inspection needs guide observa-
tion actions toward relevant objects or conditions. For example, “inspect the kitchen and turn off
any running faucets” initializes inspection and conditional shutoff requirements. Observing running
water supports the corresponding state check and activates shutoff work, but does not establish that
the faucet has been turned off.
Context Routing and Action Selection.
Deterministic routing selects committed memory within
a bounded context, prioritizing relevant facts and unresolved needs while retaining evidence ref-
erences and validity annotations. The policy receives the routed memory f

4.2
PROGRESS VERIFICATION AND RECOVERY
Visual verification and recovery feedback.
Issuing an action does not establish progress. Nav-
igation and manipulation therefore receive outcome verification; exploration supplies memory ob-
servations without separate verification. Given the tool request, expected state and visual effects,
and immediate before-and-after images, the verifier returns success, failure, or ambiguity with vi-
sual evidence. Success commits supported effects and resolves satisfied needs; failure records dis-
crepancies and recovery obligations; ambiguity leaves outcomes unconfirmed. Memory adopts the
verifier’s effect assessment without readjudicating the same images; later observations may revise it.
In the faucet example, stopped flow supports completion; continued flow leaves shutoff unresolved
and triggers recovery. The original request, evidence, and recovery state guide further observation,
renewed approach, or retry. Verified subsequent effects update memory and the agenda, returning
local recovery to task planning.
4.3

Balancing exploration, execution, and recovery budgets.
Evidence maintenance and verifica-
tion can encourage excessive exploration or conservative checking. The controller tracks scans,
searches, and navigation by room/target, and observations and retries per incident. At exploration
limits, it restricts tools and requests reselection to promote approach and interaction. The agenda
guides planning; recovery and anti-deadlock rules constrain execution. Reselection and recovery

REAL-OBSERVATION EVALUATION
We test transfer of simulation findings through offline next-tool-call evaluation on 200 distinct
AgiBotWorld2026 (AgiBot, 2026) demonstration episodes: 80 normal-execution, 60 observation-
dependent, and 60 involving anomalous or incomplete actions and repair. At each node, direct
execution and SCOPER-harness predict the next tool call from the instruction, current semantic
action segment’s head-camera images, and preceding reference actions; predictions are not propa-
gated as executed actions. We report tool accuracy and full-call accuracy (correct tool and all re-
quired arguments), separately evaluating observation-critical and repair-decision nodes. This fixed-
history evaluation tests real-visual decisions, but not active observation acquisition, consequences of
agents’ own errors, or sustained multi-step recovery. It complements closed-loop simulation testing
task completion through continued interaction. Construction and scoring appear in Appendix D.
Consistent with simulation (Table 3), GPT-6 Astra leads overall full-call accuracy at 70.8%, while
embodied-specialized models show no consistent advantage. SCOPER-harness raises full-call ac-

by 28.4 points for both, benefiting real visual decisions, especially critical nodes.

CONCLUSION
We introduced the SCOPER suite for studying embodied System 2 through closed-loop,
observation-grounded planning and recovery.
SCOPER-bench combines high-level goals,
execution-time observations, and controlled failures in 870 tasks across two simulators. SCOPER-
harness connects accumulated scene evidence and verified action outcomes to an evolving task
agenda, improving performance across the evaluated models and on real-observation decision tests.
These findings highlight the importance of using observations to determine required work and revise
decisions throughout execution. Future work will extend the current skill interface toward richer,
more continuous action spaces and broader action coverage, and adapt the benchmark and harness
to more complex scenes with greater diversity, dynamics, and interaction demands.

A
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
Evaluation compares closed-loop execution through a shared skill interface and task-level scorer.
This section specifies episode interaction, available information, and aggregation; detailed QA defi-
nitions appear in Appendix I.
A.1
INTERACTION, OBSERVATIONS, AND INFORMATION ACCESS
Each episode loads a task, initializes the scene and event schedulers, and produces initial RGB
images. The model emits one skill request from its current decision context; Binding resolves pub-
lic references, System 1 executes admitted requests, and the interface supplies images and request
records for the next decision. A separate evaluator checks goals and events.
All evaluated methods receive public instructions, tool definitions, RGB images, and permitted re-
quest records. Simulator inventories and identifiers, role mappings, ground-truth states and poses,
distances, goal progress, injection labels, teacher trajectories, and reference decompositions remain
internal. Public vocabulary defines valid references rather than scene contents. Harness memory,
agenda, and visual assessments derive from public inputs. Execution-admission signals are used
by the deterministic controller only for gating and budget accounting; executor success flags and
diagnostic records are excluded from model inputs.
Navigation, scanning, and target search each produce up to eight process-observation frames, retain-
ing the current view; ordinary manipulation supplies before-and-after RGB images. These obser-
vations enter a visual history maintained across actions. Direct evaluation models receive the latest
available frames, up to 32, together with public action-request history at each decision. The harness
uniformly samples up to eight frames from navigation, scanning, and search for observation mainte-
nance; its policy uses up to the latest four frames, public request history, and routed memory. Since
memory retains evidence from earlier observations, the policy uses fewer recent frames than direct
evaluation. When fewer frames are available, all available frames within the respective limit are
used. Instructions, public vocabulary, and tool definitions remain accessible throughout execution.
The harness uses Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.8-27B; policy, verification, memory, and agenda calls
within an experiment use the same fixed-parameter base model. Additional model calls contribute
computational cost rather than additional environmental observations; acquiring a different view-
point or position requires a public tool request and consumes the corresponding interaction budget.
Direct-model evaluation uses an input image resolution of 512×512; GPT-6 uses the max reasoning-
effort setting.

A.2
EVALUATION PROMPTS AND PER-DECISION CONTEXT
The fixed system prompt assigns the model the role of an embodied agent completing the task with
the listed skills. It supplies the instruction, tool names and argument definitions, public reference
rules, and general action prerequisites. Instructions specify objectives, scope, and conditional re-
quirements. Shared guidance covers approaching objects before interaction, assessing effects from
images, observing again when needed, and stopping after all task clauses are satisfied; the model
chooses the task-specific action sequence.
Each user turn contains the current step, remaining tool budget, the previous requested skill and
public arguments, chronologically ordered action-request history, and the current image input. The
request-history limit follows the episode’s high-level budget, capped at 48 entries, allowing preced-
ing requests to be retained within the prescribed 24-, 36-, or 48-step episodes. Each entry states what
was requested without an action-success label. Full user–assistant conversations are not replayed by
default, and old images are not repeatedly embedded in the history text; previously generated rea-
soning is not retained as permanent environmental evidence.
The model outputs one tool name and its arguments, optionally accompanied by a short rationale.
The interface parses the request before Binding and execution. The next-turn summary repeats the
requested call and states that it has finished; the model assesses its outcome from images.
The following layout distinguishes fixed interaction rules from turn-specific content. Images are at-
tached chronologically as image inputs; dynamic content comes from the public task and interaction
records. New task messages enter subsequent task context, whereas QA messages use a dedicated
answer format.
System: Complete the task using the available tools. Task: [instruction and public task
updates]. Tools: [names and argument definitions]. Follow the public reference rules and
interaction prerequisites. Use images to assess outcomes; a requested action does not es-
tablish success. Return one tool call with arguments.
Decision turn: Step: [current step]. Remaining budget: [remaining calls]. Previous re-
quests: [public action history]. Last request: [tool and arguments; call finished]. Visual
input: [ordered frames]. Choose the next call.
QA turn: Question: [identifier, text, and choices]. Use the permitted current context and
return the question identifier and selected answer value.
When a situated question is posted, a separate answer call supplies its public identifier, text, choices,
and the visual and execution context permitted at that time; the model responds with the question
identifier and selected answer value. Newly introduced task requirements are supplied as public
natural-language messages and distinguished from questions requesting only an answer. After stop-
ping, the prompt specifies an answer-only phase without further physical actions; goal checking and
answer correctness remain evaluator-side operations.
A.3
BUDGETS AND TERMINATION
Atomic, two-subtask, and three-subtask episodes use high-level call budgets of 24, 36, and 48,
respectively, shared across compared methods. Observation, manipulation, answering, and stopping
are counted under a common protocol; appended QA answers also consume this budget. Internal
verification, memory maintenance, and action reselection calls are recorded separately, enabling
separate comparisons of environment-interaction efficiency and model-call cost.
Under the standard stop-and-QA finishing protocol, stop ends physical execution and freezes its
task score. Pending questions are handled in an answer-only finishing phase using the remaining
budget, without resuming physical actions. Physical completion and subsequent answers can there-
fore be scored separately.
Task completion is determined from environment state at scoring time and applicable process condi-
tions, rather than a model’s completion claim or earlier goal attainment. Let Gi denote an episode’s
goal predicates. We set si = 1 exactly when all predicates hold and the task’s configured stopping
requirement is satisfied. A stop call freezes the goal score; if the budget expires without stopping,
the last available state is evaluated and any unmet stopping requirement remains unsatisfied. Budget

REAL-ROBOT NEXT-TOOL-CALL EVALUATION
D.1
DATA AND DECISION-NODE CONSTRUCTION
The evaluation uses real-robot demonstrations from AgiBotWorld2026, excluding simulation data.
The 200 distinct source episodes form three mutually exclusive groups: 80 normal-execution, 60
observation-dependent, and 60 involving anomalous or incomplete actions and their repair. Each
sample covers a contiguous task scope with a defined objective and termination condition within
one source video; videos are not spliced together. An episode can yield multiple decision nodes, so
episode counts differ from scored-decision counts.
Videos are segmented into semantic actions such as navigation, picking, placing, and opening or
closing containers. Local camera, torso, and gripper adjustments are generally absorbed into their
semantic segment, whereas necessary base approaches are represented as navigation. For reference
calls (a1, . . . , aK), the node after segment i receives the reference prefix through ai and targets ai+1;
the final segment is followed by finish(). Scoring begins after the first segment and does not
predict the initial action. Every node uses reference history rather than predictions from preceding
nodes.
21


Preprint
D.2
OBSERVATIONS AND CHALLENGE CATEGORIES
Visual inputs use the head-camera stream observation.images.top head, sampled at 0.5
FPS on a fixed temporal grid anchored at the start of the selected task scope. Each node receives
only grid frames within the current segment and no later than its endpoint, without accumulating raw
images from earlier segments. Textual action history retains the complete reference prefix. Future
frames, manually selected keyframes, bounding-box annotations, success or failure labels, category
labels, and reference answers are withheld.
English instructions are reconstructed from source annotations and videos, specifying execution or-
der where necessary. Observation-dependent instructions describe inspection requirements without
revealing the object or state to discover; repair instructions do not identify a failed action. Each
observation-dependent episode includes at least one node whose next call requires current visual
evidence. Nodes requiring future images to resolve ambiguity are excluded from scoring.
Recovery samples include naturally occurring anomalies with verifiable subsequent recovery and
incomplete-action nodes constructed by splitting an original action before completion. For exam-
ple, a pickup segment can be divided into an unfinished pickup and its continuation, followed by
placement. The latter preserves the original images but does not imply an actual grasp failure or
controller retry. The two sources are labeled separately, and decisions that resume the task after
repair are evaluated.
D.3
HARNESS STATE ALONG REFERENCE EXECUTION
The harness maintains memory and the agenda along the reference execution prefix. The initial
agenda is derived from the instruction, and state is updated using images and reference calls provided
up to the current node. Raw image input still covers only the current segment; earlier observations
can persist through memory. Predicted calls are not executed, do not enter reference history, and
do not authorize committing expected effects. Subsequent updates follow the reference video and
actions without future frames or answer labels.
D.4
OUTPUT INTERFACE AND METRICS
Each node freely generates one structured call without candidate options or a complete fu-
ture plan. Tools include navigate to, pick, place, open/close, turn on/turn off,
soak under, and finish, with data-dependent extensions fixed before evaluation. Object ar-
guments are matched at category level using a frozen alias mapping; instance identifiers, colors,
coordinates, and hand selection are not evaluated. Placement specifies the destination without re-
peating the held object’s category. The interface contains no inspect tool.
Tool Accuracy measures the fraction of nodes with the correct tool name. The primary metric, Call
Accuracy, requires the tool and all necessary arguments to match one complete reference. Argument
errors invalidate the call but not a correct tool name; unparseable outputs or unknown tools fail both
metrics. The argument-free finish() receives identical judgments. Equivalent references must
be matched as complete calls rather than by combining their components.
Category and overall results are aggregated over decision nodes; overall accuracy divides total cor-
rect predictions by all scorable nodes. Observation-critical Call Accuracy and Repair-decision Call
Accuracy use their respective subsets without adding them again to the overall denominator. Post-
repair continuation nodes contribute to the repair category. Because predictions do not control the
robot or subsequent observations, this evaluation reports no task success, goal progress, action cov-
erage, or average execution steps.

E.3
NAVIGATION AND OBSERVATION IMPLEMENTATION
Navigation constructs its search space from scene collision and reachability information and uses A*
to reach an interaction pose near the target. ProcTHOR searches a grid of reachable positions and
executes movement and rotation along the path. BEHAVIOR searches an occupancy grid accounting
for the agent footprint and validates connectivity and path feasibility; its eight-connected search
disallows diagonal corner cutting through blocked cells.
Navigation, scanning, and target search provide images during execution through backend obser-
vation callbacks. Scanning covers surrounding views, while target search organizes target-directed
observation. The evaluation interface selects and delivers observations to the model or harness,
connecting scene discovery to action selection throughout execution.
E.4
MANIPULATION AND STATE SYNCHRONIZATION
Manipulation controls poses, attachment relations, and object properties through rules, bypassing
low-level grasp and contact dynamics. ProcTHOR’s controlled pickup and placement maintain vi-
sual attachment and containment relations alongside simulator state operations. BEHAVIOR’s sym-
bolic executor updates held states, spatial relations, and properties and synchronizes these changes
with rendered objects and joints. Both retain object-affordance, access, and interaction precondi-
tions.
State processing follows the semantics of the corresponding operation. For example, ProcTHOR
implements cleaning through sink and faucet interactions and cooling or heating through container
and device operations. BEHAVIOR uses corresponding state operations and execution wrappers for

Manipulation skills use controlled state transitions that bypass low-level motion and contact simula-
tion while retaining immediate pre- and post-action RGB observations for outcome checking. Nav-

The teacher policy uses task specifications and environment truth to organize skill calls into refer-

scene issues to be discovered, recovery strategies address execution deviations, and composite strate-
gies coordinate subtasks. Reference trajectories support feasibility, goal, and event validation and
provide execution context for QA attachment. Evaluated models execute through the same System 1

F
CONTROLLED FAILURE INJECTION AND SCENE DISTURBANCES
Controlled interventions turn routine skill execution into closed-loop tasks requiring monitoring and
recovery. We distinguish execution failures that alter the current skill’s outcome from scene distur-
bances that change the environment after an execution milestone. Both are specified by activation
conditions and state transformations in the task configuration, allowing the same task goal to be
paired with different recovery challenges.
F.1
ACTIVATION AND SCHEDULING
We represent an event as e = (τ, r, h, ϕ, n), where τ specifies an eligible skill or milestone, r
identifies the target role, h defines activation conditions, ϕ specifies the state intervention, and n
bounds the number of firings. Conditions can depend on a particular action result, elapsed action
steps, containment or non-held states, and the owning subtask being active. Configurations also
support probabilities and cooldown intervals to control intervention frequency.
Execution failures intercept a matching skill call or modify its outcome, whereas scene disturbances
check conditions and apply changes after the relevant execution milestone. The scheduler maintains
event histories and firing counts and scopes events to their owning subtask. Eligibility and whether
the intended state change was actually applied are recorded separately for task validation.

STATE-CHANGING DISTURBANCES
Scene disturbances can reclose or reopen containers, toggle devices, re-dirty objects, and relocate or

goals.
Both backends implement disturbances through scene-state changes and synchronize the visual rep-
resentation of object poses, joints, or properties. Task validation checks final goals together with
required interventions, ensuring that reference trajectories encounter the intended challenge. The
evaluator retains private event records, while models assess consequences from protocol-provided
observations and public interaction information.
G
TASK TEMPLATES AND CONSTRUCTION
Templates translate capability requirements into instantiable object, state, and event constraints. We
organize tasks into Routine Execution, Observation-dependent, Failure Recovery, and Composite.
Below we describe shared template fields, instantiation, and representative instances. Tables below
summarize each benchmark template through its roles, initialization requirements, and goal predi-
cates.
G.1
DATASET STATISTICS AND SCENE SAMPLING
The construction target is ten task instances per base template in each simulator, together with 50
two-child and 50 three-child composite tasks per simulator. The 35 base templates thus yield a target
of 450 tasks per backend. Limited matches across diverse BEHAVIOR scenes lead to the exclusion
of 30 instances, leaving 420 BEHAVIOR and 450 ProcTHOR tasks, or 870 tasks in total. ProcTHOR
contains 130 routine-execution, 90 observation-dependent, 130 failure-recovery, and 100 composite
episodes. BEHAVIOR contains 100, 90, 130, and 100 episodes in the corresponding groups, with
the 30 excluded instances belonging to routine execution. ProcTHOR scenes are randomly sampled
from its official pool of 12,000 scenes. For BEHAVIOR, filtering out excessively large scenes leaves
a pool of 35 distinct scenes for task matching; task objects are randomly added under the template’s
compatibility constraints.
G.2
TEMPLATE SPECIFICATION AND INSTANTIATION
Each template specifies object roles, categories or category groups, affordance requirements, and re-
lations between roles. Initial conditions describe open/closed, cleanliness, containment, or location
states, and goal predicates define the desired outcome. Instruction patterns replace roles with pub-
lic object descriptions; event configurations define challenge triggers and consequences; composite
tasks additionally retain subtask identities and scopes.
Scene matching first filters candidates by object type and affordance, then checks same-room, cross-
room, distinctness, and spatial constraints. ProcTHOR profiles provide object and scene infor-
mation. The BEHAVIOR matcher can reuse the shared JSON templates and distinguishes direct
matches, matches requiring spawned assets, and matches requiring runtime validation. Bound tasks
satisfy initial conditions through permitted relocation, state configuration, or asset materialization.

Preprint
Initialization applies these configurations to concrete objects and synchronizes rendered state. Val-
idation checks initial predicates, geometry and support relations, navigation and sequential interac-
tion reachability, and whether all goals already hold. Teacher rollouts then check final goals and
required events. Scenes unable to support a template retain explicit rejection reasons.
Instructions specify task objectives, scope, and handling rules without exposing construction-time
instance bindings or step-by-step procedures. In observation-conditioned tasks, the model identifies
the concrete objects requiring attention and their current states from scene observations. Public
category terms express selection criteria rather than enumerate qualifying scene instances. The
model consequently determines action targets, necessary prerequisites, and subsequent operations.
For tasks with universal inspection requirements, construction ensures that all actionable issues
within the instruction scope are represented in template bindings and scoring goals; other relevant
objects are initialized in compliant states. Instructions such as switching everything off or closing
all containers therefore introduce no additional initial issues outside the scoring scope.
For example, clean-dishware storage requires dishware, storage, and distinguishable dirty-dishware

dishware from storage, without identifying which concrete instances belong to either group or pre-
scribing a pickup-and-placement sequence. Final goals jointly require target storage, exclusion of
distractors from storage, and container closure where applicable.
G.3
REPRESENTATIVE TEMPLATES
Routine execution includes simple placement and preparation before storage. Simple placement
transfers a pickupable object to a destination; preparation requires cleaning, cutting, heating, or
cooling before the placement goal. Multi-stage preparation combines these requirements into an
object workflow.
The dirty-dish workflow template includes dishware, sink, faucet, and storage roles and can include
a staging surface. The dish is initialized dirty and outside storage, and the goal requires it to be clean
and stored. The task connects identifying issues in the scene to subsequent cleaning and storage,
making observations determine which objects and operations require attention.
Safety inspection initializes a running faucet and an open container in one room and requires both
to be closed or switched off. Other observation templates include cross-room inspection, mixed
issues, and category-dependent storage. Latent issue records specify the actual initial conditions
and required handling, while the model identifies actionable conditions from visual observations.
The dropped-placement template binds a pickupable object and destination, with a goal of establish-
ing the correct placement relation. A one-shot placement event releases the object beside the target,
requiring recovery through approach, pickup, and return to the destination. Partial opening or clos-
ing, grasp slips, and incorrect navigation alter other execution effects, requiring different recovery
procedures toward the same final goal.
Composite tasks contain two or three subtasks and retain each child’s roles, goals, events, and iden-
tity. Runtime scheduling scopes events to their owning subtask and advances the active child at
handoff. Handoffs are managed by the evaluation process rather than requested by the model. QA
can cover different children and handoff progress; overall and per-subtask success are recorded sep-
arately.

G.5
PROGRAMMATIC INSTANTIATION AND COMPOSITION
The five observation-conditioned inspection and workflow templates instantiate scene issues through
programmatic recipes. Cross-room inspection configures one faucet or container issue in each of
two rooms; safety inspection configures faucet and container issues in one room; mixed inspection
configures faucet, container, and dirty-dish issues in one room. The dirty-dish workflow recipe
configures one dish issue, and the storage-policy recipe configures one misplaced object. Each issue
expands into concrete roles, initial states, and handling goals.
The general long-horizon constructor combines two or three subtasks while retaining their roles,
events, and goal ownership. Compatible object and state requirements allow the children to share an
episode while preserving their individual task specifications. The overall goal collects the required
child outcomes, while events and QA are scheduled within child scopes.

H
BINDING PROCEDURE
Binding connects action requests expressed in public vocabulary to instance-level System 1 exe-
cution. It comprises construction-time role binding and runtime reference resolution: the former
determines the task’s concrete scene instances, while the latter converts the model’s selected skill
and object reference into an executable request. Both backends use shared resolution semantics and
maintain simulator-instance mappings inside their adapters.
H.1
CONSTRUCTION-TIME BINDING AND PUBLIC REFERENCES
During construction, template roles are bound to scene objects according to category, affordance,
and spatial constraints. The internal mapping supports initialization, event scheduling, goal scoring,
and runtime instance resolution. The model uses instructions and observations to select public object
categories and operations, which the infrastructure resolves to concrete instances.
Tool instructions require object arguments to use a complete category name or an explicitly listed
alias from the public vocabulary; room navigation uses public room labels from the instruction.
Private roles, raw object identifiers, and invented color, position, or state modifiers are not valid
references. Broad task concepts such as dishware or storage must be translated through observation
into concrete categories supported by the interface.
H.2
RUNTIME RESOLUTION AND INTERACTION READINESS
At runtime, the resolver normalizes the public reference and filters candidates according to
reference-admission rules. Resolution first considers compatible recent interaction focus and task-
designated instances, followed by public aliases, activated observation or memory candidates, and
permitted scene or room references. Internal task mappings can therefore participate in instance se-
lection among objects of the same category: a task- or subtask-designated object can take precedence
over background objects. The model selects the public object category and skill, and the infrastruc-
ture resolves the instance. Ambiguity that the applicable rules cannot resolve remains unresolved
rather than selecting an arbitrary object.
Reference admission and interaction readiness are checked separately. By default, a model may
attempt a public concrete category before seeing the object; candidates requiring observation evi-

existence, localization, nor readiness for manipulation. Interaction additionally requires approach-
ing the target and satisfying affordance and access preconditions. After an object moves or drops,
preserving its internal identity does not establish that the previous interaction pose remains valid.
For example, an instruction publicly specifies a drawer while other drawers are present in the scene.
When the model requests navigation to drawer, the resolver can identify the corresponding drawer

but must still select the opening operation and determine from returned images whether the drawer
opened sufficiently. Likewise, when an instruction requires handling only dirty dishware, category
resolution does not tell the model whether the dishware is dirty or replace its observation-based
decision to wash it.
H.3
EXECUTION RESULTS AND THE MODEL INFORMATION BOUNDARY
After reference resolution, the executor checks interaction preconditions and executes admitted
skills. Unresolved or rejected requests retain their status for internal accounting. The model-facing
summary contains the requested skill and public arguments and states that the call has finished;
images provide evidence of the outcome. Binding diagnostics and instance mappings follow the
information-access rules in Appendix A.
System 1 provides instance-level execution support; System 2 selects actions, interprets scene ob-
servations, and assesses outcomes under the shared evaluation protocol.
I
SITUATED QA
Situated QA turns understanding of execution state into explicit questions and scores. Questions are
generated from template semantics and reference traces, while their actual answers are resolved in
the evaluated episode. We describe question types, activation, and scoring below.
I.1
QUESTION TYPES AND CONSTRUCTION
The eight QA templates in Table 13 cover object states and spatial relations, action outcomes, active
issues, new obligations, remaining requirements, and composite progress. For example, “Is the bowl
now in the cabinet?” probes a relation, “What was the outcome of the placement?” probes an effect,
and “Which requirements remain incomplete?” probes progress. Answers use the discrete choices
associated with each question type.
All eight question types are generated in a unified pipeline from task semantics, goal predicates, and
reference execution traces. Candidate questions are associated with public actions, task milestones,
or other configured activation conditions and checked for uniquely resolvable answers. Selection
considers diversity of question and action types and subtask coverage in composite tasks, with up to
six questions per atomic episode. Scheduler replay validates activation before the selected questions
are stored in a separate QA specification.
Questions use public task vocabulary without exposing private instance bindings or answer keys.
Action-anchored activation matches the tool and object role, avoiding step-offset mismatches intro-
duced by intervening answers. Answer keys are resolved from the actual evaluated episode under
the snapshot rules below, rather than copied from reference traces. All eight QA types use a single
selected answer from two to four choices.
The three progress-related templates use different scopes. Remaining-requirement questions ask
which listed requirements remain unmet at a stopping milestone, for example, whether cleaning or
storage remains incomplete. Mid-execution progress questions ask whether one or two specified
requirements hold at an intermediate point, such as whether a bowl is clean and stored, with choices
such as both, only the first, only the second, or neither. Composite-progress questions ask which
subtask is active after a handoff, with choices identifying a child or indicating that all children are
complete.

Each posted question is answered by the same task-executing model in a separate answer-only call.
This call uses the currently available public execution context and performs no additional explo-

separately from physical task completion.
I.2
ACTIVATION, QUEUING, AND ANSWER TIME
Activation types include a specified tool result, a task-action step threshold, subtask handoff, a new
human instruction, and a stop milestone. Each question fires at most once; child-specific questions
activate only within their owning subtask. Multiple eligible questions enter a queue, and the next
question is posted only when no previous question remains pending.
Action-outcome questions snapshot the evaluator state of the triggering action; delayed questions
identify the original action step. State, relation, and intermediate-progress questions resolve and
freeze their answer when posted, so later actions do not change their scoring reference. Resolu-
tion failures are recorded separately. Public questions contain the question, choices, and identifier;
answer keys remain with the evaluator.
I.3
SCORING
Situated QA uses the trigger rate and triggered accuracy in Appendix A.4: valid posted questions
divided by valid configured questions, and correct answers divided by valid posted questions. Posted
but unanswered questions count as incorrect; unposted questions are excluded from accuracy, and
queued questions are not counted as posted.
Correctness uses exact matching between normalized responses and evaluator-held answer keys.
Questions with unresolved answer keys are excluded as invalid and reported separately; missing or
unparseable responses to valid posted questions remain incorrect. The main tables report situated
QA. Because execution paths may trigger different question sets, accuracy is interpreted together
with trigger rate.
J
HARNESS DETAILS
SCOPER uses role-specific calls to the same fixed-parameter model for observation interpretation,
agenda revision, action selection, and effect verification. Deterministic code handles structural and
reference checks, memory routing, and bounded execution control. The following specification uses
the same state definitions and information flow as the main text.
J.1
COMPONENT INPUTS AND OUTPUTS
Initialization produces an agenda from the task instruction without images. Observation calls receive
the instruction, memory to maintain, and available images, and propose evidence-linked updates.
Agenda revision receives the instruction, existing items, and committed memory, and proposes ad-
ditions and modifications. The policy receives the instruction, the latest four frames, up to the latest
48 public tool-request records, routed memory, the agenda, allowed tools and argument definitions,
35


Preprint
and recovery and anti-deadlock context, and outputs one structured tool call. Procedural guidance is
supplied through item-level next intents and public tool descriptions.
Process observations from scan, navigation, and look-for calls are uniformly sampled into at most
eight frames for observation maintenance; the policy uses the latest four available frames. Verifi-
cation uses the existing immediate pre- and post-action frames, the request, and expected effects,
without implicitly moving the camera. When QA is triggered, the same model answers in a dedi-
cated turn using the question and the policy context, including the current instruction, images, public
request history, agenda, routed memory, and control context; it outputs only the answer.
J.2
AGENDA REPRESENTATION AND UPDATES
Each agenda item stores a stable identifier, requirement, mode, status, evidence references, and
optional next intent. Modes are required, ongoing, or conditional; statuses are pending,
active, watching, supported, or blocked, indicating outstanding work, current focus,
awaiting relevant evidence, evidence satisfying the current criterion, or a missing prerequisite. The
agenda contains at most eight items with at most one active item; item order does not prescribe
execution order.
Supported status is interpreted according to the requirement. A state inspection is supported when
the requested information is observed; an execution requirement is supported when visual verifica-
tion establishes its intended effect; an ongoing constraint is supported only for the current state and
remains monitored. Discovering that a condition holds activates the associated work without estab-
lishing its fulfillment. Request history and temporary planning assumptions cannot independently
support completion. Later observations contradicting supporting evidence reopen affected items.
Changes in public task requirements or relevant committed memory trigger revision rather than
rewriting the agenda at every call. Prompts ask the model to propose task-scoped additions, nec-
essary prerequisites, recovery needs, and duplicate merging; the commit layer checks fields, identi-
fiers, references, and evidence eligibility for supported status. The model judges task relevance and
semantic duplication; deterministic validation enforces the record structure and evidence require-
ments. The agenda advises the policy without directly defining action admissibility.
Initialization establishes explicit requirements and conditional inspection needs from the instruction.
Unresolved inspection items and next intents guide the policy’s choice of observation tools and
targets. New public instructions are incorporated into task context; either instruction changes or
relevant memory changes trigger agenda revision. QA questions are not treated as additional task
requirements.
For a task to inspect a kitchen and turn off running faucets, initialization establishes an inspection
need and a conditional shutoff requirement. Observing running water creates a scene fact and acti-
vates shutoff work without creating a failure incident. The shutoff requirement becomes supported
only when post-action visual evidence establishes that flow has stopped. Continued flow instead
records an execution incident and preserves outstanding work. Discovering one faucet does not
automatically complete an inspection covering the entire kitchen.
J.3
MEMORY MAINTENANCE AND ROUTING
Facts store an identifier, public subject, property or relation, value, confidence, frame and action ref-
erences, observation step, and validity status: current, stale, or contradicted. These fields
inform the model’s semantic judgments, while deterministic routines enforce record consistency and
manage memory storage and routing. Incidents store an identifier, source action, discrepancy type,
evidence, creation and update steps, handling status, and recovery attempts. Obligations store an
identifier, observe or act type, relevant public objects, optional source incident, creation step,
and open or resolved status.
Pre-decision observation maintenance distinguishes discovery from reconciliation. Discovery adds
visible objects and conditions as scene-observation facts without independently creating incidents
or obligations. Reconciliation assigns candidate facts a confirmed, contradicted, unobservable, or
unknown assessment. Confirmation refreshes supporting evidence; contradiction marks a fact as
contradicted; unobservable and unknown assessments alone do not authorize negation. A
36


Preprint
newer scene description of the same subject can supersede an earlier one. Proposals reference the
relevant facts and images; the commit layer checks memory revision, reference existence, evidence
eligibility, and consistency between assessments and mutations before commitment.
Default capacity is 64 facts, 16 incidents, and 16 obligations; routed context contains at most six
facts, two incidents, and three obligations. Fact ordering first prioritizes currently relevant objects,
then penalizes scene observations unrelated to the instruction, and then favors recent observations
with stable identifiers breaking ties. Currently relevant objects come from unresolved needs and
recent public interaction targets. Incidents prioritize unresolved entries and newer creation times;
obligations prioritize unresolved entries and older creation times. Routing preserves validity and
evidence references to distinguish historical records from current evidence.
Capacity maintenance preferentially removes stale facts and resolved records; unresolved incidents
and obligations cannot be treated as handled through silent deletion. Post-action effect commit-
ment complements pre-decision observation maintenance: the former records verification outcomes
and corresponding recovery needs, while the latter handles additions and contradictions from later
observations without readjudicating the same before/after evidence.
J.4
VERIFICATION, RECOVERY, AND PLANNING ASSUMPTIONS
Requests record selected actions, admission determines whether they may execute, and visual verifi-
cation assesses their effects. The evaluator separately determines task success under Appendix A.4;
control decisions and budget exhaustion do not establish success.
Verification success indicates visual support for the intended effect, failure indicates an observable
discrepancy, and ambiguity indicates insufficient evidence. Outputs include evidence and observ-
ability assessments; occlusion or a lack of obvious image change does not automatically establish
failure. Success commits only supported effects; failure creates or updates incidents and associated
obligations; ambiguity preserves unconfirmed effects and requests observation when needed.
Recovery state contains the original request, target, expected effect, evidence, handling phase, and
remaining attempts. The controller can require observation, approach, prerequisite satisfaction, or
retry. For placement recovery, held, released, and unknown respectively call for returning
toward the destination, localization and pickup, or further observation first. Effects of recovery calls
remain subject to verification.
If uncertainty persists after bounded observation or retry, recovery branches that permit continuation
may retain temporary planning assumptions. These reside in recovery or control context, cannot be
committed as verified facts, and cannot support agenda completion. An explicitly observed failure
cannot be assumed successful; subsequent evidence may revise or revoke an assumption. Terminal
branches end execution with unresolved state retained.
J.5
ANTI-DEADLOCK CONTROL AND EXECUTION ORDER
The exploration ledger groups scans, searches, and navigation by room or semantic target and tracks
observation and preparation calls without advancing interaction. At the corresponding limits, the
controller rejects repetitive candidates before execution, supplies a reselection reason, and restricts
tools, promoting approach and then interaction where appropriate. Relevant new evidence or ad-
mitted physical calls reset the corresponding counters under the control rules; precondition-rejected
calls and changes in timestamps or step indices do not.
Recovery observations, retries of the original action, and model reselection each have finite bud-
gets. Controller-required recovery calls take precedence over ordinary action selection, with the
agenda serving as policy context. QA uses dedicated answer turns outside exploration candidate
selection. Execution, answer, and stop requests each consume a high-level step; internal model calls
and reselection do not add environment steps.
Default control limits.
Within a no-progress phase, the default limits are two scans, two searches
and two navigation calls per semantic target, four observation calls in total, and six observa-
tion/navigation preparation calls in total. A rejected candidate permits at most two additional model
reselection requests. Recovery contexts default to two retry rounds, while dedicated placement re-

ledgers and do not extend the episode’s environment-step budget.
Exhausted dedicated placement recovery terminates with a control failure.

branches that permit continuation retain unconfirmed state and separately marked planning assump-
tions. Later evidence revises these assumptions.

HARNESS MODEL-CALL OVERHEAD
Direct execution uses one model call per high-level step. SCOPER adds role-specific calls around
action selection: the policy usually makes one call per step; discovery makes one call when new
images are available; reconciliation makes one call when existing facts can be checked; agenda re-
vision makes one call when the instruction or committed memory changes; and the verifier makes
one call after an admitted operation requiring outcome verification. These calls support action se-
lection, acquisition of new scene facts, assessment of earlier evidence, task-progress updates, and
visual outcome checks, respectively. Averaged over evaluation on the complete 870-task bench-
mark, SCOPER uses approximately 3.7× as many model calls as direct execution. Internal calls do
not add environment-interaction steps; both methods retain the same high-level step budgets.
