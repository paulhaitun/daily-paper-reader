# Hours-of-service-aware siting of charging and battery-swapping stations for long-haul electric trucks under adoption uncertainty 

Elnaz Irannezhad* a, Jia Guo a

> a

Research Centre for Integrated Transport Innovations (rCITI), School of Civil and Environmental Engineering, University of New South Wales, Sydney, New South Wales, Australia, 2052 

* Corresponding author’s email:  e.irannezhad@unsw.edu.au 

## Abstract 

Planning en-route charging and battery-swapping infrastructure for long-haul battery-electric trucks (BETs) requires models that reflect how trucks actually operate. This paper develops a mixed-integer programming framework that jointly sites charging or swapping stations and schedules each truck's charging, swapping and mandatory driver rest, so that charging time overlaps with regulated rest instead of being added to it. Energy use is derived segment by segment from road terrain with a tractive-force model, and the truck battery is modelled as a set of independently swappable packs. Staged investment under uncertain BET adoption is formulated as a multistage stochastic program with Markovian demand and solved by stochastic dual dynamic integer programming (SDDiP) with Lagrangian cuts; we show that the Lagrangian multipliers can be bounded by each station's annualised cost without weakening the cuts. Applied to twelve freight corridors on Australia's East Coast, the algorithm jointly optimises charging and battery swapping events as well as mandatory break events. A ±30% change in adoption alters the final charge-only network 

by only −12% to +13% of stations, with over 95% of stations built by the second stage. Hedging against uncertainty mainly changes which sites are chosen (71% overlap with a deterministic rolling-horizon model), not when they are built. 

Keywords: Battery-electric trucks; Charging infrastructure; Hours of service; Multistage stochastic programming; Stochastic dual dynamic integer programming; Driver rest regulations 2

# 1 Introduction 

Battery electric trucks (BETs) are widely regarded as a key pathway to decarbonising road freight, and attention is shifting from urban and regional operations to long-haul corridors (Balke et al., 2024). Their operational feasibility depends not only on battery capacity but also on how charging events are represented, how limited station capacity is allocated and how charging interacts with vehicle schedules. Unlike diesel refuelling, battery replenishment may require substantial dwell time and is affected by the nonlinear charging profile, available infrastructure and uncertain demand. Infrastructure planning must therefore connect spatial deployment decisions with operational constraints, including delivery schedules and mandatory driver rest periods. 

Return-to-base operations, in which trucks complete short routes and recharge at a depot, are a plausible early deployment pathway. Long-haul operations require a different planning problem because en-route charging or battery swapping must be coordinated with route geometry, energy use, station capacity and driver regulations. A useful planning model should therefore identify infrastructure that is feasible for representative freight movements while making its assumptions about demand, technology and regulation explicit. 

Distance-based station placement alone does not guarantee an economically or operationally feasible network. Existing heavy-vehicle rest areas, passenger electric vehicle (EV) charging sites and diesel refuelling locations may offer different combinations of land, access, amenities and electrical infrastructure. Co-locating replenishment facilities with rest areas may reduce the incremental delay associated with charging when the charging event can overlap with a required break. This is particularly advantageous, as it aligns charging dwell time with regulated driver rest periods, thereby minimising productivity impacts. Where feasible, expansion of existing EV charging infrastructure may also reduce marginal upgrade costs, though dedicated high-capacity connections or on-site energy management solutions for heavy vehicles may still be required. Finally, existing diesel refuelling sites along major freight corridors present strong candidates for early-stage retrofitting due to their established heavy-vehicle access, strategic locations, and supporting amenities; however, the significantly higher power requirements of heavy-duty charging necessitate careful assessment of electrical capacity, civil works, and site layout constraints. Importantly, infrastructure planning should adopt a network-level optimisation framework accounting for truck charging demands and prioritising strategically located stations at freight corridor intersections to maximise utilisation and reduce redundant capital expenditure (CAPEX) investments. Capacity and station siting design must be informed by freight volume projections and peak demand modelling to ensure operational reliability while maintaining cost efficiency. 

This paper develops a staged planning framework that combines uncertain BET demand, candidate-site feasibility, station capacity and route-level charging or swapping schedules. The framework considers demand stages associated with increasing BET penetration and identifies station locations and capacity requirements as demand evolves. It also evaluates whether regulated rest periods can absorb some replenishment time. BET charging demand on each corridor is derived from observed traffic volumes at several adoption levels, and its growth is represented by a Markov chain over low, mid and high adoption branches. These stochastic demands feed a cost–delay optimisation model that determines which candidate sites to open at each adoption stage, how many chargers or swap batteries each site requires, and how each truck combines charging or swapping with the mandatory short breaks and long rests of its driver. The result is a staged deployment plan whose site choices hedge against the range of adoption paths. 

The literature on BET charging infrastructure heavily focuses on mathematical optimisation methods to address facility location or vehicle routing problems. Whitehead et al. (2021) argue that current optimisation models of this nature function well for strategic planning. However, they have difficulty in representing the dynamic complexity of actual operations. The early BET studies have simplified the technical and operational constraints. For example, numerous optimisation models adopt the charging time and battery state-of-charge (SoC) as a linear relationship. The lithium-ion battery is charged following a non-linear profile, and the speed of charging is reduced when the battery is almost full, 3

which is the tapering phase. When the non-linear behaviour of the battery is ignored, it is possible that "optimistic" operational schedules will appear that cannot be realised in practice (Montoya et al., 2017). 

Furthermore, early studies often relied on other simplified assumptions, such as the requirement to always fully recharge the battery. Although overnight depot charging remains the most common strategy for fleet operations to avoid downtime, opportunity charging through battery swapping or fast charging can extend the daily operating range. Furthermore, the separation of charger deployment from vehicle scheduling usually results in the inefficient use of capital (Bertucci et al., 2024). It becomes particularly important for heavy vehicle operations, because heavy vehicles must take mandatory rests and follow the fatigue management schemes set by heavy vehicle regulators. The mandatory truck driver rest duration can be utilised for charging or battery swapping purposes. 

Three questions motivate this study. First, how much charging or swapping infrastructure do long-haul freight corridors require when energy use is derived from terrain, charging follows a non-linear curve, and charging or swapping is scheduled jointly with mandatory driver rest? Second, how sensitive is that requirement to the balance between capital cost and driver delay, and does the sensitivity differ by corridor? Third, how much does planning under adoption uncertainty, rather than for a single forecast, change which stations are built and when? 

To answer these questions, this study makes five contributions. First, unlike a flat, distance-proportional approximation of energy use, the proposed model derives energy consumption directly from the road corridor's vertical alignment. The road centreline for each route is discretised into 1-kilometre segments, each of which is assigned a terrain-derived grade. A physics-based tractive-force model is then applied to each segment to determine whether the truck draws energy from the battery (level or uphill travel) or returns energy to the battery through regenerative braking (sufficiently steep descents), and a constant auxiliary electrical load is added on top of traction demand. Segment-level energy values are summed to obtain cumulative energy consumption between the origin and destination of each route, and between consecutive candidate charging/swapping facilities along the route. 

Second, the model jointly sites stations and schedules charging or swapping together with mandatory driver rest. Charging and rest are allowed to overlap, so charging adds to trip time only when it outlasts the rest the driver must take anyway. Earlier studies schedule charging and rest along a given route with given stations (Bai et al., 2023; Wan et al., 2024; Zähringer et al., 2024; Kahlert et al., 2026); here station locations are decision variables. We implement a stylised two-tier rule (a 10-minute short break after at most 2 hours of continuous driving and a 30-minute long rest after at most 5 hours of cumulative driving) that requires more frequent rest than the minimum under Australia's Standard Hours (Section 4). Other two-tier regimes can be represented by only changing four parameters. 

Third, this study considers multi-pack batteries on trucks instead of one battery. This introduces a more realistic assumption where each battery is independently swappable. Most existing studies target passenger cars and last-mile delivery vans, which carry a single battery pack, so the battery has not been modelled as an indexed set of packs. Modular heavy-truck swapping systems already allow one to three standardised packs per truck (Barnard, 2026).  In this study, we assume each truck is equipped with a fixed number of battery modules. In a modular battery-swapping system, the number of packs exchanged during a single swapping event may vary, such that either a single pack or multiple packs can be replaced. By contrast, for charging, the onboard charging system distributes power across the individual battery modules, enabling simultaneous charging of all batteries. 

Fourth, staged investment under uncertain adoption is formulated as a multistage stochastic program with Markovian demand and solved by stochastic dual dynamic integer programming (SDDiP), building on Zou et al. (2019). At each adoption stage, the policy decides which stations to open, given the stations already built and the adoption level observed so far; an opened station is never closed. Because station costs are discounted, a station that is not yet needed is cheaper to build later, and the stochastic policy weighs this against the risk of having to add stations if adoption turns out high. Methodologically, multistage stochastic integer programs of the kind formulated here are increasingly solved with SDDiP (Zou et al., 2019), which has been applied to power-system capacity expansion (Lara et al., 2020). Stagewise-dependent uncertainty is commonly handled by modelling the random process as a Markov chain and 4

keeping a separate cost-to-go approximation for each Markov state (Dowson and Kapelevich, 2021; Löhndorf and Shapiro, 2019). We adopt this approach for BET adoption. We also show that the Lagrangian multipliers used to generate SDDiP cuts can be bounded by each station’s annualised cost without weakening the cuts (Proposition 2), and that the cuts remain valid when dual iterations or MILP solves are stopped early (Proposition 1). Fifth, a route’s peak-hour flow can be split into several parallel sub-flows, each free to use different stations, so that peak demand is served jointly by multiple capacity-constrained stations rather than by a single facility. 

Section 2 provides a review of the literature and research gap. Section 3 introduces the methods and model formulation. Section 4 presents the case study, and Section 5 presents the results. Section 6 discusses the results, and Section 7 concludes the study and outlines study limitations and future research directions. 

# 2 Literature Review 

This review is organised around four linked modelling layers: evidence on BET deployment, energy-consumption estimation, charging behaviour and scheduling, and infrastructure placement under capacity and uncertainty. We identify which assumptions are inherited when operational models are used to inform strategic station investment. The review concludes by specifying the modelling gaps addressed in this paper and the aspects that remain outside its scope. 

2.1 Operational Feasibility and Deployment Evidence 

Field evidence indicates that BET performance depends strongly on duty cycle, battery capacity, ambient conditions and the maturity of the supporting charging network. Hovi et al. (2019) for example, reported positive user experiences among seven Norwegian pilot operators, but also identified range limitations under high loads and cold conditions, which have led companies to re-route operations and be cautious about vehicle scheduling during peak hours. These findings support modelling BET operations as context-dependent rather than assuming that one vehicle and infrastructure configuration is suitable for every freight task. 

Economic studies reach a similar conclusion. Vijayagopal and Rousseau (2021) showed that the relative competitiveness of BETs depends on vehicle class, battery cost, electricity price and annual utilisation. In particular, a large battery can be economically inefficient when the duty cycle does not use its range. These results motivate explicit battery and duty-cycle assumptions in infrastructure studies, although the present paper does not perform a full total-cost-of-ownership comparison. 

At the network level, Liimatainen et al. (2019) found that electrification potential varies by commodity, vehicle class and charging strategy. Their results demonstrate that the infrastructure network can be a binding condition for long-haul electrification. They suggested that, at current battery technology, BETs can cover around 52% of road transport tasks in Switzerland and 50% in Finland; with higher-capacity batteries and fast-charging infrastructure, coverage rises to 93% and 81% respectively. This gap between current and achievable coverage underscores the importance of the charging network itself, rather than the vehicle alone, as the binding constraint on electrification. The same study identifies medium-duty trucks (under 26 tonnes) and some heavy-duty rigid trucks (26-40 tonnes) as having the strongest commercial case for early electrification, informing the vehicle classes represented in this study's model. 

These studies motivate two modelling requirements: energy and range estimates should reflect route conditions, and infrastructure should be evaluated against the operating patterns it is intended to serve. The following subsections review how these requirements have been addressed and where simplifications remain. 

2.2 Energy Consumption Modelling 5

Energy-consumption estimates affect feasible route spacing, charging requirements and the reliability of the resulting schedule. The literature generally uses either physics-based longitudinal models or data-driven models. The former are transparent and can be applied where telematics data are limited, whereas the latter can capture empirical interactions if sufficiently detailed training data are available. 

Physics-based models use vehicle-dynamics relationships to estimate the power required to overcome aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance, gravitational effects and inertia. Goeke and Schneider (2015) incorporated such relationships into an electric-vehicle routing problem, including payload effects. Tong et al. (2021) likewise showed that corridor-level energy use can vary materially with road grade and speed. These studies support using route-specific energy estimates when station spacing is a central decision. 

Physics-based models are explainable but require detailed parameters, such as drag coefficients and rolling resistance, that are not always available or accurate under real-world conditions. Data-driven approaches tackle this issue by regressing the observed energy consumption over variables such as speed, road grade and vehicle weight, using real-world empirical data. As the availability of BET telematics data increases, researchers move beyond classical statistical models and apply machine learning methods to unravel the nonlinear relationships between parameters (e.g. see, (Gonzalez et al., 2023)). Such models may be valuable for calibration, but detailed second-by-second BET data remain difficult to obtain, especially for emerging long-haul fleets. 

2.3 Charging Behaviour and Non-linear State-of-Charge Dynamics 

Once energy use is estimated, charging duration must be represented consistently with battery behaviour. A significant advance in this literature is the treatment of the relationship between charging time and battery SoC as non-linear rather than linear. Montoya et al. (2017) showed that nonlinear charging functions can materially affect electric-vehicle routing decisions. A constant-current/constant-voltage (CC-CV) profile generally produces a tapering rate at high SoC, so a linear charging-time approximation may underestimate dwell time. Piecewise-linear approximations provide a tractable way to embed this relationship in a mixed-integer model, provided that their breakpoints and approximation error are reported. Löbel et al. (2025) further discussed the limitations of coarse linear interpolation for nonlinear battery behaviour. Their findings highlight the need to document breakpoint selection and to check whether the approximation is conservative. Together, these two studies establish that charging-time realism, not just energy realism, is a prerequisite for credible station-sizing and scheduling models. 

2.4 Charging Scheduling and Grid Interaction 

With energy and charging time specified, a further literature stream considers operational scheduling and grid interaction. Bertucci et al. (2024) studied charging infrastructure placement jointly with the charging capacity in a logistics distribution network. Walz and Rudion (2024) demonstrated that charging policies affect aggregate load profiles on the electricity grid, while Balke et al. (2024) examined multi-site charging and synchronisation with rest periods, reducing peak-hour waiting time and spreading charging load. Liimatainen et al. (2019) simulated four charging-strategy scenarios, including centralised night charging and high-power on-road charging, and found that the share of routes coverable by BETs nearly doubles when a minimum charging time of 2 hours and charging power of at least 250 kW are available on-road, compared with relying on night charging alone, reinforcing the importance of high-power en-route infrastructure for range coverage. These studies suggest that vehicle schedules, charger capacity and grid impacts are interdependent. The present model captures the first two interactions but leaves electrical-network constraints outside the formulation and suggests it for future research direction. 

Shared facilities also create congestion when truck arrivals overlap. Bai et al. (2023) modelled a coordinated charging mechanism and queue information sharing between charging stations and drivers. They modelled a scenario in which stations provide waiting-time estimates on a first-come, first-served basis and trucks dynamically re-optimise charging decisions en-route. Their results indicate that capacity planning based only on individual vehicle energy needs may be 6

insufficient. In contrast, the present model represents peak concurrent demand through capacity constraints but does not model stochastic queues or first-come, first-served service explicitly. 

2.5 Charging and Battery-Swapping Infrastructure Placement 

The most closely related literature concerns the location and sizing of charging or battery-swapping infrastructure. These studies typically use facility-location, vehicle-routing or network-design formulations to balance coverage, service quality and investment cost. Their assumptions differ in whether station capacity, route-level scheduling, grid constraints and uncertainty are represented. 

Whitehead et al. (2021) used a facility-location approach to examine public charging requirements for short-haul electric trucks in Queensland. Their results illustrate how public charging can complement depot charging, while also highlighting the limitations of coverage models that omit station capacity. Speth et al. (2025) incorporated capacity constraints informed by queueing considerations, showing why station layouts that ignore service capacity may be operationally infeasible. Hurtado-Beltran et al. (2021) used GIS-based network analysis to assess electrification potential across the U.S. highway system, and proposed that existing truck-stop infrastructure can provide a useful basis for charging deployment, although electrical and site constraints remain important. Deng et al. (2024) incorporated environmental objectives into station planning and demonstrated that a small number of well-located sites can cover a large share of freight flow in a particular study area. 

Other studies consider battery-swapping systems and the management of batteries at swap stations. Outside the truck context, several studies address battery-delivery and battery-swapping station siting for passenger electric vehicles, without a truck or charging-network focus (e.g., (Liu et al., 2019; Yang and Sun, 2015; Yang et al., 2024)). Within trucking, Wang et al. (2024) jointly modelled station design and the charging of depleted batteries, while Ye (2024) considered charging infrastructure and grid supply for heavy-duty drayage. 

A smaller group of studies incorporates driver regulations or dynamic charging into operational scheduling. Zähringer et al. (2024), Wan et al. (2024), and Kahlert et al. (2026) represent aspects of charging and rest scheduling, but treat station locations as given or simplify the regulatory process. Wang et al. (2025) compare swapping and fast charging in a strategic framework, but do not use the same route-specific station-siting formulation. 

In operations research, en-route station siting is usually framed as a flow-refuelling location problem. An origin– destination flow is served only if the stations along its path allow the trip to be completed within the vehicle range (Capar et al., 2013; Kuby and Lim, 2005). Later work added Benders Decomposition algorithm for plug-in hybrid vehicles (Arslan and Karaşan, 2016) , route flexibility under full coverage (Kınay et al., 2021) , multi-period deployment (Chung and Kwon, 2015), multi-period capacitated planning with demand dynamics (Zhang et al., 2017) and non-linear charging times with heterogeneous chargers (Park and Lee, 2024). For battery swapping, Mak et al. (2013) located swapping stations for passenger EVs under uncertain demand using robust optimisation. These models treat a trip as a sequence of range-feasible legs; none of them schedules the driver’s mandatory rest, which is a defining operational constraint of long-haul trucking. 

Truck driver scheduling under working-hour rules is itself a well-studied problem (Goel, 2009). Goel (2012b) formulated the Australian truck driver scheduling problem, whose sliding-window rules limit work to at most 12 hours and require at least 7 hours of rest in any 24-hour period. Similarly, (Goel, 2010, 2012a; Goel, 2012c) formulated the truck driver schedules for the European Union, Canada and the US. These studies treat vehicle range as unlimited, whereas the present paper models the interaction between rest and energy replenishment. 

Collectively, these studies motivate a model that links station location, capacity and regulated rest scheduling. The present study is the first to address everyone of these features jointly, so the contribution is stated as an incremental integration of established modelling elements. 7

2.6 Research Gaps and Study Contributions 

The reviewed literature reveals a recurring separation between operational realism and strategic infrastructure planning. Energy models are not always connected to station siting; scheduling studies often treat station locations as fixed; and infrastructure studies frequently use static demand and simplified battery or rest representations. Studies that model terrain-based energy physics in detail (e.g. (Goeke and Schneider, 2015; Tong et al., 2021)) are not connected to siting or scheduling decisions; siting and scheduling studies that address driver rest regulations (e.g. (Kahlert et al., 2026; Wan et al., 2024; Zähringer et al., 2024)) either treat station locations as fixed or simplify the rest rules; and the placement and swapping studies reviewed treat the truck battery as a single pack and the infrastructure network as a static, single-stage design problem. 

This paper combines several of these elements in a single staged planning framework. Table 1 summarises the gaps, their planning relevance and the corresponding modelling choices. Taken together, these five contributions integrate the energy, charging-behaviour, scheduling and network-placement layers reviewed above into a single staged planning framework, closing the gap between the operational realism established by the energy-consumption, charging-behaviour and scheduling literature and the strategic, investment-timing decisions addressed by the facility-location and battery-swapping literature.

# 3 Models 

This section presents the assumptions and mathematical formulation. Given candidate locations along each route, the proposed model determines the optimal siting of charging stations and battery-swapping stations by minimising the sum of (i) the total annualised fixed cost of station establishment and (ii) the monetised cost of driver delay, both in AUD per year, subject to HoS regulations and station capacity constraints. The stochastic charging demand component is then formulated as an SDDiP framework to identify the staged station sitings and required battery inventories and/or chargers at each station. 

The principal decision variables are grouped as follows: 

• Station deployment: (i) the candidate locations at which charging or swapping facilities are opened, (ii) the number of charging units installed at each deployed charging facility; (iii) the number of fully charged replacement battery modules at deployed battery swapping station; (iv) the truck sub-flow and facility visit along a route; and (v) if charging is selected, the charging duration and departure SoC of the battery module. 

• Drivers’ rest schedule: The driver rest decision at each facility visit: no break, a short break or a long rest. 

Constraints include: 

• Energy feasibility: the battery state must support travel between the origin, consecutive facilities and the destination. 

• Facility capacity constraints: 

o Charging capacity: the number of trucks represented by the concurrent peak flow cannot exceed the available charging capacity. 

o Swapping capacity: the number of battery modules exchanged concurrently cannot exceed the available replacement battery inventory. 

o Demand growth: as the assumed BET share increases, the required service capacity may increase. Station deployment is irreversible in the current stochastic formulation, whereas charger counts and battery inventories are re-optimised at each stage unless additional monotonicity constraints are imposed. 

• Drivers Hours of Service constraints: 

o A driver must take the assumed short break after no more than the specified maximum period of continuous driving. 

o A driver must take the assumed long rest after no more than the specified maximum cumulative driving period. The exact regulatory interpretation is documented in Section 4 and should be verified against the applicable jurisdiction. 

The objective function is defined as follows: 

• The first objective minimises the total annualised station establishment cost, where the upfront establishment cost is converted to an equivalent annual cost over the planning horizon using the capital recovery factor. 9

• The second objective minimises the total delay cost incurred by trucks, calculated as the product of the delay experienced by each truck and the corresponding driver value of time. The delay at each station is determined by the time required for battery charging or swapping and, where applicable, mandatory rest and break requirements under HoS regulations. Because mandatory rest is required whether or not the truck charges, this term includes dwell time that a diesel truck would also incur; what the model can reduce is the dwell time in excess of the mandatory rest. 

• Both objectives are expressed in AUD per year (delay through the driver value of time), so they are combined by simple addition and the value of time acts as the weight on delay. 

3.1 Assumptions 

This study assumes that batteries charged en-route are not required to reach 100% state of charge (SoC), whereas batteries obtained through swapping are assumed to be fully charged (SoC = 100%). A minimum SoC of 10% is enforced throughout to mitigate range anxiety. Charging battery packs takes substantially longer than swapping them (the charging parameters used in the case study are given in Section 4); a swap can be completed in about 10 minutes, as demonstrated in the eHaul project (Technische Universität Berlin, 2026). Depleted batteries removed from a truck during a swap are subsequently recharged to 100% SoC at the station, rendering them available for future use by other trucks. 

Assumptions are outlined in two categories: 

• Data assumptions: 

o For each origin-destination pair, the input demand is represented by a peak-hour truck flow for the relevant planning stage. The model treats this aggregate flow as divisible across parallel sub-flows; it does not represent individual truck arrivals. 

o Travel speed is fixed by segment and travel time between consecutive facilities is deterministic. Congestion, weather-related speed variation and incidents are not modelled. 

o Energy consumption is calculated from tractive force as a function of grade, speed, distance, vehicle mass and the parameters in  Table 3 . All trucks are assigned the same values for these parameters within an experiment. 

o Each origin-destination pair follows a predetermined route and direction. Rerouting, detours to facilities and route choice are outside the present model. 

• Operational assumptions: 

o When charging is selected at a visit, the battery modules on the representative truck are charged in parallel according to the adopted charging-curve approximation. 

o When swapping is selected, one or more battery modules may be exchanged in the same event. The numerical experiments assume a fixed ten-minute event regardless of the number of modules exchanged; this simplifying assumption requires validation for a specific swapping technology. 

o Charging duration is represented by a convex piecewise-linear approximation of the adopted CC-CV charge curve, rather than by a constant charging rate. 

o During travel, all battery modules discharge in parallel and are subject to the same segment-level energy requirement. 

o Partial charging is permitted. Arrival SoC is constrained by the minimum reserve, while a swapped module is assigned the assumed full SoC. The phrase “minimum SoC reserve” is used below in place of “charging anxiety.” 

o All battery modules are assumed to leave the origin at the specified initial SoC, which is set to 100% in the experiments. 10

The formulation above allows every station location to choose freely between charging and swapping. However, motivated by the existing swapping business models around the world, the experimental study instead evaluates two separate, specialised variants, obtained by fixing the corresponding decision variables to zero and removing the associated capacity and cost terms. Furthermore, because swapping is typically far faster than a full charge ( 𝜌𝜌 𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠 𝑝𝑝𝑝𝑝  ≫

𝜌𝜌 𝑐𝑐ℎ 𝑝𝑝𝑟𝑟 𝑎𝑎 𝑝𝑝 , e.g. a battery swap in 10 minutes versus a full charge of more than an hour), separating two decisions will yield a clearer picture about each charge/swap decision. Accordingly, for a Charge model, no battery-swapping infrastructure is deployed ( 𝑍𝑍 𝑠𝑠𝑠𝑠 𝑝𝑝𝑝𝑝  ≡ 0) , and for a Swap model ( 𝑍𝑍 𝑐𝑐ℎ 𝑝𝑝𝑟𝑟 𝑎𝑎 𝑝𝑝  ≡ 0), no charging infrastructure is deployed. Both are special cases of the general model above; the facility-capacity, energy, and HoS constraints are otherwise unchanged. Each route’s schedule may use at most 𝑁𝑁𝑖𝑖 chargers / 𝐻𝐻𝑖𝑖 batteries at station 𝑖𝑖 . Since the peak-hour demand (𝑓𝑓 𝑟𝑟 𝑝𝑝𝑝𝑝𝑝𝑝𝑝𝑝 ) is significantly higher than the station capacities, we introduce a route-splitting method so a single route’s demand can be met by more than one station when pooled capacity requires it. This demand splitting is needed because with a single schedule per route, a route’s entire concurrent demand must be served by whichever one station its schedule visits – if that demand exceeds even the largest station’s throughput, the model is infeasible, even when spreading the same demand across two or three nearby stations would be feasible. Route-splitting resolves this by representing each route as several parallel sub-flows, each free to choose its own station. The number of parallel sub-flows |Γ r| a route is split into is the smallest number such that the route's peak-hour flow, divided evenly across sub-flows, does not exceed the throughput of the bottleneck candidate station (most capacity-constrained) along the route.

The network distance and drive time between each pair of nodes are calculated using the network analysis in ArcGIS Pro software. Energy consumption model: route distance and travel time between consecutive nodes are obtained from ArcGIS Pro network analysis. Each road centreline is discretised into contiguous segments with a nominal length of 1 km; the final segment retains its actual length. The case study covers major freight corridors on Australia’s east coast, connecting Brisbane, Sydney and Melbourne and including both travel directions. Candidate locations comprise (i) heavy-vehicle rest areas, (ii) existing passenger-EV charging sites and (iii) existing fuel-service stations. Only sites accessible in the direction of travel are retained, so the model does not require a U-turn or route detour. The candidate set is therefore a route-specific representation of feasible sites, not a complete national infrastructure inventory. 

The demand for charging stations is calculated based on a percentage of the current flow on each corridor, considering future demand growth. Passenger EV charging stations are assumed to require the lowest incremental investment due to the presence of electrical infrastructure and utility approvals. In Australia, heavy vehicle drivers must take mandatory rest under the Heavy Vehicle National Law (HVNL). Under its Standard Hours for solo drivers, a driver may work at most 5¼ hours in any 5½-hour period, 7½ hours in any 8 hours, 10 hours in any 11 hours and 12 hours in any 24 hours, with at least 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 60 minutes and 7 continuous hours of stationary rest in those periods, respectively (Heavy Vehicle (Fatigue Management) National Regulation, 2013). Stationary rest is time that a driver spends out of a heavy vehicle or in an approved sleeper berth of a stationary fatigue-regulated heavy vehicle, so the mandatory rest can be used for charging or battery swapping (opportunity charging). This study uses a stylised two-tier rule that requires more frequent rest than these minimums: a 10-minute short break after at most 2 hours of driving and a 30-minute rest after at most 5 hours of driving, following the NSW Heavy Vehicle Driver Handbook (Government of New South Wales) . The 24-hour limit and its 7-hour rest are not modelled (Section 7). We assume that charging is unattended (the driver plugs in and then rests away from the vehicle) and that swapping is automated; the few minutes needed to connect or position the vehicle are neglected, and the remaining dwell counts as rest. Because refuelling counts as work under the HVNL, this assumption should be confirmed for attended charging. 

# 5 Experimental Results 

This section reports the computational performance and station-siting results of the model variants introduced in Section 3, solved as MILP problems across all 12 routes.  The model formulation assumes that, at each en-route station, a trucking operator chooses either to charge all onboard battery packs simultaneously or to swap one or more of them. Although the proposed formulation in Section 3 handles both charging and swapping decisions within a single integrated model, the two energy-replenishment strategies are treated separately in the experimental analysis to more closely reflect current operational practice. This separation is motivated by existing battery-swapping schemes deployed globally, in which operators participating in swapping-based business models rely predominantly on battery exchange rather than independently charging onboard batteries. Such schemes also mitigate operator concerns related to battery ownership, degradation, and residual value, as battery management and replacement are typically handled within the swapping ecosystem. Commercial precedents for both modular multi-pack swapping and the exclusive adoption of a single energy-replenishment strategy are already well established. Battery-as-a-service subscriptions, first offered for passenger cars, have since been extended to freight. CATL's Qiji Energy division has deployed a modular heavy-truck swapping system in which one to three standardised 171-kWh battery modules can be assembled per truck, with users selecting the number of batteries according to their needs and optimising swap routes through a dedicated cloud reservation platform (Barnard, 2026). Automated multi-pack exchange has also been demonstrated for heavy-duty trucks outside China. In Germany's eHaul project, a swap robot approaches the truck from the left and right, lifting and removing the battery packs before retrieving and reinserting fully charged replacements, with the entire process completed in approximately ten minutes (Technische Universität Berlin, 2026). 

These practices are also reflected in the academic literature. Wang et al. (2025) directly compare battery-swapping and fast-charging infrastructure for electric heavy-duty trucks, treating them as two distinct network designs rather than a hybrid system. Their evaluation finds that battery-swapping stations significantly increase transportation efficiency by reducing vehicle downtime relative to fast charging, although they may require larger battery inventories, and that swapping stations using medium-sized batteries offer the best balance of time and labour efficiency. These practical applications and studies collectively motivate us to  assume that a trucking operator adopts either a charging strategy or a swapping strategy exclusively, rather than combining both.  These service models are treated as mutually exclusive from the perspective of individual trucks: trucks subscribed to the charging network use only public charging stations, while trucks subscribed to the battery-swapping network use only swapping stations. Therefore, the two infrastructure networks are modelled separately, while their spatial deployment may be jointly optimised. However, we provide the 29 

integrated model framework capable of handling both swapping vs. charging decisions simultaneously, for readers who wish to study a combined business model. 

To model the demand uncertainty, four demand stages (5%, 10%, 15%, 20% of BET penetration), with three stochastic demand branches (low, mid, and high) are assumed. The demand tree is modelled as a Markov chain, in which each subsequent demand stage branches three ways from its parent. The first node represents the Stage 1 demand (5% BET penetration), and then propagates into 40 distinct nodes (Stage 1: 1; Stage 2: 3 demand scenarios; Stage 3: 9 demand scenarios; and Stage 4: 27 demand scenarios).  Table 6  presents the stochastic demand transition matrix (i.e. branching probabilities). In the absence of real BET adoption data, these demand transition values are hypothesised for methodological demonstrations. The rationale behind the assumed values is as follows. The diagonal dominance (i.e. higher values assumed for diagonal cells) enforces that staying in the same state is always most likely (i.e. model adoption momentum). In other words, technology adoption processes tend to persist. For example, if demand has been growing slowly in this stage, it is more likely to keep growing slowly next stage than to suddenly swing to a completely different regime. Looking at the off-diagonal pattern within each row, the values are assumed to follow an ordered scale, encoding the assumption that demand shifts tend to happen gradually. For example, a low adoption trajectory drifting up to moderate adoption is far more plausible in one stage than leaping straight to high adoption. This assumption attempts to mirror diffusion-of-technology curves (S-curve, not step function). The low and high scenarios are assumed symmetric, indicating the absence of directional bias, meaning that the model has no prior view on whether demand is more likely to accelerate than decelerate. The mid row is a symmetric 25/50/25 split, so from the mid branch the next-stage outcome is equally likely to move up or down. The branch multipliers are 0.7, 1.0 and 1.3 times the mid-path demand.

All optimisation models were implemented in Python using the Gurobi Optimizer (version 13.0.2) via its gurobipy interface (Gurobi Optimization, 2024). Models were solved on high computational infrastructure, using 16 CPU cores and 256 GB RAM per job. Each route's SDDiP solve proceeds in two phases: a training phase (alternating forward and backward passes, building Benders-style cuts via a level-method Lagrangian dual) that stops once the lower bound stalls (relative change <= 1e-4 for 3 consecutive iterations), followed by an evaluation phase that draws J independent forward-pass replications (J = 200 by default) against the fixed, trained cuts to produce a statistical upper bound (mean 

+ 95% confidence -interval half-width), a materially more reliable convergence signal than comparing the lower bound against any single sampled path (see Section 3 and Algorithm 4). A route was classed as converged once the gap between this statistical upper bound and the proven lower bound fell to 5% or below. High demand long routes (e.g. routes 3, 4 and 5) initially closed this gap slowly under the baseline solver configuration. Diagnostic inspection of the candidate-station data revealed that a large share of candidate sites on these corridors share identical fixed installation costs and identical composite site-suitability scores (e.g. 42 of 64 candidates on Route 3 and 37 of 60 on Route 4 are tied on both cost and score). This is a well-documented pathology in facility-location-type mixed-integer programs, where many candidate locations are cost- and score-equivalent, the solver cannot distinguish between combinatorially many interchangeable solutions, which weakens the LP relaxation bound and slows branch-and-bound convergence independently of the underlying decision problem's genuine difficulty. To mitigate this, Gurobi's Symmetry parameter was set to its most aggressive detection-and-breaking level (Symmetry = 2) for these routes, directing the solver to spend additional presolve effort identifying and breaking these symmetric candidate-site structures before branching begins. With this setting, all swap-only routes except Route 9 (5.35%) and seven of the twelve charge-only routes reached the 5% target; the remaining policy-evaluation confidence bounds are reported in Table 10.

The swap-only model converges tightly and consistently: policy-evaluation confidence bounds range from 2.62% to 5.35% (mean 3.35%), and only route 9 exceeds 5%. The charge-only model yields confidence bounds of 3.07–7.9%, with four routes above 5%. The largest are on the New England Highway northbound (7.94%) and the Hume Highway northbound (7.98%). The looser convergence of the charge-only model is consistent with its harder stage subproblems. Charger occupancy depends on continuous charging durations coupled to battery state-of-charge dynamics, and demand is split across sub-flows of drivers, so the mixed-integer stage problems are larger and more often terminated at their time limit. In such cases, the cuts are built from the solver's proven bound rather than the optimal value, which weakens the cost-to-go approximation and keeps the LB lower. In addition, the charge-only objective values are 1.2–3.6 times larger than their swap-only counterparts because charging adds substantially to driver delay. Delay is more sensitive to the demand realisation than station capital cost, which widens the spread of simulated policy costs and hence the confidence interval on the UB. Even so, ten of the twelve charge-only routes converge to within about 6%, indicating that the SDDiP policies are near-optimal for both technologies, with the charge-only results on routes 1 and 7 warranting more cautious interpretation.

5.2 Driver Hours of Service and Charging Schedule 

The model determines the driving schedule for every route, and the charging (or swapping) schedule for every sub-flow, in each demand stage and for both the charge-only and swap-only models.  Figure 5  shows the driver schedule on Route 1 (Newcastle–Brisbane via the New England Highway, northbound) in the first demand stage (5% BET penetration) under the SDDiP solution. The figure reports the cumulative arrival and departure times from the origin, the dwell time and the battery SoC at each stop, and whether the stop is a short break, a long rest, or a break combined with a charging event. At 5% penetration, the route's peak-hour BET flow exceeds the 50-charger capacity of a single station, so the model splits the flow into two subgroups of drivers that recharge at different stations (using equation 31). Both subgroups follow the same timetable, completing the 718-minute trip with six stops: three short breaks and five long rests, with a combined dwell of 244 minutes. They alternate charging sites, however. Every charging event coincides with a mandatory short break or long rest, so charging is absorbed into rest time required under the hours-of-service rules rather than adding separate stops. Where the charge takes longer than the required break, the dwell is extended. Batteries are typically recharged from 10–38% SoC to the 80% charging cap, taking 11–42 minutes per event. It should be noted that a long rest does not necessarily coincide with charging to the maximum SoC. A battery is charged only as much as is needed to reach the next charging stop, or the destination, while keeping the minimum SoC reserve; on Route 1, for example, the batteries charged during long rests reach 74–78% rather than the 80% cap at two stops, and some long rests involve no charging at all. Both subgroups reach Brisbane at the 10% minimum SoC, showing that the schedule uses the available battery range fully without violating the reserve requirement. 37

5.3 Cost–delay Trade-off: Single-objective Extremes 

To test how much of each route’s station-siting decision is driven by capital cost rather than driver delay, the deterministic rolling-horizon model was re-solved under two single-objective extremes with all capacity and HoS constraints unchanged: minimising station CAPEX alone (cost-only, VoT = 0) and minimising driver delay alone (delay-only).  Figure 6  compares the stations built in the final stage (20% penetration) under the two extremes for the swap-only (a) and charge-only (b) models. The cost-only results show the least-cost network that satisfies the range, capacity and HoS constraints. 

Across the 12 routes, the delay-only objective required more charging stations than the cost-only objective (range: 2×– 7×), resulting in a significant increase in total station CAPEX when built to the delay-minimising configuration. For swapping the increase is larger still (6–38×): with no cost penalty, every additional station shortens some dwell, so the delay-only model opens almost every candidate site. Comparing the combined-objective results of the deterministic model ( Figure 4 ) with the single-objective results in  Figure 6  shows that, in the final stage, the cost-only network coincides with the combined-objective network on every charge-only route and on 11 of the 12 swap-only routes. At the assumed value of time of 3 AUD per truck-minute, the least-cost network is therefore already the cost–delay optimum, and the cost–delay trade-off is inactive. This is consistent with charging being absorbed into mandatory rest (Section 5.2), which leaves little delay for additional stations to remove; a value-of-time sweep would show at what value of time additional stations begin to pay off.

# 6 Discussion 

The results in Section 5 bear on the three questions posed in the Introduction: (i) how much infrastructure the corridors require when energy, charging and rest are modelled explicitly; (ii) how sensitive that requirement is to the balance between capital cost and driver delay; and (iii) how much planning under adoption uncertainty changes which stations are built and when. 

On the second question, the cost–delay trade-off is inactive at the assumed value of time. Comparing the combined-objective policies of Figure 4 with the cost-only policies ( Figure 6 ) shows that the least-cost network satisfying the range, capacity and HoS constraints is already the cost–delay optimum on every charge-only route, whereas removing capital cost from the objective multiplies the station count by 2–7 for charging and opens nearly every candidate site for swapping. The delay-only extreme is therefore a diagnostic rather than a plausible investment target. The practical implication is that, once charging is aligned with mandatory rest, network size is governed by range and capacity feasibility at least capital cost rather than by delay. Whether this holds at higher values of time, or for drivers whose charging cannot count as rest, remains to be tested with a value-of-time sweep. 

Comparing the deterministic rolling-horizon model versus the SDDiP model, the results illustrate the central methodological trade-off between the two approaches. Under the charge-only variant, the two approaches select similar total station counts and identical peak charger requirements in several stages, although the selected sites and early-stage costs differ. This comparison suggests that the value of the stochastic model is concentrated in investment timing and site choice under the assumed uncertainty, rather than in a uniform increase in station count. In summary, the deterministic rolling-horizon model is fast, but has no way to hedge against the possibility that the realised demand differs from that path. The SDDiP model is far more computationally expensive to solve, but produces a policy that is explicitly optimised against the full range of demand uncertainty represented in the scenario tree, which is the more appropriate criterion for capital-intensive, long-lived infrastructure decisions of this kind. 

The SDDiP model reached policy-evaluation confidence bounds of 5% or less on 19 of the 24 instances (combination of 12 routes and swapping/charging) despite the integer state space, though cost scales with route complexity, not iteration count. Per-iteration solve time varied by close to two orders of magnitude across routes, and the high-demand routes required both a significantly higher number of iterations and high-cost backward-pass dual solves per iteration. This suggests that computational budget for network-wide SDDiP deployment should be allocated by route complexity (candidate count, peak flow) rather than assumed uniform across a corridor set, an operational consideration for applying this framework at scale. 

Overall, the experiments indicate that staged investment can produce different site and capacity trajectories from a deterministic rolling-horizon policy under the assumed demand process. They also show that terrain, charging-curve, rest and capacity assumptions can influence feasibility and delay. The magnitude of these effects should be established through systematic ablation and sensitivity experiments before the model is used for policy or investment decisions. 

# 7 Conclusion 

This study developed an integrated optimisation framework for siting battery-electric truck charging and swapping infrastructure that jointly addresses five gaps identified in the existing literature: terrain-derived, physics-based energy consumption in place of a flat distance-based rate; direct integration of mandatory driver HoS rest regulations into the charging/siting decision; multi-pack batteries modelled as an independently swappable set; a multi-stage stochastic SDDiP extension that times infrastructure investment under uncertain demand growth; and station siting that allows a route's peak-hour demand to be served jointly by multiple, capacity-contended facilities rather than a single site. 

Applied to twelve major freight corridors on Australia's East Coast, using terrain data, real candidate infrastructure sites, and observed heavy-vehicle traffic volumes, the framework shows that (i) on the illustrative route every charging event fits within a mandatory break, and at the assumed value of time the least-cost network is also the cost–delay optimum; (ii) a ±30% change in adoption alters the final charge-only n etwork by only −12% to +13% stations, with 

over 95% of stations built by the second stage; and (iii) planning under uncertainty mainly changes which sites are selected (71% overlap with a deterministic plan for charging and about half for swapping). The SDDiP policies reached statistical oconfidence bounds of 2.6–5.4% for swap-only and 3.1–7.9% for charge-only routes. 

Methodologically, the SDDiP implementation combines several established elements and adds two results. It follows Zou et al. (2019) in generating Lagrangian cuts for the binary deployment state; it represents stagewise-dependent adoption by a Markov chain with cuts indexed by (stage, parent branch), as is standard for Markovian SDDP (Löhndorf and Shapiro, 2019); and it solves each Lagrangian dual with a level (bundle) method (Lemaréchal et al., 1995), which in preliminary tests converged where plain subgradient ascent did not. Proposition 1 shows that the cuts remain valid lower bounds when dual iterations or MILP solves are stopped early, so time limits weaken the cuts but never invalidate them, and Proposition 2 shows that the Lagrangian multipliers can be restricted to [0, ρ Ci ] without loss, which keeps the level method’s master problem bounded. Finally, training is stopped on lower-bound stability alone and solution quality is reported from a separate, larger-sample policy evaluation, because with the small forward samples recommended by Zou et al. (2019) the per-iteration upper bound was too noisy to serve as a stopping signal. 

This study, however, suffers from a few limitations, which set out the direction for future studies. First, the energy consumption is assumed as a function of terrain, vehicle characteristics, auxiliary loads and a fixed travel speed, and 40 

does not explicitly model traffic, weather, payload variation, or battery thermal effects on efficiency. In particular, the model relies on heavy vehicle traffic counts rather than payload data and therefore assumes a fixed vehicle mass across all trips; as Goeke and Schneider (2015) demonstrate, incorporating actual payload substantially improves energy-consumption accuracy, so more granular, trip-level payload data would be a natural refinement of the current approach. 

Second, charging and swapping stations are assumed to have the same fixed establishment cost, deliberately excluding technology-specific equipment costs (chargers, battery inventory, automated swap systems) because these are highly uncertain, rapidly evolving, and vary considerably across manufacturers. Future studies can test technology-specific, time-varying cost curves as charging and swapping equipment pricing matures and becomes less volatile and publicly available to researchers. 

Third, the transition probabilities and branch multipliers of the SDDiP model are hypothesised; they should be calibrated against historical BET uptake, fleet-operator surveys or forward order data once available. The proposed SDDiP model only carries the binary station-open decision forward, and charger counts and battery inventory are re-solved each stage without a ratchet constraint. Future studies could extend this by including the number of chargers and battery inventory with their own monotonicity constraint, so capacity expansions can be staged and irreversible. However, from the operational viewpoints and emerging business models, the economic viability of this option should be studied in more detail. 

Fourth, the adoption-branch multiplier scales exogenous traffic volumes; the model does not capture the more realistic feedback where better charging/swapping infrastructure itself accelerates BET adoption (i.e. induced demand). Future studies can consider an endogenous demand model where next-stage adoption probabilities respond to the infrastructure actually built, not just to a pre-set Markov process. 

Fifth, the hours-of-service rule is stylised, as all the routes in the case study had travel times less than 12 hours. Therefore, this study does not represent the 24-hour work limit and 7-hour stationary rest of the HVNL Standard Hours, which bind on routes longer than about 12 hours and would create overnight charging opportunities. Implementing the full sliding-window rules is a natural extension. 

Sixth, each route is optimised separately, and the peak-hour flow is assumed to load every station on the route at the same time. A joint network model with time-shifted station arrivals would capture stations shared at corridor intersections and would likely reduce the capacity required. 

Finally, this study overlooks the grid capacity and considers a fixed interconnection for the candidate sites without nearby EV charging stations. Future studies can integrate the station siting decision with the grid capacity constraints, heterogeneously introduced across candidate sites.
