# A QPTAS for Stochastic Scheduling of Bernoulli Jobs 

Junho Hwang 

Abstract 

We study the classical problem of scheduling jobs with random processing times on 

m identical machines to minimize the expected sum of completion times, for Bernoulli jobs: job j takes time pj with probability qj and time 0 otherwise, and its outcome is revealed when it starts. The benchmark is an optimal adaptive policy. We give a quasi-polynomial-time approximation scheme for every number of machines. Previously, quasi-polynomial time was known to give an O(log N )-approximation, and approximation schemes were known only for a constant number of distinct sizes. The scheme rests on a simple observation: it suffices to round the times at which machines become free, rather than the times at which jobs start, to a grid whose width is proportional to the job size, and an optimal policy stretched by a factor close to one already respects such grids. We also show that every policy that fixes the order of the jobs in advance loses a factor Ω(log N ), already on two machines, so a constant factor requires adapting the order to the observed outcomes. Further results include a polynomial-time adaptive rule with ratio min {m, 1 + ∑ 

> j

qj }, a simpler approximation scheme for a constant number of sizes, and #P-hardness of computing the optimal expected cost on two machines. arXiv:2610.11481v1 [cs.DS] 8 Oct 2026 Contents

21 Introduction 

Scheduling jobs on identical parallel machines to minimize the sum of completion times is one of the most basic problems in scheduling. With deterministic processing times it is solved by the shortest-processing-time rule [Smi56, BCS74]. When processing times are random and only their distributions are known in advance, the scheduler is a policy : it decides which job to start next based on what it has observed so far. The benchmark is the best such policy, which may adapt its decisions to the observed processing times. The problem has been studied for decades [Rot66, WP80, MRW84, WVW86], and approximation algorithms are known for broad classes of distributions [MSU99, SU05, MUV06, SSU16, GMUX20, GMUX21]. Their guarantees depend on an upper bound ∆ on the squared coefficient of variation of the processing times, and they become weak when processing times have high variance. A clean way to isolate the difficulty of high variance is to consider Bernoulli jobs . Job j has processing time pj with probability qj and 0 otherwise, and the outcome becomes known when the job starts. Such a job either finishes immediately or blocks its machine for a long time, and a good policy has to decide when to risk such a blocking. Even for Bernoulli jobs, constant-factor approximations have been elusive. For general distributions, Im, Moseley, and Pruhs [IMP15] gave a list policy with ratio O(log 2 N + m log N ), and a constant factor with (1 + ε)m machines when 

m = Ω(log N ). Eberle, Fischer, Matuschke, and Megow [EFMM19] showed that index policies lose a factor Ω(∆ 1/4), and gave an O(m)-approximation when the instance consists of identical deterministic jobs and identical Bernoulli jobs. Gupta, Moseley, and Zhou [GMZ23] gave a list policy whose ratio is ˜O(√m), where ˜O hides factors polylogarithmic in N . Recently, Antoniadis, Hoeksma, Schewior, and Uetz [AHSU25] gave a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) when the number of distinct sizes pj is a constant. They also gave a quasi-polynomial-time O(log N )-approximation for arbitrary sizes, and left open whether constant factors are possible. On the complexity side, Moseley, Pruhs, Uetz, and Zhou [MPUZ26] showed that computing the optimal expected cost, deciding whether it is at most a given value, and computing the expected cost of SEPT are #P-hard for jobs with two-point distributions.

# 3 An approximation-preserving size reduction 

This section reduces the problem to instances whose sizes lie within a polynomial ratio, at a loss of 2ε in the approximation ratio. We build on the reduction of Gupta, Moseley, and Zhou [GMZ23, Lemma 3.3 and Appendix C], which tests very large jobs first and runs very small jobs at the start, at the cost of a constant factor. Antoniadis, Hoeksma, Schewior, and Uetz [AHSU25, Lemma A.1] adapt it to turn an α-approximation for instances whose sizes are powers of c ≥ 2 into a ( c2 + cα )-approximation. The version below keeps the ratio α up to an additive 2 ε. It uses Lemma 2.2(b) to compare with the optimum, and Lemma 2.3 to bound the cost of the rare event that a very large job is positive. If m ≥ N , starting every job at time 0 is optimal. If m = 1, the policy that starts the jobs in non-decreasing order of μj (SEPT) is optimal [Rot66]; Section 5 recalls the argument for adaptive policies. We therefore assume 2 ≤ m < N . 6.1 SEPT for stochastically ordered Bernoulli jobs

Weber, Varaiya, and Wal-rand [WVW86] prove that if the processing times are stochastically ordered, then SEPT minimizes the expected sum of completion times on identical machines that become available at arbitrary given times. They assume, for convenience, that the processing times have densities, and their induction considers strategies that start a job whenever a machine becomes available. # 8 Open problems 

The main open question is whether Bernoulli jobs admit a polynomial-time approximation scheme, or at least a polynomial-time constant-factor approximation, when m is part of the input. After the size reduction, the dynamic program of Section 4 is quasi-polynomial for two reasons. It records the number of remaining jobs in each of the O(ε−1 log( N/ε )) size classes, and it records the release times as a multiset over O(ε−2 log( N/ε )) candidate values. A polynomial-time scheme needs a more compact description of both, and by Theorem 7.1 it must be adaptive. A natural alternative is to round a relaxation over start-time distributions. For Bernoulli jobs, such a relaxation can include, for every deadline u, the expected-rank constraints of the laminar matroid that allows at most m(⌊u/h ⌋ + 1) positive jobs of size at least h to start by time u.Constraints that act on one deadline at a time are not enough. On one machine, take jobs with 

pi = 2 N −i+1 and qi = 1 /p i for i = 1 , . . . , N , so that OPT = N (N + 1) /2. Start job i at time 0 with the probability that no larger job is positive, and at time pi otherwise. This satisfies every such rank constraint, because the first positive job of size larger than u together with all positive smaller jobs forms an independent set. It also keeps the expected processing in every interval below the length of the interval, because ∑
