# Price-Optimized EV and Heat-Pump Scheduling Can Exceed Uncoordinated Feeder Peaks 

Tatjana Hein 1,2 , Lukas Wagner 1, Alex Bernhard 1, and Felix Ocker 1

> 1

Institute of Automation Technology, Helmut Schmidt University, Hamburg, Germany 2HAW Hamburg, Germany tatjanasophie.hein@haw-hamburg.de, {lukas.wagner, alex.bernhard, felix.ocker}@hsu.hamburg 

Abstract —Electric vehicles and heat pumps add large, time-flexible loads to low-voltage distribution feeders. When each household independently minimizes its own charging cost against the same electricity price, this flexible demand piles into the cheapest hours, and the resulting synchronization can stress the feeder more than uncoordinated operation does. This work quantifies that effect on a rural low-voltage feeder (97 buses, 250 kVA transformer) across 160 simulation runs spanning seven scenarios, four seasons, and electric-vehicle, heat-pump, and photovoltaic penetration from 0 to 90 %. At 30 % penetration of each device, this decentralized cost minimization drives the transformer to 253 % of rated capacity, more than twice the level of uncoordinated charging, and up to 478 % at 90 % electric-vehicle penetration, with the first undervoltage violations at the far end of the longest feeder arm. The ranking is device-mix dependent: each 30 pp of heat-pump penetration adds about twelve times more to the price-based peak than to any fixed schedule, so price-based scheduling becomes the worst strategy once heat pumps are present. Only a central schedule that explicitly caps the transformer removes these demand-driven violations, while at high photovoltaic penetration a summer floor from midday reverse power flow bounds what any demand-side strategy can achieve. A regulated time-variable grid tariff added to the spot price provides no relief for heat-pump-driven synchronization and only marginal relief for electric-vehicle-driven synchronization in autumn. The simulation and optimization code is available at https://github.com/lukas-wagner/ SynchronizationEffectsAnalysis. 

Index Terms —low-voltage distribution networks, load synchro-nization, demand-side coordination 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Efforts to decarbonize the heating and transport sectors are driving large-scale adoption of heat pumps (HPs) and elec-tric vehicles (EVs) in residential areas. Both add substantial electrical loads to low-voltage (LV) feeders, and the timing of part of this consumption can be shifted within the day. When many devices on the same feeder draw power in the same hours, their individual loads add up into a coincident peak. This synchronization can overload the distribution transformer and depress bus voltages in LV networks [1]. Two kinds of time-varying cost signals steer such flexible loads away from peak hours: dynamic energy prices such as intraday spot prices, and time-variable grid tariffs. Germany’s § 14a of the Energy Industry Act (EnWG) [2] is one such tariff: in exchange for accepting operator control during congestion,

This work models only that tariff signal, not the operator-side throttling. Auniform cost signal, however, can re-create the peak it is meant to relieve: when every device optimizes against the same prices, demand concentrates in the cheapest window, as observed for public EV charging under day-ahead prices [3]. That study excludes HPs and feeder-level power flow. Whether the same amplification arises for mixed residential EV and HP adoption on a rural German LV feeder, and how price-based coordination compares there to transformer-aware scheduling, has not been systematically studied. This work addresses that gap with a scenario-based power-flow simu-lation on the SimBench 1-LV-rural2--0-sw reference network, a 97-bus, 250 kVA feeder [4] whose long arms make it a demanding case for feeder voltage. Seven scenarios, from uncontrolled heating and fixed-time charging to centralized transformer-aware optimization, are evaluated in targeted one-factor-at-a-time penetration sweeps: each device is swept over 0, 30, 60, and 90 % while the others are held at a fixed reference level, across four seasonal contexts, for a total of 160 simulation runs. Three research questions guide the evaluation: 

RQ1: How does peak transformer loading respond to increas-ing EV, HP, and photovoltaic (PV) penetration, and how do all three indicators, transformer loading, line loading, and bus voltage, compare across seasons at a reference penetration? 

RQ2: Where in the rural LV feeder do synchronization-induced overloads and voltage violations concentrate spatially? 

RQ3: Do price-based coordination strategies (intraday price, § 14a EnWG Module 3) mitigate or amplify synchronization effects relative to uncoordinated charging, and what does transformer-aware optimization achieve by comparison? The contributions are: (i) A quantification of how combined EV, HP, and PV pen-etration stresses a rural LV feeder, separating a demand-side price-synchronization peak from a summer supply-side reverse-power-flow floor that demand-side schedul-ing cannot remove. (ii) Evidence that the feeder topology determines where the stress concentrates, the longest arm, while price synchro-nization sets its severity and extent, since the end-of-line voltage violations there vanish under diversified charging start times. arXiv:2610.05916v1 [eess.SY] 5 Oct 2026 (iii) A systematic strategy and penetration comparison show-ing that the relative ordering is device-mix dependent, that only transformer-aware peak-shaving removes the demand-driven overloads, and that the § 14a EnWG Mod-ule 3 grid fee adds at most marginal relief, none for HP-driven synchronization. II. R ELATED WORK 

Prior work is assessed against the problem from Sec. I, whether price-based coordination of combined residential loads relieves or amplifies feeder synchronization. Relevant work should jointly model EVs and HPs (R1), evaluate price-based coordination (R2) against the network-oriented control it is benchmarked on (R3), span at least two seasons (R4), test a regulatory mechanism dedicated to controllable loads such as § 14a EnWG curtailment or grid fee (R5), and resolve thermal and voltage violations spatially within the feeder (R6). One line of work models EVs and HPs together and quanti-fies their combined grid impact (R1). Oliyide and Cipcigan [5] project that in a typical urban British LV network, concurrent EV and HP uptake drives transformer overload in an optimistic 2050 winter scenario, while cable loading and bus voltages stay within limits (R1, R4), but evaluate no coordination strategy (R2, R3), on an urban rather than the rural feeder targeted here. Tomaselli et al. [6] use a rolling-horizon optimal power flow to schedule battery and thermal storage in a synthetic German LV grid, combined with curative EV and HP limiting (R1, R3), and evaluate § 14a EnWG curtailment (R5): storage pre-charging resolves most load violations while curtailment adds little and can worsen overloading through HP rebound. Congestion is assessed at the HV/MV transformer rather than at individual LV bus or line level. Zimmermann et al. [1] simulate the German Federal Government energy transition pathway, with rising EV, HP, and PV shares, on two SimBench reference networks under passive operation, characterizing congestion by month and by equipment rating (R1, R4). Congestion arises exclusively from transformer over-loading and voltage-band violations with no line exceeding its rating, and the radial topology concentrates thermal stress at the transformer and feeder-entry lines while undervoltage concentrates at the remote feeder ends (R6). Another line steers charging through price signals (R2). Lu et al. [7] propose a real-time EV price mechanism that maxi-mizes aggregate welfare and shaves peaks, without modeling network constraints or synchronization effects. Panda et al. [3] quantify the aggregate consequence at over 1200 public charging points in the Netherlands, finding that cost-minimized charging under dynamic day-ahead prices increases aggregate peak demand by up to 16 % relative to unoptimized charging as charging concentrates in the same low-price period. Segmented network tariffs reduce but do not eliminate this effect. Malkova et al. [8] minimize EV charging costs at a workplace charging station under day-ahead spot prices and dynamic grid tariffs via receding-horizon mixed-integer linear program (MILP), enforcing a cluster power limit but performing no feeder-level power flow (R2, R4). A. Strategy Comparison at a Reference Penetration (RQ1, RQ3) 

Tab. III compares the grid indicators defined in Sec. III for each scenario at the EV=PV=HP=30 % reference penetration in winter, the season with the highest heat-pump-driven load. The Baseline stays well within all limits. Fixing all EVs to an 18:00 start (Fixed-times) raises transformer loading to 167.6 %, as the simultaneous charging pulse alone exceeds the rating on top of the evening household peak, giving exactly 14.0 h of overload (2 h per evening) in every season. Randomizing start times (Fixed-random) spreads the pulses and reduces the peak to 112.4 %, substantial but insufficient relief. Decentralized cost optimization performs substantially worse than either fixed scenario: each device independently shifts demand to the cheapest overnight hours (00:00–05:00), which concentrates load into a synchronized night-time peak at 253.2 % of rated capacity. Individually rational cost mini-mization thus amplifies synchronization. Although the cheap window spans several hours, the linear cost minimization does not spread load across it: all devices face the same price ranking and draw full power in the same cheapest quarter-hours. This holds across seasons: the Decentralized peak reaches 255.1 % in spring and 253.6 % in autumn, both more than twice the corresponding Fixed-random peak. The spring ratio is largest (factor 2.5), as lower thermal demand extends the HP buffer flexibility. In summer, Fixed-random drops below rated capacity (96 %) while Decentralized coordination still reaches 158.2 % from EV overnight optimization alone. Line loading and voltage follow the transformer: Decentralized reaches about 180 % line loading and 0.87 p.u. in winter, spring, and autumn and still 113 % and 0.94 p.u. in summer, whereas Fixed-random stays at or below 77 % and above 0.96 p.u. in every season. Adding the Module 3 fee (Decentralized-M3) leaves the winter peak unchanged and lowers the autumn peak by 18.4 percentage points (pp), or 7.3 % (Sec. IV-D). Centralized-cost coordination caps the instantaneous peak at 102.4 % via the transformer constraint (the excess over 100 % stems from reactive power and losses outside the active-power constraint (6)), but the optimizer fills every available hour up to that limit, yielding 12.75 h of overload per week, nearly double the 6.5 h under Fixed-random. It trades overload height for duration, saturating near 102 % in all four seasons with fewer overload hours when HP demand is lower. At this reference point, Centralized-peak is the only strategy that removes all overloads, holding the transformer peak at its pre-electrification level of 37.9 %.

D. § 14a EnWG Module 3 Grid Fee (RQ3) 

The Module 3 fee is active only in Q1 and Q4, covering the studied winter and autumn weeks. Adding it to Decen-tralized coordination leaves the peak unchanged in winter at every tested penetration (143 %, 287 %, and 478 % at 30, 60, and 90 % EV with HP=0 %), because the fee’s 00:00–06:00 reduced-fee window coincides with the cheapest intraday hours. In autumn a 4–5 pp reduction emerges across the EV sweep, as the night-time price minimum is less pronounced in week 42 and the fee gains a marginal incentive to shift demand. The larger 18.4 pp drop at the mixed reference penetration (Sec. IV-A) reflects its higher baseline peak, so in relative terms every fee effect stays below 8 %. The HP scaling (EV=0 %) shows no reduction in either season: HPs already concentrate all shiftable thermal demand into the cheapest overnight hours through the spot price signal, so their buffer constraints leave no residual flexibility for the Module 3 fee to exploit. Without a channel to feeder state, as in the agent-based scheme of Galys et al. [11], a time-variable fee alone cannot deliver comparable relief. V. C ONCLUSION 

This work quantified how a rural LV feeder responds when EVs and HPs follow a shared price signal, and how far coor-dinated scheduling and a regulated tariff relieve the resulting stress. 

RQ1: At the reference penetration (EV=HP=PV=30 %), decentralized cost minimization drives the transformer to 253 % of rated capacity, more than twice the Fixed-random benchmark, and up to 478 % at 90 % EV. HPs amplify this disproportionately, adding about 110 pp per 30 pp of HP pen-etration, roughly twelve times the 8–10 pp per step under fixed scheduling, and the mechanism holds for all three indicators across winter, spring, and autumn. In summer, PV reverse power flow instead sets a 128.9 % transformer floor that demand-side coordination cannot lower. 

RQ2: The overloads concentrate on the longest feeder arm. Line loading peaks at its entry, where the full downstream load concentrates, while cumulative impedance drives the end-of-line voltages below 0.9 p.u. Shorter branches stay within limits. 

RQ3: The strategy ranking is device-mix dependent: Fixed-times is worst in a pure-EV fleet, but once HPs join, price-responsive Decentralized scheduling becomes worse than any fixed schedule in the demand-driven regime. Centralized-cost coordination holds the peak near 102 % but stretches the over-load over time (up to 30.5 h per week) and leaves less voltage margin (down to 0.94 p.u.), whereas Centralized-peak removes all demand-driven violations, limited only by the supply-driven PV floor. The § 14a EnWG Module 3 fee gives no relief for HP-driven synchronization in either fee-active season and only a marginal 4–5 pp reduction for EV-driven synchronization in autumn. These results indicate that price-signal coordination requires either sufficient device heterogeneity [3] or explicit grid-constraint coupling [10]. Several limitations bound these findings. All optimizations assume perfect foresight over the 7-day horizon, whereas prac-tical implementations must operate under forecast uncertainty. The device fleet is homogeneous (identical EV batteries, HP ratings, buffers, and building coefficients, with a constant COP and space-heating-only demand), which likely accentu-ates synchronization relative to a heterogeneous population. The study uses a single reference network topology, and the Module 3 null result holds for the studied intraday price profile and Norderstedt fee schedule; a price minimum outside the fee window would change it. Future work should examine receding-horizon control under forecast uncertainty, time-integrated overload-duration con-straints, a fair allocation of the capped feeder capacity among households [12], and additional feeder topologies.
