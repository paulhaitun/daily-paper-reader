Abstract —This paper studies an airport campus where long stay EV parking, airport buildings, and an AI data center share a single grid connection. The campus pays a commercial electricity tariff that charges for both the energy used and the highest power drawn during peak hours. Parked EVs can send power back to the campus through vehicle to grid (V2G) chargers and lower this peak. This paper proposes a two stage method. In the first stage, a linear program schedules EV charging and discharging to maximize the operator’s profit and also sets a fixed daily payment to EV owners. In the second stage, a search chooses the share of energy savings paid to owners. The model guarantees that every owner is paid enough to take part, pays for battery wear so that EVs discharge only when it is worth the wear, and assigns charging ports on a first come, first served basis. In the case study, 400 V2G chargers are added to 1200 existing Level 2 chargers, serving 5000 expected sessions under summer and winter rates. In a summer week, V2G raises operator profit by $4978 compared with smart charging and lowers the high peak demand by 4400 kW (9 %). The best contract gives owners 70 % of the energy savings and earns 21 % more than a fixed payment alone. Doubling the V2G ports to 800 lowers the peak further but reduces profit, because owner payments grow faster than the savings. V2G pays back within 10 years if a bidirectional charger costs less than about $1800 more than a standard one. Compared with a 5 MW/10 MWh stationary battery, V2G cuts more peak demand at $409 of investment per kW instead of $1522 and needs no extra land, while the battery does not pay back within its lifetime. 

Index Terms —Vehicle to Grid (V2G), Electric Vehicle charg-ing, Demand Charge management, Peak shaving, Techno Eco-nomic Analysis, DER Aggregation, Edge Data Center

Transportation is moving to electric power, and data centers are growing at the same time. Both add large loads at the same locations on the distribution network. At large commercial sites such as airports, these two loads often share one grid connection. Their combined peak raises the site’s demand charges and exposes the operator to time of use (TOU) energy prices. At the same time, electric vehicles (EVs) that stay parked at such sites for days form a large pool of batteries that would otherwise sit idle. Using these batteries through vehicle to grid (V2G) operation can flatten the site peak, shift energy to cheaper hours, and delay the purchase of stationary storage. Two linked problems must be solved to use this potential. The first is operational: thousands of different EVs must be scheduled together with the data center and building loads under a real commercial tariff [1], while each vehicle’s arrival state of charge, departure target, parking time, and charger availability are respected. The second is economic: EV owners will only let their batteries be discharged if they are paid for the battery wear and for the trouble of taking part [2]. A payment scheme that ignores battery wear either cycles the fleet too much, which hurts owners, or fails to attract them at all [3]. Rahman et al. [4] designed fixed term V2G contracts together with online EV scheduling for virtual power plants that take part in electricity markets. Their method raised profit while still meeting each owner’s charging need before departure. Orfanoudakis et al. [5] built EV2Gym, an open source V2G simulator that includes EV, charger, transformer, user behavior, and battery wear models for smart charging and reinforcement learning research. Brinkel et al. [6] built a multi objective V2G model with battery wear and transformer limits that cut costs by 32.4% and emissions by 23.6%, and questioned whether grid reinforcement is needed. In [7], the same group compared six grid tariff designs for EV charging and found that capacity based tariffs lower grid expansion costs and are fairer, but that no single design is best for efficiency, fairness, and predictability at once. Only a few studies look at EV scheduling in airport parking. The ALIGHT report [8] studied smart charging and V2G at Copenhagen Airport. It found frequency reserve (FCR-D) to be the most profitable service, worth about C250– C350 per year per destination charger and about C2,500 per well used fast charger, with little effect on users. A study at Harry Reid International Airport compared MILP and deep reinforcement learning (DDPG) for EV scheduling with V2G [9]. MILP gave higher profit and customer satisfaction, while reinforcement learning was faster and caused less battery wear but handled the peak less well. Guo et al. [10] planned an airport microgrid with electric aircraft, parking lot EVs, and V2G using NSGA-II, and showed that V2G lowers operating cost. Vereno et al. [11] showed that automated airport garages can provide fast frequency reserve while still fully charging cars, but that regulatory approval is the main barrier. Yan et al. [12] combined LSTM forecasting and MILP to size PV, ground service vehicle fleets, and chargers at Guangzhou Baiyun airport, cutting annual cost by 624,042.60 CNY with a 3.04 year payback. None of these studies combines an airport parking fleet with a colocated AI data center under a demand charge tariff, or designs the owner payment as part of the dispatch. B. Selected Deployment: 400 V2G Ports 

Table V gives the operator cost, the charging station rev-enue, and the net cost for the three operating modes over the summer week. Figs. 3 and 4 show the campus and EV power profiles. In total, 4250 sessions are served, of which 400 are V2G vehicles. Non-V2G sessions pay $0.54/kWh in the weekday high peak window (1:00–5:00 PM), which is 20 % more than the $0.45/kWh off peak price. V2G vehicles pay a flat $0.45/kWh only for the energy they need. The net cost, which is the operator cost minus the station revenue, is used as the bottom line. Smart charging lowers the net cost by $5658 (0.69 %) compared with uncontrolled charging. The grid bill falls by $3202 (0.36 %), mostly because of lower demand charges: the campus peak drops from 49394 kW to 48580 kW, and the facility and TOU demand charges fall by $866 and $2284. The other $2456 comes from higher station revenue (+3.61 %). Because the dispatch maximizes profit, it places some non-V2G charging in the high peak window to earn the 20 % price premium. This raises the high peak energy bill, but the extra revenue is larger, and the high peak demand still stays at the campus peak of 48580 kW. V2G lowers the net cost by a further $4978 (0.61 %) compared with smart charging, and by $10636 (1.30 %) compared with uncontrolled charging. The grid bill falls by $16339 (1.85 %) against smart charging. Of this saving, $10192 (62 %) is paid to the vehicle owners, including $4508 of wear payment, and $1169 of station revenue is lost because less non-V2G charging falls in the premium window. D. Sensitivity Analysis 

To test how much the results depend on the assumptions, the four inputs that drive the owner payments and the demand charge savings are changed one at a time. For each case, both seasons are solved again with the full energy share search. The battery replacement cost crep , which scales the wear cost and the participation reserve, is changed from 98 to 70 and 130 $/kWh. All demand charge rates, including the facilities charge, are scaled by 0.75 and 1.25. The 20 % high peak retail premium is removed, and the cycle limit Nmax is set to 2 and 4. The results are shown in Table VIII. The base case matches Sections III-B–III-C, and every owner meets the participation constraint in every case. Two results hold in every case. First, V2G is profitable in summer, with weekly gains between $1686 and $8319. Second, the summer high peak reduction stays at 4400 kW, except with lower demand charges (4311 kW) and with the tighter cycle limit (3636 kW). Since 4400 kW equals 400 vehicles discharging at 11 kW each, the peak reduction is limited by the fleet’s discharge power, not by its stored energy. It reaches break even when the demand charges rise by 25 %. A 25 % change in demand charges shifts the summer gain by about $3300 per week. This equals 25 % of the $13290 per week of demand charge savings in the base case, so the gain changes linearly with the tariff. The retail premium has only a small effect, since it moves the smart charging and V2G cases by similar amounts. The cycle limit shows a seasonal trade off. Three cycles is close to the best choice in summer, since both two and four cycles lower the gain. In winter, two cycles cut the loss from $1095 to $138 per week, because the reserve owed to owners falls with the cycle allowance while the extra winter cycles bring little demand relief. A contract with a different cycle allowance in each season would therefore recover most of the winter loss. Finally, smart charging also needs ports for the 400 long stay vehicles, so the cost that V2G must recover is only the extra price of a bidirectional port over a standard one. E. Investment Comparison: V2G Versus Stationary BESS 

The stationary BESS described in Section II-F is scheduled by the same LP, this time without V2G. In the summer week it raises the operator’s profit by $6732.93 compared with smart charging and lowers the high peak demand by 3095 kW, from 48580 kW to 45485 kW, at a battery wear cost of $1715.53. Because the BESS has no owners to pay, it also stays profitable in winter, earning $2475.44 per week. Over a full year it gains $203278, more than V2G. A higher yearly gain, however, does not make the BESS a good investment for the campus, as the cost comparison in Table IX shows. First, the BESS is a much more expensive way to lower the peak. The 5 MW/10 MWh unit costs $4.71 M to buy, plus $43220 per year to operate and maintain [22]. The 400 bidirectional chargers cost $1.80 M. V2G lowers the summer high peak demand by 4400 kW at $409 of investment per kW, while the BESS lowers it by only 3095 kW at $1522 per kW, nearly four times as much. Second, V2G uses parking spaces that already exist, but the BESS needs its own land next to the campus. The 120 MWh Escondido battery fits on about one acre, including its supporting equipment [23]. At the same density, a 10 MWh unit needs about 0.083 acres. Industrial land in the Los Angeles South Bay, the area around the airport, has sold for $7.1 M per acre [24], which adds $0.59 M, or 12.6 %, to the cost of the BESS. This is the lowest likely estimate. Recent planning guidelines allow 3 acres per 100 MWh once container spacing, power equipment, and safety clearances are included [25], which would raise the land cost to $2.13 M. In addition, the NFPA 855 fire code requires minimum distances between battery units and from nearby buildings, which are hard to provide at a crowded airport site [26]. G. Representative Vehicle Dispatch and Compensation 

Fig. 6 shows how the contract works for one long stay vehicle. The vehicle has a 75 kWh battery and stays parked for 111 hours (4.62 days). It arrives at 31.1 % state of charge and leaves at its 95.0 % target. Over the week, 241.7 kWh is charged and 172.6 kWh is sent back to the campus, which equals 2.88 full cycles, with peak rates of 10.6 kW charging and 11.0 kW discharging. The wear from this cycling is valued at $11.72 and is fully paid back, so the owner loses nothing by taking part. On top of the wear payment, the owner receives $14.11 from the energy share and $0.56 from the fixed credit. The net payoff of $14.67 equals the owner’s reserve, so the participation constraint is tight for this vehicle: the owner is paid just enough to take part, and the operator keeps the rest. Fig. 6 also shows that discharge happens in the daily high peak windows and is always followed by recharging, so the departure target is met while the demand charge savings are still delivered. IV. C ONCLUSION 

This paper modeled an airport campus where long stay EV parking, airport buildings, and an AI data center share one grid connection. A two stage method was proposed that schedules V2G and designs the payment to EV owners at the same time. The method makes sure that every owner is paid enough to take part, pays for battery wear, and respects the number of available chargers. With 400 V2G chargers, V2G raised the operator’s profit by $4978 per week in summer compared with smart charging and lowered the high peak demand by 4400 kW (9 %). The best contract gives owners 70 % of the energy value plus a small daily payment, and earns 21 % more than a fixed payment alone. All 400 owners take part, and no served vehicle has to wait for a charger. Doubling the ports to 800 lowered the peak by a further 849 kW, but reduced the profit, since the owner payments grew faster than the demand charge savings. The value of V2G depends on the season. In winter, the lower demand charges no longer cover the owner payments, so V2G is best offered in summer only, which gives an annual gain of $86,761. The sensitivity analysis confirmed the summer benefit in every case and showed that V2G pays back within 10 years if a bidirectional charger costs less than about $1800 more than a standard charger. A 5 MW/10 MWh stationary battery earns more per year, but costs nearly four times as much per kW of peak reduction, needs land that adds 13 % to 45 % to its cost, and does not pay back within its lifetime.
