# Predictive Rerouting of Connected and Automated Vehicles Using Traffic and Charging Demand Forecasts 

Tony Kinchen, Ting Bai, and Andreas A. Malikopoulos, Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract — In this paper, we consider the problem of pre-dictive rerouting of connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) in mixed traffic with electric vehicle charging demand. We provide a framework that combines a diffusion convolutional recurrent neural network (DCRNN) with a routing policy that accounts for congestion, CO 2 emissions, route length, and charging demand. The DCRNN uses historical network observations to forecast traffic conditions and charging demand. These forecasts are then used to evaluate feasible alternative routes for eligible CAVs. A route change is accepted when the alternative preserves connectivity to the original destination and improves the prescribed route cost. We evaluate the proposed framework in SUMO under controlled traffic disruptions at five CAV penetration levels, ranging from 5% to 45%. We compare its performance with K-shortest-path routing, predictive-density routing, V2X proactive routing, and a reference scenario without rerouting. In the considered scenarios, the proposed framework reduces the average travel-time index by approx-imately 1.8% relative to the reference scenario and achieves the lowest average travel-time index, highest average speed, and lowest aggregate CO 2 emissions among the active routing methods. Across all five penetration levels, it accepts 41 route changes, compared with 146 for K-shortest-path routing and 153 for V2X proactive routing. The reference scenario retains lower aggregate emissions and distance traveled, illustrating the tradeoff between congestion reduction and the additional travel associated with rerouting. 

I. Introduction 

A. Motivation 

Connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) provide an opportunity to improve transportation network performance by incorporating shared information into routing decisions. In mixed traffic, however, only a subset of vehicles can respond to a prescribed routing policy. The effectiveness of such a policy depends on the information available when a decision is made, the availability of alternative routes, and the interactions between rerouted vehicles and the surround-ing traffic. These interactions impose significant challenges since a route change that benefits an individual vehicle may increase congestion on other roadway segments. B. Related Work 

Several research efforts have been reported in the liter-ature toward developing routing approaches for connected vehicles. Distributed routing through intelligent intersections has been investigated as a means of improving throughput and reducing travel time [1]. System-centric routing has also been considered in mixed traffic, where CAV rout-ing decisions account for network travel and energy costs while human-driven vehicles retain individual routing objec-tives [2], [3]. Other efforts have developed multi-objective routing approaches that account for travel time, emissions, fuel consumption, and energy use [4]–[8]. These approaches establish the importance of considering the effects of routing decisions beyond the travel time of an individual vehicle. Traffic forecasting provides a means of incorporating an-ticipated network conditions into routing decisions. Graph-based models represent the spatial relationships between roadway segments together with the temporal evolution of traffic observations. Spatio-temporal graph convolutional net-works, Graph WaveNet, and adaptive graph convolutional recurrent networks have been developed for this purpose [9]– [11]. The diffusion convolutional recurrent neural network (DCRNN) combines diffusion convolution over a directed graph with recurrent temporal modeling [12]. Extensions using graph partitioning have also been investigated for traffic forecasting [13]. These models provide a basis for estimat-ing conditions on downstream roadway segments before a vehicle reaches them. However, forecasting accuracy alone does not establish the effectiveness of the resulting routing decisions, since rerouting changes the traffic conditions that the forecasting model seeks to predict. EV charging introduces another consideration in the rout-ing problem. Charging decisions depend on battery state of charge, travel conditions, station availability, and the time required to reach and use a charging station. Research efforts on charging assignment, reinforcement learning, coordinated charging stops, and integrated transportation and power net-arXiv:2610.10532v1 [cs.NI] 7 Oct 2026 works have examined these interactions [14]–[19]. This body of work motivates accounting for charging demand when evaluating routes through roadway segments that contain or provide access to charging stations. Microscopic traffic simulation provides an environment for evaluating the effects of these decisions on individual vehicles and the surrounding network. The Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO) platform supports the simulation of traffic operations, vehicle routing, emissions, and EV energy use [20]. Through its Traffic Control Interface (TraCI), net-work observations can be supplied to forecasting algorithms and routing decisions can be applied during a simulation. Prior studies have used SUMO to examine the effects of CAV penetration on traffic performance, fuel consumption, and emissions [21], [22], and to evaluate rerouting based on connected-vehicle information [23]. The above research efforts motivate the integration of traffic forecasting, charging-demand prediction, and CAV rerouting. A central question is how predicted conditions can be used to select route changes that improve traffic performance when only a subset of vehicles is controllable. Addressing this question requires evaluating both the in-formation used to select a route and the realized network performance after the routing decisions are applied. The present study differs from purely macroscopic as-signment methods because the route decision is applied to individual vehicles during a microscopic simulation. This distinction is important for two reasons. First, the feasibility of an alternative path is determined by the current SUMO edge, the original destination, and the set of permitted downstream connections, rather than by an aggregate origin– destination flow alone. Second, the effect of a rerouting action is realized only after the vehicle interacts with the surrounding mixed traffic. Consequently, the quality of a candidate route cannot be judged solely by its standalone length or free-flow travel time. A route that appears attractive for a single vehicle may place additional demand on another corridor, alter queue formation near a disruption, or expose the vehicle to edges with elevated charging-related demand. These considerations motivate a routing policy that uses predicted local conditions while still evaluating the final outcome through the microscopic simulation outputs. The no-reroute reference is also essential for interpreting the results. Active rerouting can reduce congestion severity by moving controlled vehicles away from blocked or con-gested roadway segments, but it may also increase route length and therefore total distance and emissions. For this reason, we compare the proposed method both with active routing benchmarks and with the no-reroute reference. The active-method comparison isolates the relative quality of the routing logic when interventions are allowed, whereas the no-reroute comparison identifies the tradeoff introduced by intervention itself. This separation avoids interpreting a method as universally superior when it improves one metric, such as TTI, while not improving all aggregate quantities relative to the original routes. 

C. Contributions of This Paper 

In this paper, we provide a predictive CAV rerouting framework that combines DCRNN forecasts with a routing policy implemented in SUMO. The forecasts are used to evaluate alternative routes through a cost that accounts for congestion, CO 2 emissions, route length, and exposure to charging demand. For each eligible CAV, the policy evaluates a finite set of feasible alternatives to the original destination and applies a route change when the acceptance conditions are satisfied. Vehicles that are not eligible for rerouting retain their assigned routes. The contributions of this paper are: 1) An integration of directed graph-based traffic forecasting and charging-demand prediction with CAV rerouting in a microscopic traffic simulation environment. 2) A routing procedure that uses predicted conditions to rank feasible alternatives and applies connectivity, route-cost improvement, and route-change checks when accepting a rerouting decision. 3) A comparative evaluation with K-shortest-path routing, predictive-density routing, V2X proactive routing, and a reference scenario without rerouting under matched traffic demand and disruption schedules at five CAV penetration levels. The evaluation examines the trade-offs among travel-time index, average speed, emissions, distance traveled, recorded charging energy, and the number of accepted route changes. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-tion II presents the simulation setting, forecasting and routing methods, and performance measures. Section III presents and discusses the simulation results. Finally, Section IV provides concluding remarks and directions for future research. II. Lower raw values are preferred for reroutes, TTI, CO 2, distance, and the diagnostic EV battery-output aggregate, while higher raw speed is preferred. The radar plot is therefore a relative visualization, not a replacement for the raw values in Table II and the line plots.

Table III provides an internal analysis of the accepted DCRNN reroutes. Across all penetration levels, DCRNN ac-cepted 41 reroutes. Within the DCRNN route-scoring records, these accepted reroutes were associated with cumulative reductions of 403.675 in the TTI objective, 113275.495 in the CO 2 objective, 10.219 in EV charging-risk exposure, and 1387.290 m in route length. The internal reroute records there-fore indicate that the accepted DCRNN route changes were consistent with the objective components used by the proposed routing policy. These values should be interpreted as route-score and candidate-route evidence rather than as independent counterfactual measurements of the network state. The realized network comparison is provided by the no-reroute and active-method results above, whereas Table III explains why the DCRNN policy accepted the specific route changes that it applied. The largest objective reductions occur in the higher penetration cases, where more eligible CAVs are available and the same disruption schedule creates more opportunities for prediction-informed bypasses. The 5% case has more accepted DCRNN route changes because two vehicles receive repeated accepted updates, but the total objective reduction is smaller than in the higher-penetration cases. This reinforces the interpretation that reroute count alone is not the key measure; the route-score change associated with each accepted reroute also matters. The aggregate results should therefore be read as a set of coupled tradeoffs rather than as a single scalar ranking. Predictive-density routing is useful as a low-intervention refer-ence: it changes few routes, but its conservative behavior leaves more vehicles exposed to the disruption-induced congestion. K-shortest-path routing and V2X proactive routing represent higher-intervention alternatives. They create many more route changes, but additional route changes do not necessarily improve network-level outcomes once the new routes interact with the same mixed-traffic demand. DCRNN occupies an in-termediate regime. It performs more reroutes than predictive-density routing but far fewer than K-shortest-path and V2X, while achieving the strongest aggregate TTI and speed among the active methods. This pattern is consistent with the intended role of prediction: it is most useful when it supports selective acceptance rather than unrestricted rerouting. The comparison with no-reroute clarifies the scope of the benefit. Since the no-reroute reference does not deviate vehicles from their originally assigned paths, it can retain lower total distance and lower aggregate emissions. This is not contradictory to the active-method result. The proposed method is intended to reduce congestion severity under disruptions while limiting unnecessary intervention. In the reported runs, this is reflected by the 1.8% average TTI improvement relative to no-reroute and by the lower TTI, higher speed, and lower CO 2 among active methods. The results do not support a claim that rerouting strictly dominates the original routes on every aggregate measure. The safety diagnostic outputs also indicate that CAV rerouting did not introduce CAV involvement in the scripted disruption incidents. These diagnostics are useful for verifying that the routing logic avoided unsafe CAV behavior, but they are not interpreted as the primary performance objective. The main contribution of the proposed method is its ability to improve the congestion–rerouting tradeoff through selective predictive rerouting. The diagnostic nature of these safety rows is important because the scripted disruption events are imposed to create controlled congestion and conflict conditions, not to estimate a network-wide crash rate. Thus, the safety outputs are used to check that the routing logic does not place CAVs into the constructed incident windows, while the mobility and emissions metrics are used to evaluate network performance. Overall, the results support the conclusion that DCRNN provides the strongest congestion–rerouting tradeoff among the evaluated routing methods. DCRNN achieves the lowest average TTI, improves average TTI by approximately 1.8% relative to no-reroute, and uses substantially fewer route changes than K-shortest-path and V2X routing. Among the active routing methods, DCRNN also achieves the lowest aggregate CO 2 emissions and the highest average speed. The no-reroute reference remains lower in aggregate CO 2 and total distance, so the proposed method is not interpreted as improving every metric relative to the uncontrolled case. IV. Conclusion In this paper, we presented a predictive routing framework for CAVs in mixed traffic with EV charging demand. The framework combines DCRNN forecasts with a routing proce-dure that evaluates feasible alternatives using congestion, CO 2

emissions, route length, and charging-demand terms. We eval-uated the framework in SUMO under controlled disruptions at five CAV penetration levels. Across the considered scenarios, DCRNN yielded the lowest average TTI, highest average speed, and lowest aggregate CO 2 emissions among the active routing methods, while accepting fewer route changes than K-shortest-path and V2X routing. The comparison with the no-reroute reference showed an aggregate TTI improvement, although the benefit varied across penetration levels. The reference retained lower aggregate emissions and distance traveled, illustrating the trade-offs associated with routing intervention. Future work will extend the evaluation to larger networks and repeated demand and disruption realizations. We will also examine the individual contributions of traffic forecasting and charging-demand information through controlled compar-isons, and incorporate explicit charging queues and station availability to evaluate the effects of routing decisions on charging service. Additional work should also separate predic-tion quality from routing-policy quality by replaying matched candidate route sets under alternative forecast sources. Such ablations would clarify when the observed benefits are caused by improved congestion prediction, by the multi-objective acceptance rule, or by the interaction between the two. Finally, future experiments should replace the diagnostic EV battery-output aggregate with a verified incremental charging-energy measure so that EV energy and charging service can be evaluated with the same confidence as TTI, speed, CO 2, and distance.
