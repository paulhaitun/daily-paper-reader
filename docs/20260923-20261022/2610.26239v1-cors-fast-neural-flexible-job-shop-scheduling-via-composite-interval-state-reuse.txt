Abstract 

Neural solvers for the Flexible Job-Shop Scheduling Prob-lem (FJSP) typically refresh graph representations and update feasibility after every operation–machine decision. However, only feasibility must remain exact at every step, whereas graph encoding is substantially more expensive and can potentially be refreshed less frequently. We present CoRS, which de-couples them: an interval-level graph representation is reused across multiple sequential decisions, while a stepwise feasi-bility state is updated after every action. This two-timescale design amortizes graph encoding without parallelizing action generation or relaxing scheduling constraints. CoRS further develops a feasibility-preserving composite-interval decod-ing scheme with dynamic constraint tracking and boundary state synchronization, which reduces representation refresh frequency while maintaining continuous feasible schedule construction. Furthermore, to alleviate representation stale-ness caused by interval-level state reuse, CoRS introduces enhanced static features as a compensation mechanism. By providing more comprehensive structural information of the scheduling problem, these features improve the model’s per-ception of overall problem characteristics. Extensive exper-iments on synthetic and multiple public FJSP benchmarks demonstrate that CoRS significantly reduces inference time while maintaining competitive solution quality, achieving fa-vorable speed–quality trade-offs under tight wall-clock bud-gets and low-latency scenarios. 

# Introduction 

With the advent of the Industry 4.0 era, the rapid devel-opment of information technologies such as the Internet of Things (IoT) (Li, Xu, and Zhao 2015) and artificial intelli-gence (AI) (Mao et al. 2019) has accelerated the digitaliza-tion of manufacturing systems, making cloud manufacturing an important mechanism for modern manufacturing (Bo-Hu et al. 2010; Ren et al. 2017). As a core issue in manufactur-ing systems (Liu, Chen, and Chou 2015), scheduling prob-lems require efficient decision-making based on cloud data. With the increasing scale and complexity of manufactur-ing systems, the response speed of scheduling decisions has

become a critical challenge in cloud manufacturing environ-ments (Kovacova and Lăzăroiu 2021; Zhang et al. 2025). As a classical NP-hard Combinatorial Optimization Prob-lem (COP) (Xie et al. 2019), the Flexible Job-Shop Schedul-ing Problem (FJSP) has become a modeling prototype for diverse applications, including medical resource schedul-ing (Abderrabi et al. 2021), semiconductor manufactur-ing (Ghaedy-Heidary et al. 2024), and aero-engine produc-tion (Zhou, Yang, and Zheng 2019), owing to its enhanced flexibility (Chaudhry and Khan 2016). In practical manufac-turing systems, scheduling systems are required to respond rapidly to changes in production states within limited time. Consequently, efficiently solving the FJSP has become a cen-tral challenge (Akbaripour et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2025). Given the significance of FJSP, numerous methods have been proposed, including exact algorithms (Tutumlu and Saraç 2023), Priority Dispatching Rules (PDRs) (Branke, Hildebrandt, and Scholz-Reiter 2015), and metaheuris-tics (Xu et al. 2025). However, these methods suffer from lim-itations in solution quality, generalization, and computational efficiency (Xiao et al. 2025; Han and Yang 2021; Zhang et al. 2023), making them inadequate to meet the requirements of cloud manufacturing. Recently, deep learning has intro-duced new methods for solving FJSP. Neural combinatorial optimization methods show strong modeling and decision-making capabilities for scheduling problems (Wang, Guo, and Liu 2025; Liao et al. 2025; Smit et al. 2025). Most studies formulate the scheduling process as a Markov De-cision Process (MDP), adopt autoregressive (AR) decision-making, and integrate reinforcement learning for end-to-end optimization to obtain high-quality solutions (Yuan et al. 2024; Zhao and Deng 2024). However, most existing neural FJSP solvers primarily fo-cus on state representation learning or backbone architecture design (Xu, Li, and Li 2024), while implicitly assuming that the complete graph representation must be refreshed after every scheduling decision. In fact, the global state usually undergoes only limited changes between consecutive deci-sion steps (see Appendix A for analysis). So existing meth-ods perform complete feature encoding for highly similar states, resulting in substantial computation and significantly degrading solution efficiency. Motivated by this observation, we question this assump-tion and explore a scheduling approach that reduces state up-arXiv:2610.26239v1 [cs.AI] 19 Oct 2026 dates while preserving dynamic feasibility. We propose the Composite Regressive (CR) mechanism, which redefines the state update process in neural FJSP solving. Unlike existing methods that implicitly assume the complete graph repre-sentation must be refreshed after every scheduling decision, CR introduces representation-refresh frequency as an under-explored design dimension and formulates a two-timescale state evolution mechanism. Specifically, CR factorizes the scheduling state into two coupled components with differ-ent update frequencies: an interval-level representation state and a stepwise feasibility state. The interval-level represen-tation state is refreshed only at composite-interval bound-aries, while the stepwise feasibility state maintains dynamic constraints and ensures schedule feasibility by progressively updating the feasible action set. Based on this mechanism, we further develop the 

Co mposite Regressive Scheduling (CoRS) model for solv-ing FJSP. Unlike blockwise or parallel autoregressive meth-ods, CoRS does not reduce the number of action-generation steps; it reduces the number of representation-refresh steps. Unlike naive representation caching, CoRS explicitly tracks the evolving feasibility state and periodically reconstructs the full scheduling state. By reusing interval-level graph rep-resentations across consecutive scheduling decisions, CoRS reduces the number of graph encoding operations from T

to ⌈T /X ⌉, while maintaining scheduling feasibility through the progressive update of the stepwise feasibility state. However, reducing the frequency of state refreshes may cause the interval-level representations to fail to timely cap-ture certain dynamic changes, thereby affecting the quality of action ranking. To alleviate this issue, CoRS introduces enhanced static features as a compensation mechanism. By providing more comprehensive structural information of the scheduling problem, these features improve the model’s per-ception of global scheduling characteristics and enhance the effectiveness of interval representations under low-frequency updates. Meanwhile, state synchronization at interval bound-aries further limits the accumulation of representation stal-eness, ensuring that its impact is mainly restricted to the ranking among feasible actions rather than compromising scheduling feasibility. Specifically, we first propose a two-timescale state evolu-tion mechanism that decouples the scheduling state into an interval-level representation state and a stepwise feasibility state. The interval-level representation state adopts compos-ite intervals as the state refresh units rather than traditional decision steps, and is updated only at composite-interval boundaries. By reusing interval-level state representations across consecutive scheduling decisions, this mechanism re-duces encoding of complete graph representations and alle-viates the inference overhead caused by frequent state up-dates. Meanwhile, the stepwise feasibility state continuously tracks dynamic constraint changes, ensuring the feasibility of sequential schedule construction. Furthermore, we intro-duce enhanced static features as a compensation mechanism for interval-level state reuse. By providing more compre-hensive structural information of the scheduling problem, these features improve the model’s perception of overall problem characteristics and mitigate representation staleness caused by low-frequency state refreshing. Extensive experi-ments conducted against four state-of-the-art methods (Wang et al. 2023; Zhao et al. 2025; Song et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2026) and four well-known PDRs demonstrate that CoRS can generate competitive solutions while significantly improving inference speed. The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows: 

• We expose representation-refresh frequency as an under-explored design axis in neural FJSP solving and formulate a two-timescale state evolution that decouples interval-level graph representations from stepwise feasibility up-dates. 

• We develop a feasibility-preserving composite-interval decoding scheme with dynamic constraint tracking and boundary state synchronization. It reduces graph-encoder invocations from T to ⌈T /X ⌉ while retaining sequential and feasible schedule construction. 

• We provide same-backbone and interaction studies that isolate the effect of representation reuse and show that enhanced static features increasingly compensate for rep-resentation staleness as the interval length grows. Fixed-budget comparisons further demonstrate the practical quality–latency trade-off. 

# Related Work 

Traditional methods for solving FJSP mainly include exact methods, PDRs, and metaheuristic algorithms. Exact meth-ods construct FJSP as a mathematical model and explore the entire solution space to find the optimal solution, such as branch and bound (Brucker, Jurisch, and Sievers 1994), integer programming (Meng et al. 2020), and constraint pro-gramming (Müller et al. 2022). PDRs directly guide the scheduling decision process using manually designed pri-ority rules, such as the Shortest Processing Time (SPT) rule (Chen and Matis 2013) and the Most WorK Remain-ing (MWKR) rule (Branke, Hildebrandt, and Scholz-Reiter 2015; Sels, Gheysen, and Vanhoucke 2012). Metaheuris-tic algorithms iteratively explore the solution space through carefully designed search strategies to obtain high-quality solutions, such as genetic algorithms (Gao et al. 2019) and particle swarm optimization (Nouiri et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2009). However, exact methods struggle to handle large-scale instances (Demir and İşleyen 2013; Zhang et al. 2023). PDRs based on fixed heuristics fail to capture complex scheduling patterns, resulting in low solution quality (Chen and Matis 2013). Metaheuristic algorithms require extensive iterative searches, incurring high computational costs (Caldeira and Gnanavelbabu 2019). Furthermore, traditional methods typ-ically solve each unseen instance from scratch, which limits their generalization ability. In recent years, deep learning-based neural solvers have received increasing attention for solving FJSP. To enhance the expressive power for complex scheduling problems, ex-isting neural FJSP solvers often incorporate graph neural networks (Zeng, Li, and Bai 2022) or Transformer-based architectures (Xu, Li, and Li 2024) to model operation– machine interactions and precedence dependencies.Very re-cently,ReSched (Xiao et al. 2026) explored simplified state representations for Transformer-based FJSP solving and re-ported promising results. Parallel autoregressive methods, such as PARCO (Berto et al. 2026) and MACSIM (Luttmann and Xie 2025), alleviate the sequential dependency of con-ventional autoregressive approaches by predicting multiple actions within a single decoding stage. These methods mainly accelerate scheduling from the perspective of action gen-eration. However, existing solvers typically still require the complete graph-state representation to be refreshed after each decision. Such frequent representation updates and environ-ment interactions introduce substantial computational over-head, significantly increasing inference time. As the scale of FJSP grows, inference time increases linearly with the num-ber of time steps (Demir and İşleyen 2013), thereby exposing significant efficiency bottlenecks in large-scale or real-time scheduling scenarios. Recently, some studies have explored fast solving ap-proaches based on fixed state representations, such as non-autoregressive (NAR) methods. NAR methods have been widely applied to the traveling salesman problem (Dat et al. 2025) and the vehicle routing problem (Xiao et al. 2024b). However, NAR models generally suffer from degraded so-lution quality (Joshi, Laurent, and Bresson 2019). This lim-itation mainly arises because NAR models make decisions based on static state representations, making it difficult to capture the dynamic changes in scheduling states caused by sequential decisions. As a result, scheduling solutions can be generated efficiently, but they often deviate from high-quality solutions (Xiao et al. 2024a). In FJSP, the relation-ships among nodes are highly complex and the scheduling state changes continuously with decisions, making it difficult to complete all decisions under a fixed state representation. Consequently, to our knowledge, fully non-autoregressive neural construction methods that directly generate complete feasible FJSP schedules remain underexplored; existing fast-learning or two-stage approaches either decompose the prob-lem, rely on solver-assisted post-processing, or still construct schedules sequentially. Overall, existing neural FJSP solvers mainly focus on state representation learning and network architecture design, while representation-refresh frequency, as an important de-sign dimension affecting inference efficiency, remains largely unexplored. Although fixed state representation methods can reduce update overhead, they struggle to adapt to dynami-cally changing feasibility constraints in FJSP. To address this problem, we propose the CoRS, which decouples interval-level representation reuse and stepwise feasibility updates through a two-timescale state evolution design, reducing state refresh overhead while maintaining dynamic feasibility dur-ing scheduling. This makes CoRS complementary to recent representation- and backbone-level advances in neural FJSP solving. 

# Preliminaries 

This section introduces the CR mechanism and presents the formulation of the FJSP. 

Composite regressive mechanism. The CR mechanism adopts composite intervals as the update units for graph-state representations, rather than re-encoding the complete graph representation after each scheduling decision. Within each composite interval, CoRS reuses the interval-level graph-state representation and only updates the stepwise feasibility state to track dynamically changing constraints during scheduling. At composite-interval boundaries, the interval-level graph-state representation is synchronously re-constructed, reducing redundant graph encoding overhead and preventing outdated information from accumulating due to infrequent representation updates. Through this two-timescale state evolution mechanism, CoRS reduces graph-state refresh frequency and inference overhead while en-suring that action selection satisfies dynamic constraints through stepwise feasibility updates, thereby achieving a bet-ter efficiency–quality trade-off. Under the CR mechanism, the entire FJSP scheduling pro-cess is divided into multiple consecutive, non-overlapping composite intervals B. Bτ denotes the τ -th composite inter-val, which contains X time steps and is defined by: 

Bτ = {t | (τ − 1) X + 1 ≤ t ≤ min( τ X, t final )} (1) where τ = 1 , 2, . . . , τ max , tfinal denotes the final time step of the entire scheduling process, and τmax represents the total number of composite intervals. 

Flexible job-shop scheduling problem. The FJSP is de-fined as follows. Given a set of n jobs J = {J1, J 2, . . . , J n}

and a set of m machines M = {M1, M 2, . . . , M m}, each job Ji consists of ni operations Oi = {Oi1, O i2, . . . , O in i }.Each operation Oij has a compatible machine set Mij ⊆ M

and can be processed on any machine Mk ∈ Mij with pro-cessing time pijk , where the completion time is denoted by 

Cij . The objective is to minimize the maximum completion time Cmax = max {Cin i | i = 1 , 2, . . . , n }. The scheduling process must satisfy the following constraints: the opera-tions of each job must follow their precedence constraints, and each machine can process at most one operation at a time with non-preemptive processing. 

# Methodology 

This section introduces the CoRS model, the scheduling model based on the CR mechanism, which achieves efficient inference for the FJSP. Figure 1 illustrates the workflow of CoRS. Through a specially designed interval encoding net-work and decoding algorithm, CoRS generates high-quality scheduling policies while significantly reducing inference time. In the following, we sequentially present the MDP, the feature encoding scheme, the interval encoding network, the composite-interval decoding algorithm, and the reinforce-ment learning algorithm.

Feature Encoding 

Unlike conventional methods that re-encode state represen-tations at each decision step, CoRS reduces redundant en-coding operations by reusing interval-level representation state within composite intervals. Therefore, the state rep-resentation obtained from a single encoding process needs to incorporate more instance-related static information to support effective decision making over subsequent multiple scheduling steps. Based on this consideration, we propose an enhanced static feature encoding scheme. Specifically, given an FJSP instance as input, we encode it into operation features hij ∈ R12 , machine features hk ∈ R10 , and OM pair features h(ij,k ) ∈ R8. We enhance the static features by incorporating information such as the average processing time of successor operations and the total number of fastest operations for each machine, as detailed in Appendix B.

# Experiments 

This section describes the experimental setup, reports results on synthetic and public datasets, and analyzes the impact of the composite interval time step X as well as the ablation results. 

Experimental Settings 

Baselines. The four selected PDRs are described as fol-lows: FIFO schedules the OM pair that becomes available first; MOPNR schedules the OM pair whose operation has the largest number of remaining successor operations; SPT schedules the OM pair with the minimum processing time; MWKR schedules the OM pair with the maximum remain-ing average processing time. The three selected baselines are described as follows: HGNN (Song et al. 2022) models jobs and machines with a heterogeneous graph neural network to capture the relationships between operations and machines; DANIEL (Wang et al. 2023) adopts a reinforcement learning framework with a dual-attention network to improve solu-tion quality; DOAGNN (Zhao et al. 2025) decouples the disjunctive graph into a dual graph structure and applies a dual operation aggregation graph neural network to repre-sent operation states and their dependencies;ReSched (Xiao et al. 2026) proposes a Transformer-based simplified state representation framework, improving solution quality. 

Datasets. We use n × m to denote an FJSP instance with 

n jobs and m machines. The model is trained on randomly generated instances of sizes 10 × 5, 20 × 5, 15 × 10 , and 

20 × 10 , and evaluated on two synthetic datasets (SD1, SD2) and four public datasets (Hurink, Barnes, Brandimarte, and Dauzere). Detailed dataset descriptions are provided in Ap-pendix D. Implementation details are in Appendix E. 

Experiment Results and Analysis 

Performance Comparison Table 6 (Appendix F) and Ta-ble 7 (Appendix G) report the performance of CoRS and baseline methods on the synthetic and public datasets. LB denotes the known optimal solution for public datasets, while OR-Tools denotes results obtained by the Google OR-Tools exact solver on synthetic datasets with a time limit of 1800 s. Metrics include makespan ( Cmax ), relative gap to LB or OR-Tools (Gap), and average solving time per instance (Time/s). G and S denote the greedy and sampling strategies, respec-tively, where the sampling strategy performs 100 samples by default. On the synthetic datasets, CoRS achieves significantly faster inference than all AR baselines. When X = 2 , the speed improves by 42.94%–83.14%; when X = 5, the improvement further increases to 154.46%–238.76%. On the public benchmark datasets, CoRS achieves a 160.14%– 489.86% speedup at X = 5 . This acceleration mainly bene-fits from the CR mechanism, which reuses state encoding in a NAR manner within composite intervals, thereby substan-tially reducing state encoding operations. While maintaining efficient inference, CoRS still produces high-quality solutions. When X = 5 , the average gap to the best model on SD1 is only 4.04%, which further reduces to 0.71% on the public benchmarks. Compared with PDRs, CoRS also achieves an 18.27%–41.18% improvement with comparable solving time. These results mainly benefit from the enhanced static features. Although increasing X reduces the model’s perception of dynamic information and degrades solution quality, the enhanced static features can still effec-tively represent the global state, thereby maintaining stable performance.

On SD2, CoRS im-proves solution quality by 64.34% over PDRs while achiev-ing a 129.85% inference speedup. These results demonstrate the strong generalization capability of CoRS. This is mainly due to the stable composite-interval state modeling of the in-terval encoding network and the effective constraints of the composite-interval decoding algorithm, allowing the model to maintain high inference efficiency and solution quality as the scale increases.

Impact of Composite Interval Steps (X) We analyze the effect of X on model performance using 10 ×5 instances from SD1, normalizing all metrics relative to the AR case ( X = 1 ). As shown in Figure 4 (Appendix J), increasing X substan-tially reduces inference time, which is only 22.1%–23% of AR at X = 10 , while solution quality remains stable, vary-ing by 3.3%–7.6%. This is primarily attributed to the pro-posed CR mechanism, which reduces inference time by de-creasing state encoding, while the combination of enhanced static features with dynamic features maintains stable per-formance at larger time steps. In practical applications, the selection of X should be adjusted according to the trade-off between inference efficiency and solution quality. For sce-narios with stringent real-time requirements, a larger X can be adopted to reduce the frequency of state encoding and decrease inference latency. In contrast, for scenarios where solution quality is prioritized, a smaller X should be selected to increase the frequency of state updates and maintain high-quality scheduling solutions.

# Conclusion 

This paper proposes the Composite Regressive (CR) mecha-nism and develops the CoRS scheduling model based on this mechanism for solving FJSP. CoRS adopts a two-timescale state evolution mechanism that decouples the interval-level representation state from the stepwise feasibility state. Fur-thermore, CoRS designs a feasibility-preserving composite-interval decoding scheme , reducing state refresh overhead while maintaining sequential and feasible schedule construc-tion. In addition, enhanced static features are introduced to al-leviate representation staleness caused by interval-level state reuse. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CoRS signifi-cantly reduces inference time while maintaining competitive solution quality, achieving superior speed–quality trade-offs under tight time budgets and low-latency scenarios. A current limitation is that the composite interval length is manually selected. The current method assumes a static determinis-tic FJSP and an exact stepwise feasibility tracker. Moreover, stale representations may still affect the ranking among fea-sible actions, and the achievable speedup depends on graph encoding being a major component of inference cost. Fu-ture work will study adaptive interval selection and extend composite interval state reuse to stronger Transformer-based FJSP backbones for improved speed–quality trade-offs across instance scales and distributions.
