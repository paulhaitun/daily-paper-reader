ABSTRACT
Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have achieved strong performance in visual
perception and spatial reasoning, leading to growing interest in using them as
high-level planners for embodied AI. However, physical tasks require models to
integrate spatial information, reason over temporal states, and generate multi-step
plans under various constraints, raising the question of whether current VLMs
can reliably support such reasoning and planning independently of execution.
We introduce Plan-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating the high-level reason-
ing and multi-step planning capabilities of VLMs relevant to embodied worlds,
while isolating the influence of action execution. It covers three complemen-
tary dimensions, spanning 16 subclasses and 699 curated samples.
Extensive
evaluation of 28 VLMs reveals room for improvement, with even the strongest
model achieving only 75.0% accuracy. We further find that model scaling gen-
erally improves these capabilities.
Ablation studies also indicate larger mod-
els show increasing potential as high-level planners. Overall, Plan-Bench pro-
vides a lightweight probe for evaluating VLMs’ reasoning and planning capa-
bilities relevant to embodied environments and assessing their potential as de-
cision makers for physical tasks.

INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) (OpenAI, 2026, Qwen Team, 2026a, Diao et al.,
2026d;b) have substantially strengthened their capabilities in visual perception and spatial reasoning,
leading to growing interest in using them as high-level planners for embodied AI (Zhang et al., 2026,
Liu et al., 2026, Yang et al., 2026), particularly in complex physical tasks. In such settings, mod-
els often need to integrate spatial information, reason over evolving temporal states, and generate
multi-step plans under various constraints. These demands expose an important and underexplored
challenge: can existing VLMs go well beyond perception to reason and plan in embodied worlds?
Notably, existing benchmarks have not evaluated these capabilities of VLMs for embodied tasks. As
shown in Figure 1, multimodal reasoning benchmarks (Fu et al., 2026, Yao et al., 2026) primarily
focus on visual information. Embodied benchmarks (Fan et al., 2026, Han et al., 2026) mainly
evaluate embodied tasks, mixing errors from decisions and execution. LongAct (Zhu et al., 2026)
attempts to reduce the influence of control and better assess decision making, but evaluates agents
rather than VLMs. It therefore remains unclear whether VLMs can perform reliable reasoning and
planning in embodied scenarios when high-level decisions are separated from low-level execution.
To bridge this gap, we introduce Plan-Bench, a benchmark designed to evaluate VLMs’ high-level
reasoning and multi-step planning relevant to embodied tasks. As illustrated on the right side of
Figure 1, Plan-Bench systematically covers three dimensions: Spatial Cognition, Temporal Reason-
ing, and Multi-Step Planning, spanning 16 subclasses and 699 samples. It is constructed through
a three-stage pipeline consisting of task definition, dual-source visual data acquisition, and man-
ual QA construction with quality assurance, resulting in a diverse and carefully curated benchmark

RELATED WORK
2.1
MULTIMODAL REASONING BENCHMARKS
Existing benchmarks have gradually evolved from perception toward complex reasoning. MME (Fu
et al., 2026) evaluates both perceptual and cognitive abilities, while LENS (Yao et al., 2026) or-
ganizes multimodal evaluation from understanding to reasoning. Recent benchmarks such as Vi-
suLogic (Xu et al., 2026b), MIRA (Zhou et al., 2026), and PuzzleWorld (Li et al., 2026a) further
explore chain-of-thought and open-ended multi-step reasoning. Beyond single-image reasoning,
MuirBench (Wang et al., 2025a), MMIU (Meng et al., 2025), EgoPlan-Bench2 (Qiu et al., 2026),
among others (Li et al., 2026b, Meng et al., 2025) extend evaluation to multiple images or frames,
covering temporal changes and long-range visual evidence. These benchmarks broaden the scope
and complexity of multimodal reasoning. However, existing benchmarks rarely target the reason-

2.2
MULTI-STEP TASKS IN EMBODIED ENVIRONMENTS
Recent embodied systems increasingly employ VLMs (Diao et al., 2024, Diao et al.) as decision
makers. Harness VLA (Zhang et al., 2026), Goal2Skill (Liu et al., 2026), and HiVLA (Yang et al.,
2026), among others (Shi et al., 2025, Li et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2025c), adopt hierarchical frame-
works that separate high-level reasoning and planning from low-level execution. Meanwhile, several
benchmarks have begun to evaluate these capabilities in embodied settings. EmbodiedBench (Yang
et al., 2025b) and RoboCerebra (Han et al., 2026) evaluate embodied agents through interactive tasks
or hierarchical VLM-VLA systems, where performance jointly reflects reasoning, grounding, and
action execution. RoboSPA (Fan et al., 2026) further evaluates VLA models on reasoning and pro-
cedural planning, but still couples reasoning with low-level execution. LongAct (Zhu et al., 2026)
attempts to reduce the influence of control to better assess planning, yet evaluates complete embod-
ied agents rather than VLMs alone. Plan-Bench instead decouples embodied action execution and
directly evaluates VLMs’ reasoning and planning by constructing a benchmark of 699 questions,
providing a lightweight interface for isolating the capabilities required by embodied tasks.
3
PLAN-BENCH
We introduce Plan-Bench, a benchmark designed to systematically evaluate the reasoning and plan-
ning capabilities of VLMs in embodied tasks. In this section, we first describe the construction
pipeline, then present the details of data distribution, and finally introduce the evaluation protocol.
3.1
CONSTRUCTION PIPELINE
To ensure high quality and validity, we adopt a task-driven, multi-stage construction pipeline, as
illustrated in Figure 2. The overall process consists of three stages:
Capability Taxonomy and Task Definition. We first organize the capabilities required for rea-
soning and planning in embodied environments into a taxonomy. The taxonomy comprises three
core categories corresponding to Spatial Cognition, Temporal Reasoning, and Multi-Step Planning.
Based on this taxonomy, we further define a set of subtasks, balancing category diversity with task
relevance to the targeted capabilities. This step provides explicit guidance for data acquisition, de-
termining what types of visual evidence and task conditions should be collected or constructed.
Dual-Source Data Acquisition. We collect visual data from two sources. For subclasses requiring
precise control over factors such as scene layout and viewpoint, we employ controlled-generation
platforms such as Three.js (Danchilla, 2012) and OmniGibson (Li et al., 2023) to configure task-
relevant variables for consistent construction. For those that benefit from more natural visual distri-
butions, we select suitable images from existing datasets such as CLEVR (Johnson et al., 2017) and
Captaincook4d (Peddi et al., 2024), among others (Yang et al., 2019, Dai et al., 2026, Ding et al.,
2023, Lin et al., 2020), to increase visual variety and capture greater real-world scene complexity.

QA Annotation and Quality Assurance. Based on the acquired images, we manually construct
2,500+ questions and answers according to the task specification of each subclass. All samples
are then independently reviewed by three annotators who are not involved in the initial annotation
process. The review considers answer correctness, image-QA consistency, and relevance to the
corresponding task definition. A sample is accepted only when three reviewers agree that it satisfies
all criteria. Finally, this process yields 699 high-quality questions to construct Plan-Bench.
3.2
DATA DISTRIBUTION
Plan-Bench comprises 699 instances, including 183 single-choice questions and 516 open-ended
questions. Our benchmark covers 16 subtasks across three primary categories, providing a compre-
4


hensive evaluation of VLMs’ reasoning and planning capabilities in diverse embodied task settings.
Figure 3 presents representative examples from each category in Plan-Bench. The design motiva-
tions and task compositions of these three categories are detailed below:
Spatial Cognition. This category contains 259 instances across six subtasks: Occlusion Order,
Mirror Distinction, Action Recovery, Target Discovery, State Recognition, and Spatial Relations. It
is designed to reflect embodied settings where VLMs may observe the same scene through multiple
cameras or viewpoints and must integrate these observations to form a coherent understanding of
the environment. It also considers challenging spatial cues such as mirrors and reflections, requiring
VLMs to distinguish reflected content from physical entities and reason correctly about the scene.
Temporal Reasoning. This category contains 210 instances across five subtasks: Progress Track-
ing, Temporal Ordering, Persistent Counting, Instruction Alignment, and Motion Retrieval. This
category includes many egocentric multi-image sequences, designed to reflect embodied tasks such
as cooking and navigation, where VLMs must reason over temporally evolving observations from a
first-person perspective. It evaluates whether VLMs can understand temporal dependencies across
sequential observations, including ordering events, tracking target actions or states, and identifying
recurring objects across frames. These tasks further assess whether models can preserve relevant
historical context and maintain consistent reasoning throughout extended embodied interactions.
Multi-Step Planning. This category contains 230 instances across five subtasks: Constraint Se-
quencing, Capacity Planning, Rearrangement Planning, Dependency Planning, and Route Plan-
ning. It reflects embodied scenarios where VLMs must first reason about task goals, scene con-
straints, and action dependencies to produce plans before execution. It evaluates whether VLMs can
translate such information into action sequences toward a target. Successful planning may indicate
the potential of VLMs to serve as more stable high-level decision makers in physical systems.
3.3
EVALUATION PROTOCOL
We evaluate model responses using an LLM-as-judge approach. Many open-ended questions in
Plan-Bench admit multiple valid answers due to equivalent object references, alternative orderings,
or other semantically equivalent forms. To handle such cases, we pair these samples with answer
hints that specify additional valid alternatives or equivalence conditions beyond the ground-truth
answer, such as valid order swaps, position exchanges, or alternative object names. DeepSeek-V4-
Flash (Xu et al., 2026a) serves as the judge, taking the model output, ground-truth answer, and
answer hint as input to determine whether the response should be considered correct under these
acceptable alternatives. To validate the reliability of the evaluation process, we randomly sample 200
instances for manual verification, and the judge achieves 100% agreement with human evaluation
on these samples. The full judge prompt is provided in the Appendix B.2 for reproducibility.

4.3
ABLATION STUDY
Strong overall performance does not imply consistent fine-grained capabilities. Figure 4 reveals
variation across the 16 subtasks. Even the strongest models exhibit weaknesses on individual tasks.
Qwen3.8-Max, for example, exceeds 70% accuracy on several subtasks but reaches only 38.0% on
Persistent Counting, while Kimi-K3 drops to 10.0% on Rearrangement Planning despite strong per-
formance elsewhere. Models with similar overall accuracy may still differ across tasks. Qwen3.5-2B
and Ovis2.5-9B achieve comparable overall scores, yet differ considerably across Progress Track-
ing, Spatial Relations, Constraint Sequencing, and Capacity Planning. Overall, strong or similar
aggregate performance can still coexist with task-specific weaknesses and capability differences.
More views do not necessarily lead to better spatial cognition. Current VLMs remain suscepti-
ble to interference from misleading viewpoints. We reorganize Spatial Relations under the Spatial

LIMITATIONS AND DISCUSSION
We construct Plan-Bench to evaluate the reasoning and planning capabilities of VLMs in embod-
ied tasks across Spatial Cognition, Temporal Reasoning, and Multi-step Planning, while several
limitations remain to be further explored and addressed. (i) Continually evolving frontier models.
Although we have evaluated diverse models, frontier systems evolve rapidly and performance may
change across versions. Future work will track newly released models to assess progress in these
capabilities. (ii) Limited data scale and task coverage. Plan-Bench includes 699 samples across 16
subcategories, while there is still room to expand both the dataset scale and task diversity. Further
expansion will enable a more comprehensive evaluation of reasoning and planning. (iii) Potential
errors in LLM-as-judge. Our LLM-as-judge achieves 100% agreement with human evaluation on
200 sampled cases, but some errors may still exist in the remaining samples. We will further explore
more robust and programmatic evaluation procedures to improve evaluation accuracy and reliability.
6
CONCLUSION
We introduce Plan-Bench, a lightweight and systematic benchmark for evaluating the high-level
reasoning and multi-step planning capabilities of VLMs in embodied scenarios independently of
action execution. Plan-Bench covers Spatial Cognition, Temporal Reasoning, and Multi-Step Plan-
ning, spanning 16 subclasses and 699 curated samples. Evaluation of 28 VLMs shows that current
models still have room for improvement, with the best-performing model achieving only 75.0%
accuracy. Ablation studies further reveal that strong overall performance does not guarantee con-
sistent capabilities across subtasks, and that models remain sensitive to misleading evidence and
prone to hallucinations during reasoning. We also find that scaling generally improves results, while
architecture and training may also influence performance, with Thinking bringing additional gains,
particularly for Multi-Step Planning. Our results suggest that larger VLMs show greater potential as
high-level planners, but reliable reasoning and planning across diverse embodied tasks remain chal-
lenging. Overall, Plan-Bench provides a principled and easy-to-deploy testbed for tracking progress
in these capabilities and assessing VLMs as high-level decision makers for embodied AI.

A.1
TASK DEFINITIONS
Plan-Bench consists of 16 subtasks that instantiate different reasoning and planning challenges in
embodied scenarios. Below, we provide detailed definitions of each subtask and clarify the specific
capability evaluated by each task.
Occlusion Order (OO). Given a scene containing partially occluded objects, the model is required
to infer their relative ordering from visual occlusion cues. This task evaluates whether the model
can reason about object ordering when complete object boundaries are not directly observable.
Mirror Distinction (MD). The model must distinguish real objects from their reflections and cor-
rectly reason about the scene content. The task is designed to evaluate whether the model can
distinguish real objects from reflections and correctly handle reflection-induced visual ambiguity.
Action Recovery (AR). Given partial information from one viewpoint, the model is asked to recover
the corresponding information that is missing or unclear from another viewpoint. This task examines
whether the model can compare observations across multiple views and integrate complementary
visual cues to reconstruct the complete scene information.
Target Discovery (TD). The model must reason about the target’s spatial relationship under mirror
reflections and plan a feasible path toward it. This task evaluates spatial reasoning and path planning
in reflection-ambiguous scenes.
State Recognition (SR). Given multiple visual observations of a scene, the model must integrate
information while identifying redundantly observed content. This task evaluates multi-view scene
understanding and the ability to distinguish new evidence from repeated information.
Spatial Relations (SpR). The model must infer spatial relationships between specified entities from
one or multiple views. The task evaluates spatial reasoning and the ability to integrate relevant
evidence while ignoring misleading observations.
Progress Tracking (PT). Given a sequence depicting an ongoing activity, the model is required to
determine the current progress or stage of the activity. This task evaluates whether the model can
track how an embodied procedure evolves over time.
Temporal Ordering (TO). The model must determine the correct temporal order of events, actions,
or scene states from sequential visual evidence. The task evaluates the ability to recover temporal
dependencies and distinguish earlier events from later ones.
Persistent Counting (PC). The model is asked to count target entities across a visual sequence
while maintaining their identity over time. This task evaluates whether the model can accumulate
information across frames without repeatedly counting the same persistent entity.
Instruction Alignment (IA). Given an instruction together with a sequence of visual observations,
the model must identify the observation that corresponds to the specified instruction. The task
evaluates the alignment between language-described actions and their visual realization over time.
Motion Retrieval (MR). The model is required to identify the motion or action segment that
matches a specified query from a temporal sequence. This task evaluates whether the model can
recognize and retrieve task-relevant motion patterns among multiple observed actions.
Constraint Sequencing (CS). Given a set of actions together with ordering constraints, the model
must construct a valid action sequence satisfying all specified constraints. The task evaluates multi-
step planning under explicit requirements on the relative order of actions.
Capacity Planning (CP). The model is required to produce a feasible plan under capacity limita-
tions on containers or other task-relevant entities. This task evaluates whether the model can jointly
reason about action ordering and limited capacity when constructing a multi-step solution.
Rearrangement Planning (RP). Given an initial and a target configuration, the model must de-
termine a sequence of actions that transforms the scene into the desired arrangement. The task
evaluates planning capabilities and the consequences of successive rearrangement operations.

A.3
DATASET FILTERING PROCEDURE
We perform a multi-stage filtering procedure to improve the quality and reliability of Plan-Bench.
Starting from 2,580 candidate QA pairs, we remove samples that fail one or more quality require-
ments and finally obtain 699 validated samples. The entire filtering process is conducted before
model evaluation and is independent of model performance.
Each candidate sample is examined according to five quality considerations: (1) visual sufficiency,
where the answer must be determinable from the provided visual evidence; (2) annotation correct-
ness, including the validity of reference answers and answer choices; (3) capability alignment, en-
suring that the sample measures the intended reasoning or planning ability; (4) redundancy control,
which removes highly correlated samples originating from similar scenes, videos, or viewpoints; and
(5) other quality issues identified during manual review. The detailed statistics of removed samples
are reported in Table 7.
For samples collected from videos or multi-view environments, redundancy filtering is performed
at the scene level rather than only at the QA level. Samples sharing the same underlying scene,
highly similar viewpoints, or overlapping temporal segments are grouped together. When multiple
samples evaluate equivalent reasoning patterns, only the highest-quality instance is retained to avoid
over-representing specific environments.
To examine whether filtering introduces unintended changes in benchmark composition, we com-
pare the category distribution before and after filtering. As shown in Table 6, the retention ratios
remain similar across Spatial Cognition, Temporal Reasoning, and Multi-Step Planning. The final
benchmark maintains coverage across all three dimensions and 16 subtasks while removing low-
quality or redundant samples.

B.1
MODEL PROMPT TEMPLATES
Since Plan-Bench contains both single-choice and open-ended questions, we implement a unified
prompting function to support both question types within the same evaluation pipeline. As shown

prompting framework while preserving the output requirements of different question types.

B.2
LLM-AS-JUDGE PROMPT
We use DeepSeek-V4-Flash as an LLM-based judge to evaluate model responses. For each sample,
the judge receives the model output, ground-truth answer, and an optional answer hint specify-
ing additional valid alternatives or equivalence conditions. A response is labeled True when it is
semantically equivalent to the ground-truth answer or satisfies an equivalence condition explicitly
defined by the answer hint; otherwise, it is labeled False. The judge is instructed to evaluate only
answer correctness and to return no additional explanation.
System Prompt (Answer Equivalence Judge)
You are a precise answer-equivalence judge for evaluating model responses.
Your task is to determine whether a model output should be considered correct with respect to
the ground-truth answer and the provided answer hint.
The ground-truth answer specifies the reference answer, while the answer hint provides
additional valid alternatives or equivalence conditions that should be accepted.
Judge the model output according to the following rules:
1. Semantic Equivalence: Return True if the model output expresses the same answer as
the ground-truth answer, even if the wording or formatting differs.
2. Answer-Hint Equivalence: Return True if the model output satisfies any valid alterna-
tive or equivalence condition explicitly specified in the answer hint, including acceptable
order swaps, position exchanges, alternative object names, or equivalent action descrip-
tions.
3. Partial Correctness: Return False if the response is only partially correct, omits re-
quired information, or contains an incorrect component.
4. Contradictory or Additional Content: Return False if the response contains informa-
tion that contradicts the ground-truth answer or violates the conditions specified by the
answer hint.

B.3
ANSWER HINT AND EQUIVALENCE RULES
Answer hints handle multiple valid answers in open-ended questions. Among the open-ended
questions in Plan-Bench, 486 samples require answer hints across 13 subcategories, including Oc-
clusion Order, Mirror Distinction, State Recognition, Rearrangement Planning, and Dependency
Planning. These hints specify valid equivalence conditions beyond the reference answer, such as
interchangeable object references, flexible orderings, position exchanges, and equivalent action se-
quences. As illustrated in Figure 10, the answer hint is provided only to the LLM judge, allowing
semantically equivalent predictions to be accepted without exposing additional information to the
evaluated model.
B.4
HUMAN VERIFICATION OF THE JUDGE
To further assess the reliability of our LLM-based evaluation, we conduct a human verification
study on 200 responses generated by Qwen3.8-Max, Gemma-4-31B-it, and Step3-VL-10B. We
adopt stratified sampling to ensure diverse and balanced coverage across the three evaluation di-
mensions and their corresponding subclasses, selecting 67 samples from Spatial Cognition, 67 from
Temporal Reasoning, and 66 from Multi-Step Planning. Among the 200 sampled instances, 133
contain answer hints specifying acceptable alternatives or equivalence conditions, such as alterna-
tive object names, interchangeable orderings, or equivalent action sequences, while the remaining
67 have no answer hints. All 200 responses are independently evaluated by human annotators and
DeepSeek-V4-Flash based on the same ground-truth answers and answer hints. The two evaluations
agree on all 200 samples, achieving 100% agreement, including the 133 cases involving answer
hints. These results demonstrate that our LLM-based judge can reliably account for equivalent valid
answers and provide consistent evaluation for Plan-Bench.

ADDITIONAL RESULTS
C.1
RESULTS ON ALL 16 SUBTASKS
Tables 8–10 report model accuracy on all 16 subtasks of Plan-Bench, revealing differences that are
hidden by the three dimension-level scores. In Spatial Cognition, GPT-5.6-sol achieves 82.9% on
Mirror Distinction and 87.5% on State Recognition, but only 30.0% on Target Discovery and 31.3%
on Spatial Relations. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview shows a contrasting profile, reaching 75.0% on Spa-
tial Relations but only 22.0% on Occlusion Order. Qwen3.5-27B also varies sharply across the
category, with 56.8% on Spatial Relations but 18.0% on Occlusion Order. These contrasts indicate
that success on one form of spatial evidence does not reliably extend to the other visual reason-
ing demands within the same dimension, even when the tasks draw on similar visual scenes and
viewpoints that expose different but complementary scene cues about object locations.
Temporal Reasoning likewise exhibits substantial variation between subtasks.
Qwen3.5-27B
achieves 96.7% on Progress Tracking and 80.0% on Temporal Ordering, but only 28.0% on Per-
sistent Counting. GPT-5.6-sol follows a similar pattern, scoring 93.3% on Progress Tracking and
30.0% on Persistent Counting. Even Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, which reaches 40.0% on Persistent Count-
ing, performs much better on Progress Tracking at 86.7%. These gaps suggest that inferring task
progress and event order is easier for these models than maintaining accurate counts when objects
or actions recur across visual sequences and must be distinguished from previous observations or
distractors that appear between relevant activity steps in multi-frame observations.
In Multi-Step Planning, strong results on one type of constraint do not consistently transfer to other
planning problems. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview scores 93.3% on Capacity Planning but only 10.0% on
Rearrangement Planning. Similarly, Qwen3.5-27B scores 90.0% on Capacity Planning but 44.0%
on Route Planning. Rearrangement Planning is particularly difficult for several smaller models:
InternVL3.5-8B and NEO-9B-SFT both score 0.0% despite obtaining 30.0% and 53.3% on Ca-
pacity Planning, respectively. Thus, the detailed results expose task-specific weaknesses in spatial
reasoning, temporal tracking, and multi-step planning that aggregate accuracy cannot capture.
C.2
ADDITIONAL ERROR ANALYSIS
Figure 11 presents two additional Target Discovery failures by Qwen3.8-Max. In both cases, the tar-
get appears in a mirror, so selecting the correct action requires the model to associate the reflection
with a real object and infer that object’s location. In the first example, the model notices the similar-
ity between the reflected picture and the directly visible picture, but speculates that another identical
picture exists elsewhere. It therefore selects an action that leads away from the actual target. In the
second example, the model likewise treats the reflected and visible paper towel dispensers as two
separate objects, incorrectly concluding that the target is behind the camera.
These examples show how hallucination can arise when the model fails to establish the correspon-
dence between a reflection and a physical object. Although it recognizes their visual similarity, it
hallucinates a second, identical object and reasons about its nonexistent location. This mistaken
interpretation then leads to an incorrect navigation decision, showing how object hallucination can
propagate from scene understanding to action planning in Target Discovery.
