# SecFD-FL: Frequency-Decoupled Federated Learning with Secure Aggregation for Charging Load Forecasting in Internet of Electric Vehicles 

Mamunur Rashid, Member, IEEE, Nan Chen, Member, IEEE, Syed Ali Asad Rizvi, Member, IEEE, and Amiya Nayak, Fellow, IEEE 

Abstract —Accurate charging load forecasting is essential for the reliability and efficiency of the Internet of Electric Vehicles (IoEV), yet it is challenging on two fronts: charging demand is highly stochastic, and the data collected by distributed sensors is privacy-sensitive. Although federated learning (FL) preserves data privacy, collaborative training still remains vulnerable to an honest-but-curious server that can invert shared model updates to reconstruct private data. To address both data volatility and privacy concerns, we propose SecFD-FL, a frequency-decoupled federated learning framework for IoEV comprising: 1) a dual-stream gated recurrent unit (GRU) network that separately models short-term and long-term charging load volatility, and 2) a lightweight secure aggregation layer for privacy preservation. Specifically, the secure aggregation layer employs a zero-sum additive masking protocol that perturbs each client’s upload with high-variance noise, preventing the server from reconstructing private data while preserving the exact aggregate. Extensive experiments have been conducted on both EV-level and charging station-level using real datasets. The experiment results show that SecFD-FL reduces global RMSE by up to 60% over centralized baselines, achieving Jain’s fairness up to 0.991 across clients, and reduces DLG-attack reconstruction correlation by 8 times to 15 times relative to FedAvg — all with no additional per-round communication cost beyond a one-time key exchange. 

Index Terms —Internet of electric vehicles (IoEV), federated learning, charging demand, privacy preservation, secure aggre-gation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# DRIVEN by the imperative climate change, the global transportation sector is undergoing a rapid transition toward electrification, with the worldwide stock of electric vehicles (EVs) projected to exceed 250 million units by 2030 [1]. However, range anxiety persists as a primary barrier to mass EV adoption, which necessitates strategic deployment of fast charging station (CS) networks [2]. Empowered by Internet of Things (IoT) technologies, EVs, CSs, and smart meters can interconnect to form the Internet of EVs (IoEV) to sense and collect EV data, which serves as a critical foundation for real-time operation at both edge and cloud ends [3]. EV charging demand forecasting is critical. However, high-granularity forecasting is a challenging task as the charging demand profile can be volatile and spatially heterogeneous [4], [5]. Therefore, novel and effective forecasting approaches are urgently needed [6]. In the literature, EV charging demand forecasting has evolved from statistical methods toward data-driven learning. Statistical models are inexpensive and interpretable but cannot capture the nonlinearity and stochasticity of EV loads [7]. Conventional machine learning (ML) methods, such as support vector machine and random forests (RF), improve nonlinear mapping but depend on hand-crafted features and struggle with long-range temporal dependencies in multi-scale charging behaviour. Deep learning (DL), a subclass that learns hier-archical representations directly from data, overcomes these limitations: recurrent architectures such as long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent unit (GRU) have become the dominant paradigm for extracting sequential patterns from charging demand [6]. However, deep models still rely on cen-tralized data aggregation, which poses severe privacy risks to individual users, particularly in IoEV, where data are collected by geographically distributed IoT devices and transmitted over communication networks. Federated learning (FL) emerges as a potential solution by enabling collaborative training that exchanges only local model updates rather than raw charging demand data [1]. FL has already been applied to EV-related forecasting tasks for privacy preservation, including blockchain-secured charging-load prediction [1], decentralized charging-demand forecasting [8], and clustered or transfer-learning-based station forecasting [9], [10]. However, existing works still have two critical limitations. First, feeding raw and highly volatile load signals directly into local models degrades forecasting accuracy, as these models struggle to capture rapid stochastic spikes of EV charging patterns [11]. Second, while FL avoids the direct transmission of raw data, the exchanged gradients remain vulnerable to inversion attacks, such as Deep Leakage from Gradients (DLG) [12]. Consequently, standard FL alone can-not simultaneously guarantee high forecasting accuracy and rigorous privacy protection. To address aforementioned challenges, we propose a novel frequency-decoupled federated learning scheme for charging load forecasting in the IoEV. First, to achieve high-granularity EV charging forecasting, we introduce the Complete En-semble Empirical Mode Decomposition with Adaptive Noise arXiv:2610.12909v1 [eess.SY] 9 Oct 2026 2

(CEEMDAN) method to decompose each participant’s (an EV charging station or an individual EV) charging load into a set of intrinsic mode functions (IMFs) [13]. Through IMF analysis, we observe that EV charging loads can be divided into two streams: a high-frequency residual stream representing transient charging spikes and a low-frequency trend stream capturing seasonal and behavioral regularities. Therefore, a dual-stream GRU network is designed to learn different frequency streams independently before combining them into a unified forecast. Second, to preserve client privacy during model exchange, we integrate a lightweight zero-sum additive masking protocol that delivers cryptographic-grade protection of local updates against gradient inversion attacks with no additional per-round communication overhead beyond a one-time key exchange. Early efforts in charging demand forecasting for IoEV predominantly relied on classical ML algorithms applied to handcrafted features. Rathore et al. [14] benchmarked several regression-based learners, including RF, XGBoost, linear re-gression, artificial neural networks, and deep neural networks, for EV energy consumption prediction, with RF emerging as the most accurate model. Sun et al. [15] developed a support vector regression model trained on thirteen handcrafted fea-tures drawn from charging records, weather conditions, and calendar properties. More recently, Palaniappan et al. [16] introduced a stacked meta-fusion regressor that layers RF, XGBoost, and decision tree models to enhance predictive performance. Although these ML-based methods improved upon purely statistical baselines, they remain heavily reliant on manual feature engineering and exhibit limited capacity to capture both the long-range temporal dependencies and nonlinear dynamics inherent in EV charging behavior. To overcome these limits, recent literature adopted DL archi-tectures that automatically extract spatio-temporal features. Several studies couple convolutional and recurrent components for station-level forecasting, including Conv-LSTM/BiConv-LSTM encoder–decoders [17] and a CNN optimized by the niche-immunity lion algorithm [18]. Others incorporate graph structure: an edge-based graph attention network combined with LSTM [19], and a graph-transformer that jointly models spatial and temporal patterns [20]. Further gains are reported from multi-feature fusion into LSTM inputs [21] and from a lite Transformer–GRU that fuses attention with sequential processing under dynamic conditions [22]. Despite their strong predictive performance, all of the aforementioned centralized DL frameworks require raw charging data to be aggregated at a central server. Such aggregation poses significant privacy and security risks, since EV charging records implicitly disclose users’ mobility patterns, daily routines, and home or workplace locations. To address the privacy limitations of centralized training, FL has emerged as a promising paradigm in which clients collaboratively train a shared model without exposing raw data. Danish et al. [1] proposed Block-FedL, a blockchain-augmented FL framework that employs a Bidirectional LSTM for client-side load forecasting and replaces the centralized aggregator with on-chain smart contracts, thereby ensuring tamper-resistant and verifiable model updates. Wang et al. [8] further removed the central coordinator by introducing decen-tralized synchronous and asynchronous FL variants, the latter offering improved robustness in heterogeneous, real-world set-tings. Several studies have integrated transfer learning into the FL pipeline to better cope with client heterogeneity. Wang et al. [9] proposed a domain-adaptive clustered federated transfer learning scheme that leverages maximum mean discrepancy to cluster clients with similar feature distributions and applies a multi-head attention transformer to capture salient spatio-temporal patterns. Other works have focused on architectural and security enhancements within the FL framework. Bian et al. [23] designed an attention-based convolutional model trained under FL, in which each service provider retains its time-series data locally and shares only model parameters. Wang et al. [24] proposed a lightweight authentication scheme for FL-driven energy prediction, although the contribution is primarily concerned with secure authentication rather than forecasting accuracy. Saputra et al. [25] developed an FL-based energy demand forecaster in which charging stations exchange model updates with a centralized provider server, while Perry et al. [10] introduced a clustering-driven FL approach that groups stations by behavioral similarity prior to aggregation. Although existing FL-based methods successfully decouple model training from raw data sharing, several critical limita-tions remain unaddressed. First, existing frameworks feed raw load signals directly into the predictive backbone, neglecting the multi-scale, non-stationary nature of EV charging load in which transient and stochastic spikes coexist with long-term seasonal and behavioral trends. These components are difficult to learn jointly with a homogeneous architecture. Second, 3

the shared gradients among all clients remain susceptible to gradient inversion attacks, such as DLG, which can reconstruct private samples from observed updates. However, this vulner-ability is rarely examined or mitigated in prior studies. Third, comprehensive benchmarking against state-of-the-art federated optimizers (e.g., FedProx, FedAdam, SCAFFOLD, FedDyn, Per-FedAvg) on heterogeneous, CS-level and EV-level datasets is largely absent, leaving the relative merits of competing FL designs in the EV forecasting context unclear. The proposed SecFD-FL framework directly addresses these gaps by 1) developing a dual-stream GRU to capture both short-term and long-term load trends; 2) embedding a lightweight zero-sum additive masking protocol to defend DLG attacks; 3) conduct-ing extensive multi-level benchmarking against representative federated baselines. A. System Model and Problem Formulation 

System model. We consider a federation of K clients coordinated by a single central server that collaboratively train a shared charging-load forecasting model without exchanging raw data. The framework can be generalized and evaluated at different deployment levels. At the station level , each client is an EV charging station and the server is operated by the charging-network operator; the trained model supports station-side load monitoring and operation planning. At the user level ,each client is an individual EV (or its associated user account) and the server is operated by the charging-service or fleet operator; here the model supports user- and fleet-level demand anticipation. In both settings, every client trains locally on its own data and uploads only model updates; the server’s role is restricted to aggregating these updates and broadcasting the result, and it never accesses raw charging records.

A. Datasets 

To assess SecFD-FL across both granularities at which charging forecasting is typically deployed in IoEV, we use one 

station-level dataset from City of Palo Alto and one user-level 

dataset from EV WATTS. 

1) Station-level data — City of Palo Alto: The first dataset is the publicly released EV charging records of Palo Alto [29], which logs every charging session at municipally operated stations, including session start time, charging duration, and energy delivered (kWh). The dataset comprises five munic-ipally operated charging stations, identified by their media access control (MAC) addresses; we use all five as federated clients ( K = 5 ), each containing multi-year session histories. 

2) User-level data — EV WATTS: The second dataset is the EV WATTS [30], which logs per-vehicle charging events across a heterogeneous national fleet. To ensure each client has sufficient history for sequence modelling, we retain only vehicles with at least 300 recorded sessions and a lifespan of at least 365 days, yielding 35 eligible EVs. For a fair comparison with the station-level dataset, we randomly draw 

K = 5 representative clients for the federated experiments. 

B. Data Preprocessing 

Because both datasets record discrete charging sessions rather than continuous time series, we first convert each session log into an hourly load profile. Each session’s en-ergy is distributed across the hours it spans in proportion to the minutes falling in each hour, assuming approximately constant charging power within a session [31], after which hourly energies are summed over overlapping sessions and idle hours are set to zero. This conserves total session energy and avoids the artificial spikes of start-time aggregation. Palo Alto retains each station’s full 14 ,462 -hour history, whereas EV WATTS is aligned to a common 8,760 -hour ( 365 -day) window. Each client’s hourly load is then decomposed by CEEMDAN ( I = 100 trials, Eqs. (3)–(4)) and zero-padded to a uniform M = 12 IMF channels across the federation, which has no effect on training since zero channels contribute zero gradient. Finally, supervised sequences are formed with a 24 -hour lookback window ( τ = 24 , stride one) and split chronologically into 80% training and 20% test, with the per-IMF Min–Max scaler (Eq. (7)) fitted on the training portion only to prevent temporal leakage. The resulting tensors have shape (14 ,462 , 24 , 12) for Palo Alto and (8 ,736 , 24 , 12) for EV WATTS clients. 

C. Baselines 

We benchmark SecFD-FL against two complementary fam-ilies of methods: (i) centralized deep-learning backbones trained on raw and CEEMDAN-decomposed inputs, and (ii) representative federated optimizers trained on the CEEMDAN-decomposed signal. All baselines share the same lookback window, train/test split, and optimization budget as SecFD-FL to ensure a controlled comparison. 

1) Centralized backbones: Five centralized backbones serves as baseline algorithms. LSTM stacks long short-term memory layers whose gated cell state retains long-range dependencies without the vanishing-gradient issue of vanilla recurrent neural networks [6]. GRU stacks Gated Recurrent Unit layers, replacing LSTM’s separate cell state with aleaner update/reset gate design for faster convergence [27]. CNN applies stacked 1-D convolutional layers to capture local temporal patterns through shift-invariant filters rather than recurrence [6]. Seq2Seq with Attention couples an LSTM encoder–decoder with Bahdanau-style attention to mitigate the information bottleneck of a single context vector [32]. Trans-former stacks self-attention blocks with positional encodings, replacing recurrence with parallel attention over the lookback window [6]. 

2) Federated optimizers: We select six federated algorithms as baseline algorithms. FedAvg [33] aggregates client updates via a sample-size-weighted average and serves as the founda-tional baseline, though its convergence degrades under client drift. FedProx [34] adds a proximal term penalizing deviation from the global model to bound local drift under non-IID data. FedAdam [35] treats aggregated client updates as pseudo-gradients and applies server-side Adam-style momentum for more stable convergence. Per-FedAvg [36] formulates feder-ation as a MAML-inspired meta-learning problem, producing 7

a global initialization that is rapidly personalized per client. SCAFFOLD [37] introduces client and server control variates that correct local gradient drift, accelerating convergence under heterogeneity. FedDyn [38] applies dynamic regularization via a per-client dual variable and a server-side error-feedback state to ensure convergence to the true global optimum.

V. R ESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

In this section, we present the performance results and comparison of the proposed SecFD-FL scheme with the afore-mentioned baseline algorithms in terms of (a) the effectiveness of CEEMDAN-driven frequency decoupling on forecasting accuracy; (b) the aggregation performance; (c) the defense performance against DLG; and (d) the communication and compute overhead. 

A. Effectiveness of Frequency Decoupling 

Table II compares five centralized models trained on two types of inputs: (i) the raw hourly load (raw case) and (ii) the CEEMDAN-decomposed load (C-D case). First, it is observed that CEEMDAN-decomposed inputs consistently improve the performance of recurrent models (LSTM, GRU, Seq2Seq) and CNN on both datasets. For example, on the Palo Alto data, GRU reduces RMSE from 1.44 to 0.80 kWh ( −44 .4% ) and raises R2 from 0.33 to 0.79 . On EV Watt, GRU improves RMSE from 5.69 to 3.55 kWh ( −37 .6% ) and R2 from 0.59 

to 0.79 . LSTM and Seq2Seq show comparable gains on both datasets. This pattern confirms that separating fast transient spikes from slow seasonal trends helps the model learn each component more effectively. Second, among all models, GRU with CEEMDAN input achieves the best overall results. For the Palo Alto dataset, GRU records the lowest RMSE (0.80 kWh) and highest 

R2 (0.791 ). On EV Watt, GRU again leads with RMSE of 

3.55 kWh and R2 of 0.793 . This motivates our choice of GRU as the base model in SecFD-FL. Recurrent models (LSTM, GRU, Seq2Seq) outperform CNN and Transformer because they are designed to capture sequential dependencies across time steps, which is important for EV charging patterns that depend on recent history. On the other hand, transformer performs poorly on both datasets, with negative R2 values in most cases. This is expected because attention-based models lack a built-in preference for local temporal patterns and tend to overfit on short, noisy univariate signals [39]. CNN shows moderate improvement with CEEMDAN but still lags behind recurrent models due to its limited ability to model long-range dependencies. 8

B. Aggregation Result Comparison 

Table III compares the aforementioned FL benchmarks with the proposed SecFD-FL. We highlight three observation from the findings. 

Heterogeneity-aware methods do not always help. Fed-Prox, SCAFFOLD, and FedDyn show poor performance on at least one client, producing negative R2 values. For the EV Watt dataset, SCAFFOLD records an R2 of −0.529 and RMSE of 9.82 kWh, while FedDyn gives R2 = 0 .248 and RMSE of 6.63 kWh. For the Palo Alto dataset, the same methods also lag behind FedAvg, with SCAFFOLD reaching only R2 = 0 .181 . These methods apply corrections (proximal terms, control variates, or dynamic regularizers) that were designed for non-IID classification tasks. When applied to highly stochastic charging signals, these corrections can over-constrain the local updates and hurt convergence instead of helping it. 

Frequency decoupling improves federated learning. By separating the model into residual and trend streams, SecFD-FL reaches a global RMSE of 2.28 kWh and R2 of 0.927 

on EV Watt, and 0.689 kWh RMSE with R2 of 0.847 on Palo Alto. The improvement comes from two sources: stream-specific aggregation weights match each stream’s statistical nature, and local retention in the residual branch preserves client-specific transient patterns that would otherwise be averaged out. SecFD-FL achieves a Jain’s fair-ness index of 0.871 on EV Watt and 0.991 on Palo Alto, the highest among all tested federated methods. This means that SecFD-FL maintains consistent performance across all clients, rather than favoring some at the expense of others.

D. Communication and Compute Overhead 

The SecFD-FL model contains 46,860 trainable parameters. Using 32-bit floating-point representation, each parameter re-quires 4 bytes, giving a total model size of 183 .05 KB. In each communication round, every client uploads its masked model weights ( 183 .05 KB) to the server, and the server broadcasts the updated global model ( 183 .05 KB) back to each client. With K = 5 clients, the total upload per round is 

5 × 183 .05 = 915 .25 KB, and the total download (broadcast) is also 915 .25 KB. This gives a total communication cost of 

1,830 .5 KB per round and 89 .4 MB over the full 50 rounds of training. The SecAgg masking protocol adds no additional per-round communication overhead. Each client generates its mask locally each round using a pseudorandom number generator. It is seeded with the pairwise secrets established once during initialization. Since masks are computed on the client side, they are never transmitted over the network. The only setup cost is a one-time pairwise key exchange, which requires each pair of clients to exchange a 64-byte shared secret. For K = 5 

clients, there are K(K−1) /2 = 10 pairs, giving a total key-exchange cost of 10 × 64 = 640 bytes ( 0.625 KB). This is negligible compared to the model traffic. In terms of compute, mask generation takes 10 .4 ± 0.7 ms per client per round, measured on a standard CPU. A single round of local training (15 epochs on ∼7000 samples) takes several seconds. Therefore, the mask generation adds less than 

0.1% to the total training time per round. Over 50 rounds, the total mask-generation time is approximately 0.52 seconds. 

E. Per-Station Forecast Quality 

Fig. 5 shows the actual and predicted hourly energy demand for each of the five EV clients on the EV Watt dataset. The model accurately tracks both the long periods of low or zero demand and the sudden high-energy charging events. For example, client 1 has several peaks exceeding 100 kWh, and the predicted curve closely follows these spikes in both timing and magnitude. A similar plot can be generated for the Palo Alto dataset, where comparable tracking quality is observed. Across all five clients, the global R2 is 0.9265 .Per-client R2 values range from 0.8598 (client 4) to 0.9523 

(client 5), with clients 1, 2, 3, and 5 all exceeding 0.93 . This narrow range shows that SecFD-FL performs well across all clients, not just a few. The high Jain’s fairness index ( 0.871 )reported in Sec. V-B further confirms this. SecFD-FL avoids the common problem in federated forecasting where the global model works well for some clients but poorly for others. VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper introduced SecFD-FL, a federated learning framework for charging load forecasting in IoEV that couples CEEMDAN-driven frequency decoupling with stream-specific GRU model and a zero-sum secure-aggregation layer. By decomposing each client’s raw load into high-frequency tran-sients and low-frequency trends, and processing the two bands through a dual-stream GRU with stream-specific aggregation rules, SecFD-FL attains forecasting accuracy on par with or superior to six representative FL algorithms across both user-level and station-level real-world datasets. The embed-ded zero-sum masking protocol provides cryptographic-grade resistance against gradient-inversion attacks, reducing DLG reconstruction correlation by up to 15.6 × while incurring no 10
